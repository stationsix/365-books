# Batch 1 verification notes, ranks 14 to 16 (2026-11-26)

Three separate review agents, one per book. None saw the drafting or the drafters' notes. About 175 factual claims checked. All three rated high confidence and set to verified.

This run the drafting agents could open pages, so the drafts were already sourced. The reviewers still treated every claim as unchecked.

## Fixes applied
- The Art of War (about 45 claims): the five factors reworded to Giles's own terms: Heaven as "night and day, cold and heat, times and seasons", Earth as distances, danger and security, open ground and narrow passes, and Method and discipline. Chapter II costs now follow the text. One real error was fixed: the draft said Sun Tzu advises foraging on the enemy instead of bringing supplies from home, but the text says "Bring war material with you from home, but forage on the enemy". Spies: the draft called skimping on them "foolish", but Giles calls it "the height of inhumanity". Water and the direct and indirect methods now match Giles. Yinqueshan dated to the Han tombs of 134 to 118 BC. The panel note is now quoted as recorded, "Pick one translation (Griffith or Minford)", with the reason for using Giles.
- Competitive Strategy (about 75 claims): bio matched to the HBS bio and Free Press named. Competitive moves (ch. 5) added to the structure and rewritten to what the book says: improve position without costly warfare, with commitment making threats credible. An unsupported line about strategic group maps cut. Fragmented and global industry definitions corrected to Porter's. Critiques sharpened: Hill (1988, *AMR*) argued that low cost and differentiation can be combined, Miller (1992) argued for a middle ground, D'Aveni's view follows Shay and Rothaermel (1999), and "long and dated" is labeled as our caveat. Trimmed from about 1,725 words. The reviewer added a "stuck in the middle" quote, which rested on what looked like an unauthorized full scan of the book hosted on a university site. The orchestrating session turned it into a paraphrase, because no legitimate source had it word for word. The scan is not listed as a source.
- Monetizing Innovation (about 55 claims): author titles set to those at publication, per Wiley's May 2016 release: Ramanujam a partner and board member, Tacke co-CEO. Survey given as 1,615 companies in 40 countries. Porsche timing set to "second half of the 1990s". Dodge Dart rewritten to the excerpt: launched 2012, priced after engineering, sold about a quarter of analysts' predictions. Optimizely given as pricing on monthly unique visitors in experiments, per the publisher excerpt. An Uber positioning claim with a single source cut. Pricing goals set to revenue, profit and market share. The "5 percent of business cases" figure softened. Critiques tied to Brikman's review or labeled as our caveats.

## Confirmed and worth noting
- The Art of War: all 13 Giles chapter titles. Both quotes are exact Giles wording. Traditional attribution to King Helü's court and later doubts about authorship come from Wikipedia. Britannica's early Warring States dating is given alongside the traditional one. The display year "c. 5th century BC" fits both.
- Competitive Strategy: Free Press 1980, 16 chapters in three parts plus two appendices. Five forces, entry barriers, competitor analysis components, market signals, strategic groups, the evolutionary processes and decline strategies all come from the MAAW chapter notes. Nothing from *Competitive Advantage* (1985) is presented as being in this book.
- Monetizing Innovation: the Wiley chapter 1 excerpt confirms the 72 percent figure, the Porsche Cayenne story (launched 2003, about half of Porsche's profit), the four failure types and the nine rules. Monetization models, leaders/fillers/killers and behavioral tactics each rest on two sets of notes.

## Process notes for future batches
- Public-domain translations make verification far stronger: the reviewer can check every paraphrase against the complete text. That is how it caught a reversed claim about supplies.
- Tell reviewers not to rely on full-text copies that look unauthorized, and to keep quotes to legitimate excerpts.
- Drafters still give authors their current job titles. Check titles against the publisher's release from the year the book came out.
