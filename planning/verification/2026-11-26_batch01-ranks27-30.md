## The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning (rank 30)

Separate review pass. Drafting notes not opened. About 50 factual claims checked (publication, bio, book length, GE and Ansoff history, the three fallacies and the grand fallacy, programming steps, roles of plans and planners, deliberate and emergent strategy, both quotes, every critique, list data). Confidence high; status set to verified. Reading text about 1,390 words (At a glance through Related books, prompts and headings excluded).

### Fixes applied
- Big idea: "works within existing categories and preserves them" set to the HBR article's wording (planning rearranges established categories; real strategic change requires inventing new ones).
- Concept 2: Ansoff's planning horizon was given as "the period for which a firm can forecast with some confidence" and called "misplaced faith". Corrected to Ansoff's 1965 definition as both articles give it (forecasts within about plus or minus 20 percent) and Mintzberg's own reaction ("a most extraordinary statement", paraphrased as calling the claim extraordinary).
- Concept 3: "conversations, customer contact, intuition" not in either article; replaced with the articles' gossip and hearsay.
- Concept 4: "procedures, checklists and timetables" cut to "procedures" (checklists and timetables unsourced). Systems line matched to HBR ("process more information, at least hard information... never internalize it, comprehend it, synthesize it"). "A formal system cannot learn" set to the LRP Part I point that, in a literal sense, planning never learned.
- Concept 5: "Neither is good or bad on its own. Workable strategies mix some central control with room to learn" replaced with LRP Part II's point: viable strategies usually have both qualities; purely deliberate signals unwillingness to learn, purely emergent unwillingness to think ahead.
- Concept 7: analysts were said to "challenge managers' mental models", which neither article says of analysts. Analysts now hand results over as inputs to weigh and pose the right questions (HBR "soft analysts"; LRP Part II); challenging conventional assumptions moved to catalysts (HBR). Left-handed planners' fit corrected from "looser organizations" to project-based (adhocracies) and professional organizations, right-handed to large, stable bureaucracies (LRP Part II).
- Lens: "the risk he warned about most" softened to "the risk he warned about" (no source ranks his warnings).
- Prompt 2: "including in experiments and apparent failures" unsourced; replaced with the HBR scope (own activities and competitors'). Prompt 3: "Most long-range forecasts, he argued, extend the recent past forward" not confirmed in that form; cut.
- Critiques: Ansoff 1991 described to the SMJ abstract (Mintzberg's design school critique deficient in method and fact; Mintzberg's own model valid in a limited but important context), replacing "model of emergent strategy fit only a limited context". Added Mintzberg's 1991 reply "Learning 1, Planning 0" (Puyt and Antoniou, 2026). Brews and Hunt reworded to their abstract (formal planning and incrementalism both part of good planning, especially in unstable environments) instead of "helped performance even in unstable environments". Lorenz line kept with Thinkers50's "mellowing of Mintzberg".
- Read it or skip it: added that HBR changed the title without his consent, attributed to Mintzberg (his own site, which calls the article a summary of the book).
- Sources: dropped the academia.edu copy and Tsoukas (1994) paper (not relied on); added the encyclopedia.com Mintzberg entry. Status, review date and finding set.

### Confirmed and worth noting
- Publication: Free Press, New York (Maxwell Macmillan Canada, Toronto), 1994, with the subtitle as given (WorldCat 28675146); Prentice Hall International edition, xix + 458 pages, references pp. 417 to 443, so "over 450 pages" and "more than 25 pages of references" hold (NeuAxis catalog record).
