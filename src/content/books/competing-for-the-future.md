## At a glance

**Cutting costs and fixing processes only keeps a company level with today's rivals; leadership in the future goes to companies that form their own view of where the industry is heading and build, years in advance, the competencies that will open those markets.**

Gary Hamel and C. K. Prahalad · 1994 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

When the book appeared in the autumn of 1994, C. K. Prahalad was on the faculty of the University of Michigan's business school, where he had taught since 1977, and Gary Hamel taught strategic and international management at London Business School. The book's author note says they had written seven *Harvard Business Review* articles together, and two of them, "Strategic Intent" (1989) and "The Core Competence of the Corporation" (1990), had won McKinsey Awards. The book develops those ideas into one argument.

The argument is aimed at managers busy with downsizing and reengineering. Hamel and Prahalad treat those trends as a way of catching up and ask a different question: how a company can imagine the way its industry could change, and get to that future first.

The twelve chapters move from "Getting Off the Treadmill" and "Learning to Forget" through industry foresight, strategic architecture, stretch and leverage, to core competencies as "gateways to the future." *Business Week* named it the best management book of 1994. Prahalad died in 2010.

## Key concepts

### 1. Getting off the treadmill · Transcends

The book opens by asking readers to look around their company at the issues preoccupying senior management. In the early 1990s those were restructuring and reengineering, which the authors treat as not sufficient on their own. They put the logic in ratio terms: restructuring raises measures such as return on investment by shrinking the denominator (investment, costs, headcount), while growing the numerator (revenue and profit from new business) is harder and slower. Getting off the treadmill means shifting attention from the first to the second.

*Why it transcends:* Using AI mainly to cut costs is the same move with a new tool. The question of what the savings are for still decides whether a company grows.

### 2. Competing for industry foresight · Transcends

The first contest, in the authors' telling, is intellectual: to build a better set of assumptions about how an industry will change and so become its "intellectual leader." By their estimate, senior managers spend less than 3 percent of their time building a shared view of the future; a getAbstract summary reports their advice to spend 20 to 50 percent for several months while forming one. In an HBR adaptation published ahead of the book, they cite Electronic Data Systems as a company that created its future through industry foresight.

*Why it transcends:* AI can supply trend reports by the hundred. Forming a distinct point of view from them, and spending leadership time on it, is the part the book says is missing.

### 3. Crafting strategic architecture · Evolves

Foresight has to be turned into a plan. "Not only must the future be imagined, it must be built; hence our term, 'strategic architecture.'" The architect, in the authors' metaphor, dreams of a structure that does not yet exist and also draws the blueprint for building it. Strategic architecture is that blueprint at company level: a process of discovery that maps a path into the future, largely by naming the competencies a company must acquire to get there.

*Why it evolves:* The blueprint idea holds, but the timelines shrink. When a capability can be bought or rented in months, the map needs redrawing far more often than the book's ten-year horizon suggests.

### 4. Strategy as stretch and leverage · Transcends

"It is not cash that fuels the journey to the future, but the emotional and intellectual energy of every employee." The stretch chapter asks readers to imagine choosing, years earlier, between pairs such as Volkswagen and Honda, Upjohn and Glaxo, or CBS and CNN. Stretch means setting long-term goals well beyond current resources. The chapter brings back strategic intent from the authors' 1989 article, which credited Canon's rise to resourcefulness rather than resources. Leverage, the subject of the next chapter, is the other half: doing more with the resources a company already has.

*Why it transcends:* AI changes which resources are scarce, not the case for ambition that outruns them. Small teams with large ambitions and borrowed tools are the book's argument in a new setting.

### 5. Core competencies as gateways to the future · Evolves

The 1990 article had defined core competence as the collective learning of an organization, illustrated by NEC and GTE. The book takes the idea further. The authors write that a key challenge is to build, in advance, the competencies that will act as gateways to tomorrow's opportunities, and to find new uses for the ones a company already has. Business units are geared to market share; competencies, as Stuart Crainer and Des Dearlove summarize the pair's view, are geared to "opportunity share" wherever it appears. A later chapter notes that most companies build their identity around strategic business units and argues that this needs an equally explicit core competence focus.

*Why it evolves:* Some technical skills that took years to build can now be bought from a model provider. The competencies that stay distinctive are more likely to be data, judgment, relationships and the way people and tools are combined.

### 6. The core competence agenda · Evolves

To make this practical, the book offers a two-by-two grid of existing and new competencies against existing and new markets. Writing in *Long Range Planning* in 1999, Jeffrey Shay and Frank Rothaermel describe its four cells. "Fill in the blanks" means mapping which competencies support which current product markets, to strengthen the company's position there. "Premier plus ten" means building new competencies for existing markets. "White spaces" are new markets for existing competencies, and "mega-opportunities" are new markets that need new competencies, often reached through partnerships or acquisitions.

*Why it evolves:* AI shifts weight toward the white spaces, since existing competencies combined with new tools can reach markets that were out of range.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Competing for the Future* was written against downsizing, and its warning gains force now that AI is often sold first as a way to cut costs. In the book's terms, that is denominator management. The AI reading here is ours, not the authors'.

Three concepts transcend. The treadmill warning, the call for senior time spent on foresight, and the case for ambition beyond resources all apply directly.

Three evolve. Strategic architecture, core competencies and the competence agenda remain useful frames, but the time scales move. Some competencies the book pictures as a decade's work can now be rented, which makes the question of what is hard to copy more pressing.

The risk is to mistake access to AI for a competence. If every rival can buy the same model, it is the combination of skills, data and judgment around it that the book's ideas point toward.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

In the *Journal of Marketing Management* in 2000, Daragh O'Reilly analyzed the book's rhetoric and its two main metaphors, a race and construction work. He argued that it shows naivety about how language and thought relate, and that it reads as a positioning exercise for its authors with a confused and unhelpful approach to strategy. A getAbstract review of the German edition praised its clear structure but found the methods behind its results mostly hidden from the reader. Aimed more at the concept than the book, Kevin Coyne, Stephen Hall and Patricia Gorman Clifford argued in *The McKinsey Quarterly* in 1997 that core competence had a mixed record in practice and that successful core competences are rarer than many imagine. Our caveat: like many strategy books, it draws lessons from companies that had already won, so readers cannot see how often the same approach failed.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead a company or unit and suspect that efficiency programs are crowding out thinking about where your market is going, or you want the full version of the core competence idea in one place. **Skip it** if you want a step-by-step method; the 1990 article "The Core Competence of the Corporation" and the prompts above cover the most-used ideas in far less time.
