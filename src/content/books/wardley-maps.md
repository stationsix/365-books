## At a glance

**Most companies make strategy without a map. Simon Wardley offers one: chart what your users need, chart how evolved each piece is, and you can finally see where to move.**

Simon Wardley · 2016 to 2018 (published online in chapters) · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Wardley starts with a confession. Running a software company in the mid-2000s, he realized he had no idea what strategy actually was. He had plenty of vision statements, SWOT analyses and stories, and nothing that showed where the business stood or which way the ground was moving. Generals use maps. Business leaders, he argues, mostly use the equivalent of a list of nice-sounding destinations.

His fix is the Wardley Map. Draw the chain of components needed to serve a user, with the most visible to the user at the top. Then place each component along an axis of evolution, from brand-new to commodity. Once the map exists you can talk about position and movement, spot what's about to become a commodity, and choose where to fight.

The book is unusual in form. Wardley published it free, chapter by chapter, on Medium under a Creative Commons license (CC BY-SA 4.0). It reads like what it is: a practitioner thinking out loud, irreverent and opinionated, with plenty of detours and diagrams.

## Key concepts

### 1. Without a map, you're just telling stories · Transcends

A useful map needs three things: an anchor (the user and their needs), position (where things sit relative to each other) and movement (how they change). Most strategy documents have none of these. Wardley borrows Sun Tzu's five factors (purpose, landscape, climate, doctrine and leadership) and loops them into a repeating cycle of observing, orienting, deciding and acting, a nod to John Boyd's OODA loop.

*Why it transcends:* Any team drowning in AI announcements needs exactly this: a way to see its own position instead of reacting to headlines.

### 2. Two axes: visibility and evolution · Transcends

The vertical axis is the value chain, from things the user sees and cares about at the top to hidden plumbing at the bottom. The horizontal axis is evolution. Together they turn a vague business into a picture that people can point at, argue about and improve.

*Why it transcends:* The method works for any product or service, and AI products are no exception. It's a common language for technical and business people, which most AI conversations lack.

### 3. Everything evolves · Transcends

Every component moves from left to right over time. It starts in **genesis** (new, uncertain, rapidly changing), moves to **custom built**, then **product** (stable, with several suppliers), and finally **commodity** (standardized, often sold as a utility). Each stage needs different management: experimentation on the left, efficiency on the right.

*Why it transcends:* AI is a live demonstration. Capabilities that were genesis-stage a few years ago are now sold as metered utilities. The map helps teams spot what to stop building themselves.

### 4. Climatic patterns · Transcends

Some forces are outside anyone's control, and Wardley catalogs them as climatic patterns. Examples: efficiency enables innovation, because each new commodity becomes a platform for new things built on top. Past success breeds inertia, because companies cling to what made them money. Characteristics change as components evolve.

*Why it transcends:* These patterns explain why cheap AI capability sparks a wave of new products, and why successful incumbents are often slowest to respond.

### 5. Doctrine: principles that apply everywhere · Transcends

Doctrine is the set of practices Wardley believes work in almost any context. They include focusing on user needs, using a common language, challenging assumptions, removing duplication, using the right methods for each stage of evolution and thinking small, in terms of small teams.

*Why it transcends:* None of these principles depend on the technology of the moment, and several (challenge assumptions, remove duplication) matter more as AI multiplies the output of every team.

### 6. Gameplay · Evolves

Once you can see the board, you can play. Wardley describes moves such as accelerating a component's evolution through open source, slowing a rival with fear, uncertainty and doubt, and a pattern he calls innovate, leverage, commoditize: provide a component as a utility, let others build on it, watch which new uses take off, and then commoditize those too.

*Why it evolves:* The moves still work, and AI has made them more visible. Open model releases accelerate evolution, platform providers watch their ecosystems for winners to absorb, and the pace of each round is much faster.

### 7. Pioneers, settlers and town planners · Evolves

Different stages of evolution need different kinds of people. Pioneers explore the uncharted. Settlers turn early ideas into useful products and build trust. Town planners industrialize at scale. Wardley argues for organizing around all three rather than expecting one team to do everything.

*Why it evolves:* The three roles remain, but AI shifts the balance. Much of the town planners' industrializing work is being automated, and the handoffs between roles happen faster.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

Of the three pilot books, this one feels most written for the moment, even though Wardley developed it long before the current AI boom. Its central insight, that everything evolves from novelty to commodity, is the story of AI itself. The map helps leaders ask a sharper question than "should we use AI?": which of our components are commoditizing, what new things does that make possible, and where should we stop spending effort?

Verdict: 5 of 7 key concepts transcend and 2 evolve. The book's weakness isn't that its ideas expire. It's that they take work to absorb. That makes this one of the books where a good summary is most useful.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into Claude or any AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach inside a Claude Project.

### 1. Draw your first map

Map one product or service from user need down to the plumbing.

```text
Simon Wardley's mapping method: start with a user and their need at the top. List the components that serve that need, with the most visible to the user at the top and hidden plumbing at the bottom. Then place each component on an evolution axis: genesis (new, uncertain), custom built, product (stable, several suppliers) or commodity (standardized, sold like a utility).

Ask me questions about one of my products or services until you understand its components. Then give me a map as a table with columns: component, visibility (high, medium, low), evolution stage, and a one-line reason. If you can draw diagrams, also sketch it as a simple map.
```

### 2. Run a commodity check

Find things you build yourself that you could buy as a product or utility.

```text
Using Simon Wardley's evolution stages (genesis, custom built, product, commodity), review the components below that my team builds or maintains itself.

First ask me about our constraints (cost, data, compliance, skills). Then estimate each component's stage and flag anything near commodity that we could replace with an off-the-shelf product or utility service, including AI services. Explain the risk of switching for each one.

[list components here]
```

### 3. Check your doctrine

Score your organization against Wardley's universal principles.

```text
Simon Wardley lists doctrine, principles that work in almost any context, including: focus on user needs, use a common language, challenge assumptions, remove duplication and bias, use appropriate methods for each stage of evolution, and think small (as in small teams).

Ask me five questions about how my organization works, one at a time. Then score us 1 to 5 on each principle, citing evidence from my answers, and suggest one practical fix for the weakest, especially duplicated tools or overlapping AI efforts.
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a Claude Project's instructions to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my strategy mapping partner, grounded in Simon Wardley's "Wardley Maps." Core ideas: anchor on user needs; map components by visibility and evolution (genesis, custom built, product, commodity); climatic patterns such as "everything evolves," "efficiency enables innovation" and "past success breeds inertia"; doctrine such as focusing on user needs and removing duplication; gameplay such as using open approaches to speed up evolution.

Keep a living map of my business in this project as a table (component, visibility, evolution stage). When I bring you a decision, place it on the map first, name the climatic patterns at play, and suggest gameplay options. Update the map when things move.
```

## Critiques and caveats

The book is sprawling and repetitive in places. It was written as a series of blog posts, and it shows. Placing components on the evolution axis involves judgment, so two people can map the same business differently. Wardley offers his own experience and examples rather than systematic evidence that mapping improves results. The free online format is generous but has no editor tightening it.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you work in technology strategy, product or architecture, or if you like learning by drawing. It's free, so the only cost is time. **Skip it** if you want a short, polished read; start with this summary and an online mapping tool instead.
