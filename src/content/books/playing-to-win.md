## At a glance

**Strategy is a small set of linked choices that commit an organization to winning: what winning means, where to play, how to win there, and the capabilities and systems that make it real.**

A. G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin · 2013 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Lafley ran Procter & Gamble as chief executive from 2000 to 2009. Martin, a former strategy consultant at Monitor Group and dean of the University of Toronto's Rotman School of Management from 1998 to 2013, advised P&G as a consultant during those years. The book, published by Harvard Business Review Press, is their joint account of the method they used.

Their complaint is that most organizations confuse strategy with a vision statement, a budget or a list of activities, all of which avoid the hard part: choosing. Lafley and Martin define strategy as an integrated set of choices that positions a company to win, and they mean win, not participate. A company that sets out merely to take part, they argue, never makes the tough calls.

The book builds a five-question framework, then adds a way to analyze the situation and a way to choose between options, each illustrated with P&G cases told from inside.

## Key concepts

### 1. The strategic choice cascade · Transcends

Strategy comes down to five linked questions. What is our **winning aspiration**? **Where will we play**? **How will we win** there? What **capabilities** must we have? What **management systems** do we need? The answers must reinforce each other, and the process is iterative: changing one answer forces a look at the others. The cascade is also nested. P&G as a whole answers the five questions, then each category, brand and region answers them again within the frame set above it.

*Why it transcends:* The five questions are about commitment, not information. AI can help draft options for each box, but it cannot decide which ones an organization will stake itself on.

### 2. Where to play and how to win · Transcends

These two choices are the heart of the book. Where to play picks the field: which geographies, product categories, customer segments, channels and stages of the value chain. Choosing where to play also means choosing where not to. How to win is the recipe for beating rivals on that field, and the authors follow Michael Porter in naming two basic routes: lower cost or differentiation. In their words, "Where to play selects the playing field; how to win defines the choices for winning on that field."

The Olay story shows both. Facing an aging brand, P&G weighed buying a skin care business or launching a new line, and instead chose to reinvent Olay for women aged 35 and up who were starting to notice signs of aging. Rather than move into prestige department stores, it worked with mass retailers to create a "masstige" offer, led by Olay Total Effects at a premium price, so prestige-level skin care sat on drugstore and mass-market shelves.

*Why it transcends:* AI reshapes many playing fields, but every company still has to choose which ones to enter and how it will be better there.

### 3. Capabilities as an activity system · Evolves

Capabilities are the activities an organization performs so well that they underpin its how-to-win choice. Lafley and Martin borrow Porter's activity system: a one-page map of a few core capabilities and the supporting activities that link them. The system should be feasible, distinctive and hard for rivals to copy because the pieces reinforce each other. For P&G the core list included deep consumer understanding, innovation, brand building, go-to-market skill with retailers, and global scale. That fit explains why acquiring Gillette in 2005 made sense: P&G could apply those same capabilities to Gillette's brands.

*Why it evolves:* Some capabilities that were hard to copy, such as consumer research and analysis, are now easier to buy or build with AI. The test of distinctiveness gets stricter, and the advantage moves to how the pieces combine.

### 4. Management systems · Evolves

The fifth choice is the plumbing: the structures, processes and measures that carry the strategy into daily work. They cover how strategy is reviewed, how resources are allocated and which measures show whether the choices are working. Without them, a clear strategy stays on paper.

*Why it evolves:* The need is permanent, but the tools change. Reviews, dashboards and resource planning can run faster and more continuously, which tempts organizations to measure activity instead of progress toward the winning aspiration.

### 5. The strategy logic flow · Transcends

Before making choices, the authors want a clear picture of the situation, built from four areas of analysis. **Industry**: how the market is segmented and how attractive each segment is. **Customers**: what channels and end consumers actually value, often found through close observation rather than surveys alone. **Relative position**: how the company's capabilities and costs compare with rivals'. **Competition**: how rivals are likely to react to each move. The flow keeps strategy from being made in a vacuum.

*Why it transcends:* AI makes it much cheaper to gather and summarize this analysis. The four questions remain the right ones, and answers now arrive in hours instead of weeks.

### 6. What would have to be true · Transcends

The book's most widely borrowed tool is a way to choose. A team frames a real choice between at least two distinct options, writes each as a story of how the company could win, and then asks, for each one, what would have to be true for it to be a great choice. The team then picks the conditions it is least confident about, designs tests for them, and lets the results make the decision. The question turns debate from defending favorites into examining conditions.

*Why it transcends:* This is exactly the kind of structured question an AI assistant handles well, as a partner that lists conditions and proposes tests. The judgment about which tests matter stays with the team.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Playing to Win* holds up well because it treats strategy as a decision problem, not a research problem. The cascade asks leaders to commit, and commitment is exactly what cannot be automated. AI lowers the cost of everything around the choices: analysis, drafts of possibilities, even candidate tests. That makes the book's discipline more valuable, since cheap options tempt a team to keep everything on the table.

Two areas shift. Capabilities that once separated P&G from rivals, such as consumer insight at scale, are now partly available to anyone with good data and models, so a distinctive activity system has to rest on combinations that are harder to copy. Management systems also face pressure to run faster, which risks mistaking motion for progress.

The examples come from slow-moving consumer goods. In faster markets the cascade still works, but it needs revisiting more often.

Verdict: 4 of 6 key concepts transcend and 2 evolve. None expire.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Fill in your choice cascade

Turn your current plan into the five linked choices and see where it is vague.

```text
I just read a summary of "Playing to Win" by A. G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin. They define strategy as five linked choices: a winning aspiration, where to play (geographies, segments, channels, products, value-chain stages), how to win there (lower cost or differentiation), the capabilities that must be in place, and the management systems that support them. The choices must reinforce each other, and choosing where to play also means choosing where not to.

Ask me three questions about my organization and its competitors. Then map the plan below onto the five choices, flag any box that is empty or reads as a goal rather than a choice, and name one place where two choices contradict each other.

[paste plan or strategy here]
```

### 2. Run a "what would have to be true" session

Compare two or more options by testing their conditions instead of arguing about them.

```text
Lafley and Martin ("Playing to Win") recommend choosing between options by asking, for each one, "what would have to be true" for it to be a great choice. List conditions across the industry, customers, our relative position and likely competitor reactions. Then find the conditions we are least sure of and design tests for those.

Ask me to describe the decision and at least two distinct options, one question at a time. For each option, list the conditions that would have to be true, mark the three we are least confident about, and propose a cheap, fast test for each. Do not tell me which option you prefer.
```

### 3. Check your capabilities map

Test whether your capabilities really support how you plan to win.

```text
"Playing to Win" says capabilities should form an activity system: a few core capabilities and supporting activities that reinforce each other, so the whole is feasible, distinctive and hard to copy. The capabilities must match the how-to-win choice.

Ask me what we believe our how-to-win choice is and what we do especially well. Then draw the activity system as a list of core capabilities and links, mark which capabilities a competitor could now copy or buy cheaply with AI tools, and suggest where our real distinctiveness lies.

[paste a description of your key activities here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

The evidence is one company's experience, told by its former chief executive and his adviser, so the cases lean toward success stories and the authors' own judgment of why they worked. Consumer goods is a stable, well-understood industry, and the framework says less about markets where the playing field itself is changing fast. Lafley returned as P&G's chief executive in 2013, the year the book came out, which is a reminder that winning choices need renewing.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead a business unit, run strategy reviews, or need a shared vocabulary for strategic choices across a team. **Skip it** if you already use the cascade and want sharper diagnosis of a specific challenge; *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* covers that more directly.
