## At a glance

**People don't buy products; they hire them to make progress in a particular circumstance, and companies that understand that job, rather than the customer's profile, can innovate on purpose instead of by luck.**

Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, David S. Duncan · 2016 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Clayton Christensen, the Harvard Business School professor behind the theory of disruptive innovation, wrote this book with three collaborators: Taddy Hall of the Cambridge Group, former *Harvard Business Review* editor Karen Dillon, and David S. Duncan of the consultancy Innosight. Disruption theory is about how companies respond to new competitors. This book takes on a different question: what makes a customer choose a product at all?

Companies know more about their customers than ever, yet most new products still miss. Their diagnosis is that firms collect correlations, such as which kinds of customers buy what, but not causes. The cause, they argue, is the **job** a person is trying to get done. Understand the job and innovation becomes far less of a gamble.

The book builds the case through stories from companies such as Intuit, Airbnb, American Girl and Southern New Hampshire University, then turns to how an organization can find jobs, design around them and stay focused on them as it grows.

## Key concepts

### 1. Jobs to be done · Transcends

A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. The authors chose the word "progress" on purpose, because it signals movement toward a goal rather than a static need. When people buy something, they **hire** it to do that job. If it does the job well, they hire it again next time; if not, they **fire** it and find something else. It echoes a line Theodore Levitt was fond of telling his students: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole.

*Why it transcends:* The question "what is this hired to do?" doesn't depend on the technology doing the work. It applies as well to an AI agent as to a power drill.

### 2. The circumstance, not the customer · Evolves

The book's best-known example is the milkshake. In the version reported by Harvard Business School Working Knowledge in 2011, a fast-food chain had asked customers who fit its milkshake-buyer profile how to improve the product, made the changes, and saw no lift in sales. A researcher working with Christensen then spent a day in one restaurant and found that about 40 percent of milkshakes were bought early in the morning by commuters taking them to go. They faced a long, dull drive with one free hand, and they knew they'd be hungry by mid-morning. A thick shake through a thin straw beat a bagel or a doughnut. Parents buying a treat for their children were hiring the same product for a different job. The lesson: customer data is built to show correlation, and the 2016 HBR article that previewed the book warns that this makes firms "masters of description but failures at prediction."

*Why it evolves:* AI makes description nearly free, from dashboards to synthetic customer personas. That raises the value of the thing the milkshake researcher did: watching real people in a real circumstance.

### 3. Functional, social and emotional dimensions · Transcends

Jobs are not only practical. The HBR article puts it plainly: jobs are "never simply about function." Each has social and emotional sides, which often matter more than the functional one. Parents choosing childcare, the book's excerpt notes, weigh trust and how others will see their home, not just hours and price. Southern New Hampshire University's ads for its online programs, the HBR article reports, spoke to pride and family as much as career advancement.

*Why it transcends:* People adopting AI tools at work weigh how they will look to colleagues and whether they still feel competent. Those social and emotional jobs often decide adoption more than features do.

### 4. Where to look for jobs · Evolves

The authors give practical places to hunt. Look for **nonconsumption**, people who buy nothing because nothing fits: Wharton's review of the book notes Airbnb found that 40 percent of guests would not have traveled at all without it. Look for **work-arounds**, as when Intuit saw small businesses running their books on Quicken, a product built for individuals. Look for **negative jobs** people want to avoid, and for **unusual uses**, as when Vicks noticed people taking NyQuil just to sleep and created ZzzQuil.

*Why it evolves:* The hunting grounds are unchanged, but the evidence is easier to see: support logs, search queries and AI prompts record work-arounds and unusual uses.

### 5. Design the whole experience and organize around it · Transcends

Knowing the job is not enough. A company has to turn it into a **job spec** and then line up its processes to deliver it every time. Southern New Hampshire University rebuilt its online program around working adults: fast replies, personal advisers and quick financial-aid decisions. The HBR article reports a 34 percent compounded annual growth rate over six years and revenue near $535 million by fiscal 2016. American Girl, the article notes, sells stories, stores and memories, not just dolls, and rivals have failed to dent it. Experiences are harder to copy than products.

*Why it transcends:* AI makes features easier to copy than ever. A company that has organized its whole experience around a job has the kind of advantage that copying a feature can't match.

### 6. Measure the job, not the product · Evolves

The authors warn that measures of efficiency are easier to track than measures of whether the job got done. Insight about jobs is fragile, Wharton's review reports, because it comes as stories rather than numbers. The fix is to measure the customer's progress. Amazon, for example, tracks when an order reaches the customer, not when it ships. In a Quartz interview about the book, Christensen argued that customers are unpredictable but the jobs they need done stay stable, which makes innovation more predictable.

*Why it evolves:* AI products can track task completion, not just usage, so job-level metrics are newly practical. The risk is the same one the book names: optimizing what is easy to count.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

This reading is ours; the book predates today's AI tools and Christensen died in 2020. Jobs theory may be the best single question to ask about an AI product: what job, in what circumstance, is someone hiring it to do?

What holds is the core. Progress, circumstance and the social and emotional sides of a job explain why some AI tools spread inside companies and others sit unused. An open-ended chat box that can do almost anything is hired for almost nothing until people find a specific job for it.

What changes is the evidence. The book's warning about correlation is sharper now, because AI can produce endless confident descriptions of customers, including simulated interviews. At the same time, real behavior is easier to capture, and AI can help sort hundreds of genuine customer interviews. The discipline is to use those tools on real circumstances, not invented ones.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. Read alongside *The Innovator's Dilemma*, it covers the question that book leaves open: not who an AI shift will hurt, but what people will actually hire it for.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Name the job

Find out what customers are really hiring your product or service to do.

```text
I just read a summary of "Competing Against Luck" by Clayton Christensen and coauthors. Its core idea: customers don't buy products, they hire them to make progress in a particular circumstance. A job has functional, social and emotional sides, and the circumstance (where they are, what pressures they face, what else they could use) matters more than who the customer is.

Ask me four questions about my product, who buys it, and the moment they decide to buy. Then write two or three candidate job statements using verbs and nouns, not adjectives, and for each one list what else customers might hire for the same job, including doing nothing. Tell me which candidate my answers support best and what I should observe to check it.

[paste a product description or a few customer comments here]
```

### 2. Plan a switch interview

Prepare to interview recent buyers about the moment they hired or fired a product.

```text
"Competing Against Luck" argues that customer data shows correlation but not cause, and that the cause of a purchase is the job the customer was trying to get done. The best evidence comes from real circumstances: what was happening when someone started looking, what they tried, and what they fired to hire something new.

Ask me who my recent new customers and recent lost customers are and how I can reach them. Then draft a 30-minute interview guide that reconstructs the story of one purchase or cancellation, step by step, covering the functional, social and emotional sides. Avoid questions that ask people what features they want.
```

### 3. Hunt for unmet jobs

Look for opportunities in nonconsumption, work-arounds and odd uses.

```text
"Competing Against Luck" names places to look for jobs: nonconsumption (people who buy nothing because nothing fits), work-arounds people invent, negative jobs people want to avoid, and surprising uses of existing products.

Ask me three questions about my market and the evidence I have, such as support tickets, reviews or usage data. Then go through each of the four places, list the signals in my material that point to an unmet job, and rank the three most promising opportunities by how often the struggle appears and how poorly current options handle it.

[paste support tickets, reviews or usage notes here]
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my jobs-to-be-done coach, grounded in "Competing Against Luck" by Clayton Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David S. Duncan. Core ideas: customers hire products to make progress in a particular circumstance and fire them when they fall short; the circumstance matters more than customer demographics or product features; jobs have functional, social and emotional sides; look for jobs in nonconsumption, work-arounds, negative jobs and unusual uses; turn the job into a spec and organize the whole experience around it; and measure the customer's progress, not just product activity.

Whenever I bring you a product idea, feature, metric or customer finding: ask what job it serves and in what circumstance, what the customer would fire to hire it, and what real evidence supports it. Push back when I describe customers instead of their circumstances. Keep a running note in this project of the jobs we've identified, the evidence for each, and what we've changed because of them.
```

## Critiques and caveats

Jobs to be done has rival versions, and their founders disagree. Tony Ulwick of Strategyn, whose Outcome-Driven Innovation defines a job as the task or goal a customer is trying to accomplish, argued in 2013 that the milkshake story gets the job wrong: a boring commute is a context, and the job is getting breakfast on the go. Product consultant Cyril Le Roux has written that the two definitions leave many product managers and marketers confused, and that the progress version demands comfort with abstraction; he treats the two as tools for different jobs, not rivals. Theodore Kinni, reviewing the book for strategy+business, found that it feels intuitively right but that putting it to work will take real effort. Our own caveat: the evidence is mostly success stories, which show the idea working but not how often it fails.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you build products or services, decide what to fund, or keep losing arguments to customer data that explains nothing. **Skip it** if you want a step-by-step research method; the HBR article "Know Your Customers' 'Jobs to Be Done'" covers the core in a fraction of the time, and Ulwick's *Jobs to Be Done* offers a more process-driven version.
