## At a glance

**Most new products fail because price is decided last; companies that find out early what customers value and will pay, then design the product around that price, turn innovation into money far more reliably.**

Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke · 2016 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Both authors come from Simon-Kucher & Partners, a consulting firm that specializes in pricing and monetization. When the book came out, Madhavan Ramanujam was a partner and board member in its San Francisco and Silicon Valley office who had led more than 125 monetization projects for internet, software and technology clients, and Georg Tacke was the firm's co-CEO. The book draws on the firm's 30 years and more than 10,000 projects.

The problem is stark: a 2014 Simon-Kucher survey of executives at 1,615 companies in 40 countries found that 72 percent of new products launched in the previous five years missed their revenue and profit goals or failed outright. The authors trace the root cause to one habit: companies design, then build, then market, and only then price. The authors reverse that order, so what customers will pay shapes what gets built.

It opens with a tale of two cars, then runs in three parts: the problem, nine rules for getting monetization right, and success stories plus an implementation process.

## Key concepts

### 1. Design the product around the price · Transcends

In the second half of the 1990s Porsche, a sports car maker, began planning an SUV. It asked potential customers what they would pay, learned they would pay more for a Porsche SUV than for rivals, and was ready to walk away if the price would not support success. Customer input dropped the famous manual six-speed transmission and added large cup holders. The Cayenne, launched in 2003, came to account for about half of Porsche's profit. Fiat Chrysler, by contrast, engineered its Dodge Dart first and put a price on it afterward; in 2012, its launch year, it sold about a quarter of what market analysts had predicted. As the authors put it, "Porsche succeeded by designing the product around the price."

*Why it transcends:* Building is getting cheaper and faster, which makes building the wrong thing easier too. Asking what customers will pay before committing is still the cheapest test there is.

### 2. Four flavors of failure · Transcends

The authors name four ways an innovation fails to make money. **Feature shock** crams too many features into one product, leaving it often overpriced and out of step with what customers want. A **minivation** suits the right market but is priced too low to reach its potential. A **hidden gem** is a potential blockbuster that never gets properly launched, usually because it sits outside the core business. The **undead** is a product customers do not want that gets launched anyway, answering a question nobody asked or the right question in the wrong way.

*Why it transcends:* The labels describe how organizations misjudge value, not a technology, so they fit any launch.

### 3. Have the willingness-to-pay talk early · Evolves

The first rule is to discuss price with customers during development, not just before launch. Without it, a team cannot prioritize features and will not know whether customers will pay until the product is on the market. The method runs in stages: explore the customer's problem, show the solution, gauge its perceived value, and only then talk price. Techniques range from direct price questions to having customers pick the most and least valued features from rotating lists.

*Why it evolves:* AI can draft interview guides and sort answers by segment. It cannot stand in for real buyers saying what they would pay.

### 4. Segment by needs and willingness to pay · Evolves

The authors warn against one-size-fits-all offers. Averages hide real differences: an average of $60 may conceal one group at $20 and another at $100. Segments should follow needs and willingness to pay rather than demographics, and packaging follows from the data. Features sort into **leaders**, the must-haves that drive purchase; **fillers**, nice-to-haves that do not sell a product on their own; and **killers**, features most customers do not want and that can sink a deal.

*Why it evolves:* Usage data and AI analysis make finding segments far easier. The judgment about which segments are worth serving remains a choice.

### 5. Go beyond the price point · Evolves

The authors separate the price level, the number on the tag, from the monetization model, how a company charges, and argue the model often matters more. The book describes five models: subscription, dynamic pricing, market-based pricing set by bidding, pay as you go, and freemium. Optimizely, an A/B testing company, chose a usage-based model that charged by the monthly unique visitors included in experiments, and Uber's surge pricing is the book's example of dynamic pricing.

*Why it evolves:* AI products pay real compute costs for every use, which pushes pricing toward usage and makes generous free tiers costly. The menu still applies; the economics have shifted.

### 6. Pick a strategy and build an outside-in business case · Transcends

Teams must choose a pricing goal, since goals such as revenue, profit and market share can conflict. The strategy then takes one of three forms: **maximization** (the highest price that meets the goal), **penetration** (price low to win share fast, raise later) or **skimming** (start high for early adopters, then lower). The business case should be built outside in, from customer willingness to pay and price elasticity, rather than inside out from costs and hopes. In the authors' experience, most business cases are built inside out, so they tell teams what they want to hear.

*Why it transcends:* AI speeds up the spreadsheet, but a business case built on wishful inputs is still wishful.

### 7. Behavioral tactics and price integrity · Evolves

The last rules cover selling. The innovation will not speak for itself, so value must be communicated segment by segment. Customers are not fully rational, and the book lists tactics that use this: the compromise effect, where buyers drift to the middle option; anchoring with a high reference price; pricing to signal quality; razor and blade pricing; pennies-a-day framing; and price thresholds. Teams should also protect price integrity: when a launch is slow, try non-price fixes before discounting.

*Why it evolves:* AI shopping assistants that compare offers could blunt some framing tricks. The advice to resist knee-jerk price cuts holds either way.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Monetizing Innovation* is about a decision AI does not make for you: what a product is worth to a particular customer. Designing around the price, the four failure types and the outside-in business case all transcend.

Four concepts evolve. The willingness-to-pay talk and segmentation get cheaper to run with AI tools, but they depend on real customers, and simulated answers are a new temptation. Monetization models face the biggest shift: when each use carries a compute cost, flat subscriptions and free tiers need rethinking.

For teams building AI products, feature shock is the warning to heed: it is easy to add what the model can do, and harder to learn what customers will pay for.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve. None expire.

### 1. Diagnose a launch

Check a product or plan against the book's four failure types.

```text
In "Monetizing Innovation," Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke name four ways innovations fail to make money: feature shock (too many features, overpriced, hard to sell), minivation (right market, priced too low), hidden gem (a potential hit never properly launched, often because it sits outside the core business) and undead (a product customers do not want, launched anyway).

Ask me three questions about the product, its customers and how its price was set. Then say which failure type it is most at risk of, what evidence points that way, and the one step I should take this month to reduce the risk.

[paste a description of your product and its pricing here]
```

### 2. Plan a willingness-to-pay conversation

Prepare customer interviews that bring price in early.

```text
"Monetizing Innovation" says to discuss price with customers during development, not after. The conversation runs in stages: explore the customer's problem, present the solution, gauge perceived value, then discuss price. Feature questions sort features into leaders (must-haves that drive purchase), fillers (nice to have) and killers (features most customers do not want).

Ask me two questions about who I am interviewing and what stage the product is at. Then write a 30-minute interview guide that follows those stages, includes a most-and-least-valued feature exercise, and lists what answers would tell me to change the price or cut a feature.

[paste your product concept and feature list here]
```

### 3. Choose a monetization model

Compare ways of charging before settling on a price level.

```text
"Monetizing Innovation" separates the price level (how much) from the monetization model (how you charge) and argues the model often matters more. It describes five models: subscription, dynamic pricing, market-based pricing, pay as you go and freemium, and three strategies: maximization, penetration and skimming.

Ask me three questions about my customers, my costs per customer and my goal (revenue, profit or market share). Then compare the models that could fit, show how each would affect revenue if my cost to serve rises with usage, and recommend one model and one strategy with the main risk of each.

[paste a description of your product, customers and costs here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing the book on his blog, Yevgeniy Brikman warned that asking people to predict their own future buying can mislead, and pointed readers to *The Mom Test* and *Continuous Discovery Habits* for interview methods that focus on past behavior. Our own caveats: the cases come largely from Simon-Kucher's client work, so the evidence is selected by the people selling the method, and the book tells success stories rather than comparing them with failures that followed the same process. Some behavioral tactics can also feel manipulative if pushed too far.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you manage products, run a startup or own pricing for a new offer and want a step-by-step process. **Skip it** if you only need the main idea; the four failure types and the leaders, fillers and killers sort above carry much of the value, and *7 Powers* will help more with defending profits once you have them.
