## At a glance

**In Perez's account, each technological revolution since 1771 has followed the same arc: a speculative frenzy pays to install the new technology, a crash forces new rules, and only then does a broad golden age of deployment arrive.**

Carlota Perez · 2002 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Carlota Perez, born in Caracas in 1939, is British-Venezuelan. In Venezuela she was the founding director of technology at the Ministry of Industry from 1980 to 1983, and founded FINTEC, a venture capital agency. She has held posts at Sussex, Cambridge, the London School of Economics, Tallinn and University College London.

She wrote the book as the Internet bubble burst, shaking belief in a "new economy." Perez argues the episode was not new. Technological revolutions arrive about every half century, and two or three decades of turbulent adaptation pass before their full benefits spread. Those decades bring the greatest excitement in financial markets and end in crashes. Schumpeterian economists, she says, neglect finance, and students of financial crises neglect technology; the book joins them.

It runs 198 pages, with a preface by the innovation economist Chris Freeman. Part I describes the great surges, Part II follows financial capital through each phase, Part III asks what drives the sequence, and an epilogue dated June 2002 assesses the moment.

## Key concepts

### 1. Five great surges of development · Transcends

Perez counts five revolutions, each dated from a visible "big-bang." Arkwright's cotton mill at Cromford in 1771 opened the Industrial Revolution in Britain. The 1829 trial of the Rocket locomotive for the Liverpool to Manchester railway opened the age of steam and railways. Carnegie's Bessemer steel plant in Pittsburgh in 1875 opened the age of steel, electricity and heavy engineering. Ford's first Model T in 1908 opened the age of oil, the automobile and mass production. Intel's microprocessor, announced in 1971 in Santa Clara, opened the age of information and telecommunications. She calls them "great surges of development" rather than long waves, since each reshapes society, not just output.

*Why it transcends:* The list still frames the biggest question about AI: whether it opens a sixth surge or belongs to the fifth.

### 2. The techno-economic paradigm · Transcends

A technological revolution is a cluster of interrelated new technologies, products and industries, not one invention. It usually rests on a cheap, all-pervasive input, such as cheap chips in the fifth surge. Around it forms a techno-economic paradigm: a model of best practice for using the new technologies that becomes the new common sense guiding their spread. A mature paradigm filters out innovations that do not fit it, so incumbents become both agents and victims of its closing, and outsiders funded by idle capital carry the next revolution.

*Why it transcends:* Asking what new common sense a technology creates, rather than what it does, is still the most useful question about AI in a company.

### 3. Installation and deployment · Transcends

Each surge has two halves. In installation, new industries and infrastructure are built, old ones decline, and finance leads. Income tends to concentrate and growth diverges between sectors, regions and countries. In deployment, the paradigm spreads through the whole economy under a new socio-institutional framework, and production leads. The deployment halves are the golden ages: the Victorian boom, the belle époque and the mass-production era. Between them lies the mismatch between a fast-moving economy and slow-moving rules, education and habits built for the last revolution.

*Why it transcends:* The split between building AI infrastructure and putting it to broad use is the clearest way to read today's AI spending and disappointments.

### 4. Four phases and the turning point · Evolves

The book's core chapters run through four phases. In irruption, financial capital starts a love affair with the revolution and funds a burst of new firms. In frenzy, finance decouples from production and a bubble grows, along with what the book calls "the vast disproportion between paper wealth and real wealth." Then comes the turning point, usually a crash and recession that force regulation and changeover. In synergy, finance and production recouple and growth spreads. In maturity, markets saturate and idle money looks for the next revolution. Frenzies center on the new infrastructure, from canal mania to the Internet bubble.

*Why it evolves:* The sequence held up, but Perez herself warns that the dating is loose and that crises help define the phases, so the model shows direction better than timing.

### 5. Financial capital and production capital · Evolves

The book defines both by behavior, not by assets. Financial capital is the agents who hold wealth as money or paper assets and act to increase it. Production capital is the agents who create new wealth by making goods and services. Financial capital "is mobile by nature while production capital is basically tied to concrete products" through equipment and networks. Finance can invest without knowing much about what it funds, which lets it back newcomers and drift into speculation. Production capital is rooted in an area of competence.

*Why it evolves:* Cash-rich technology giants now act as financier and producer at once, so the line Perez drew runs inside firms as much as between them.

### 6. Society shapes the technology · Transcends

Chapter 3 rejects the view of technology as an outside force. A revolution shakes society, and society steers it back through conflict and compromise. Financial collapse, a couple of decades after the big-bang, is the strongest pressure for institutional reform and state action. Chapter 15 draws the policy lesson: institutions change more slowly than technology, so reforms should be designed before the crisis that makes them possible, and the near future is often not an extrapolation of the near past.

*Why it transcends:* AI rules, skills and labor markets lag the technology now, and her advice to draft the new rules before the crash still applies.

### 7. The 2002 call: a turning point at hand · Expires

The epilogue dates the end of the fifth surge's installation to March 2000, when the NASDAQ bubble deflated. It sees a turning point that could lead to depression, a gilded age or a golden age, and calls for institutional imagination: regulating the casino economy, letting production capital lead and drawing more countries into the global economy.

*Why it expires:* In her January 2010 epilogue to the Russian edition, Perez wrote that she had still thought core countries could regulate finance; instead easy money fed a second, financial bubble that burst in 2008.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book predates today's AI, but Perez has since written about it; her later views are credited below, and the rest is our reading. In "What Is AI's Place in History?" (Project Syndicate, March 11, 2024) she argues that AI is a revolutionary technology but not a new technological revolution: it belongs to the information revolution that began with the microprocessor in the 1970s, possibly as a third leap after computers and the internet, and reaching a golden age depends on politics more than technology. In a Financial Times column by John Thornhill, republished in *The Irish Times* on August 23, 2025, she is reported saying that she had not seen a golden age without a crash.

Our reading: the framework fits the present AI boom unusually well. Data centers, chips and power look like installation infrastructure financed on expectations, as canals and railways were. Her test of paper wealth against real wealth is the right one for AI valuations. What changes is who holds the money: some of the biggest AI builders pay for much of it from profits of earlier businesses, which blurs her line between the two capitals.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend, 2 evolve and 1 expires.

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing for EH.Net in 2003, Bertrand Roehner of the University of Paris 7 praised the cross-era comparison but noted that five cases are a small sample, most tables are qualitative, and the narrative needs real data. Perez concedes much of this herself. She calls the book a stylized narrative and a think-piece, admits that using crises to date the phases is partly circular, and warns that applying the model rigidly would defeat its purpose. The biggest test came after publication: the turning point she saw in 2002 did not resolve as she expected, and by 2010 she was describing a double bubble.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you invest in, fund or plan around new technology and want the deepest version of the bubble-then-golden-age argument that AI debates keep citing. **Skip it** if you want practical tactics: it is a compact academic history, and the introduction and several chapters she posts free on her website, carlotaperez.org, give the core model.
