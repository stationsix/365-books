## At a glance

**Established companies can beat the innovator's dilemma, but only if their leaders run the mature business for efficiency and build new ones for speed at the same time, under one senior team and one shared identity.**

Charles A. O'Reilly III and Michael L. Tushman · 2016 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Charles O'Reilly is the Frank E. Buck Professor of Management at Stanford Graduate School of Business. Michael Tushman, who took his PhD at MIT, is an emeritus professor at Harvard Business School. Each has won a Distinguished Scholar Award from the Academy of Management, and they dedicate the book to their colleagues at the consulting firm Change Logic.

The book takes up the puzzle Clayton Christensen made famous in *The Innovator's Dilemma*: why do successful firms find it so hard to adapt? Challenging his pessimism about incumbents, the authors argue that the problem lies mainly in leadership, not strategy, technology or luck, and that the cure is ambidexterity: exploiting today's business while exploring tomorrow's.

This summary follows the fully revised second edition (Stanford Business Books, 2021), which updates every chapter, adds chapters on culture and on three innovation disciplines, and carries a foreword by Steve Blank. Its ten chapters run in three parts: frameworks, cases and leadership.

## Key concepts

### 1. The success syndrome · Transcends

Winning companies align people, structure, metrics, incentives and culture with the winning strategy. That alignment makes them excellent at what they already do, and it also creates inertia, because a firm tuned for cost, control and efficiency rejects work that needs flexibility and experiments. The authors call this the success syndrome. Cautionary cases include Sears and Kodak. Smith Corona, O'Reilly told Stanford GSB in 2016, had one of the first word processors, but its leaders chose not to fund it. "It is a leadership issue, not a technology issue," he said.

*Why it transcends:* A firm tuned to its current product will find reasons not to fund an AI venture that threatens it.

### 2. Explore and exploit · Transcends

The book builds on James March's distinction between exploiting known resources and exploring new ones. Both are essential, and they compete for the same money and attention. Exploitation needs efficiency, discipline and incremental improvement; exploration needs search, risk and tolerance for failure. The first edition's description puts the task plainly: "Firms must remain competitive in their core markets, while also winning in new domains." An ambidextrous firm does both at once. Large firms, O'Reilly has argued, are often better placed than small ones, because one bad bet will not sink them.

*Why it transcends:* The tension between running the core and trying the new is not about any technology, and AI makes both halves louder at once.

### 3. Innovation streams and existing assets · Evolves

Long-lived firms adapt less by leaping into the unknown than by using assets they already own in new markets, with new capabilities, or both; the book maps the options on two axes, capabilities and market scope. Ball Corporation began with mason jars and became an aerospace leader; Tushman credits the power of its overarching identity. O'Reilly has described how Fujifilm, as film sales peaked in 2000, redirected its capabilities into regenerative medicine, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals and LCD films, while Kodak went bankrupt in 2012.

*Why it evolves:* Proprietary data, customer relationships and workflows are now the assets an AI venture can draw on, which makes the inventory question more urgent but no less concrete.

### 4. Separate, then integrate · Evolves

Ambidexterity's structural core is to give exploratory units their own processes, structure and culture while tying them to the existing business through the senior team. In their 2004 *Harvard Business Review* article, the authors compared four ways of organizing breakthrough projects; by one summary of that article, the ambidextrous design reached about 90 percent of its innovation goals. Ciba Vision, a case in the book, is the classic example: in 1991 it set up autonomous units for six new lens projects, tied managers' pay to company-wide results, and grew from about $300 million to more than $1 billion in revenue within ten years.

*Why it evolves:* AI can let a small exploratory team draw on the core's data and systems without a large new unit, which changes how much separation is needed, not whether the integration has to happen.

### 5. Getting it right versus almost right · Evolves

The book's central case is IBM. In September 1999 Lou Gerstner was angry that a unit under financial pressure had dropped a promising initiative, and a study found that IBM had failed to capture value from 29 technologies and businesses. In 2000 IBM launched its Emerging Business Opportunities process. It learned to put experienced, well-connected managers in charge, after younger leaders had often failed. In the authors' 2009 study with J. Bruce Harreld, EBOs added $15.2 billion in revenue from 2000 to 2005. The book pairs IBM with Cisco, another deliberate, repeated attempt at ambidexterity.

*Why it evolves:* The design still applies, but AI ventures can reach a market in months, so review cycles built for multiyear bets need a faster rhythm.

### 6. Ideation, incubation and scaling · Evolves

New in the second edition, this chapter argues that innovation needs three disciplines: generating ideas, testing them through disciplined experiments, and scaling the survivors into real businesses. Steve Blank's foreword observes that many firms manage ideation, fewer master incubation, and relatively few scale new ventures. He calls scaling the crux of ambidexterity and warns that lean methods inside large firms often devolve into innovation theater.

*Why it evolves:* AI makes ideation and early prototypes cheap, which moves the bottleneck to scaling.

### 7. Leaders, teams and culture as linchpins · Transcends

The last part makes leaders the decisive variable. In the first edition's account, success rests on a clear strategic reason for exploring, leaders' commitment to fund and shield new ventures, separation so they can build their own alignment, clear criteria for folding them back in, and a common identity across old and new units. The second edition adds a chapter on culture as advantage or drag, with newer examples such as Microsoft and General Motors. The amount of exploration, O'Reilly said in 2016, depends on how near disruption is.

*Why it transcends:* No tool decides which ventures get money, talent and protection from the core; that stays a leadership choice.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

Nothing we read from O'Reilly or Tushman addresses today's AI tools, so what follows is our reading of their framework, not their view.

Most established firms now face exactly the book's problem: a core business that pays the bills and a new technology that could reshape it. The success syndrome explains why AI pilots stall inside well-run companies, and the separate-then-integrate design suggests why spinning them off entirely can fail too, since the new venture loses the data, customers and channels that are the incumbent's real advantage.

What changes is the cost of exploration. AI makes ideation and early testing cheap, so the scarce discipline is scaling, the one the second edition stresses. It also blurs the line between exploring and exploiting: the same tool can speed up today's operations and build tomorrow's product.

Read it as the hopeful answer to *The Innovator's Dilemma*: Christensen explains why leaders miss shifts, and this book explains how some have not.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve.

## Critiques and caveats

Dimária Silva e Meirelles, reviewing the first edition in the Brazilian journal *RAE* (2017), praised its simple arguments and varied cases but found it thin on theory, light on dynamic capabilities and business models. The evidence is largely case studies chosen by the authors, and the 90 percent figure comes from their own earlier study. Julian Birkinshaw and Cristina Gibson argued in *MIT Sloan Management Review* (2004) that separate units can become isolated from the core, and proposed contextual ambidexterity, where individuals choose between both kinds of work, as a complement. The flagship case aged too: O'Reilly himself said in 2016 that IBM was less ambidextrous than under Gerstner and Sam Palmisano.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead or fund new ventures inside an established company and want a researched design for running them alongside the core. **Skip it** if you work at a startup or want a step-by-step field guide: the authors' *Corporate Explorer*, written with Andrew Binns, is the more practical companion.
