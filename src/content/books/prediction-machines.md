## At a glance

**AI is best understood as a sharp drop in the cost of prediction, and when prediction gets cheap, people use far more of it and the things that go with it, above all human judgment, become more valuable.**

Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb · 2018 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

The three authors are economists at the University of Toronto's Rotman School of Management who studied the internet's economics before turning to AI. Agrawal is a founder of the Creative Destruction Lab, a program for science-based startups, where Goldfarb is chief data scientist.

Their starting point is a question economists ask of any new technology: what does it make cheaper? Computers made arithmetic cheap. AI, they argue, does the same for prediction, which they define in a 2017 *MIT Sloan Management Review* article as using information you have to generate information you did not have. An HBR article they wrote in 2016 put it plainly: "Machine intelligence is, in its essence, a prediction technology."

The book sets aside hype about general intelligence and works through cheap prediction in five parts: Prediction, Decision Making, Tools, Strategy and Society. A 2022 updated edition added material on how prediction fits into decision-making; the authors' system-level sequel, *Power and Prediction*, came out the same year.

## Key concepts

### 1. Cheap changes everything · Transcends

When the price of something falls, people use more of it, including in places it never appeared before. Cheap arithmetic first served users such as the US Census Bureau and the Defense Department, then turned photography from a chemistry problem into an arithmetic one; in their 2016 HBR article, digital camera parts gained value while film chemicals lost it. Cheap prediction, the authors argue, follows the same path. Problems never framed as prediction get reframed: self-driving cars moved past hand-coded rules once engineers asked one prediction question, what would a human do?

*Why it transcends:* Today's language models generate text by predicting what comes next, so the 2018 thesis describes their mechanics well, and the "what got cheaper?" question still cuts through hype.

### 2. Prediction, and little else · Expires

The book is careful about what AI is not. Chapter 13 sets today's tools apart from the human-like general intelligence of science fiction: "The current generation of AI provides tools for prediction and little else." Its examples are narrow tools, each making one prediction, such as what a shopper will buy or whether an alarm is real, to serve one decision inside a business.

*Why it expires:* The mechanism holds, but the picture of AI as a narrow, single-purpose tool does not describe general assistants that write, code, summarize and plan for anyone who asks.

### 3. Complements gain, substitutes lose · Evolves

Cheaper prediction lowers the value of its substitute, human prediction, and raises the value of its complements. The book names three complements: data, judgment and action. The 2016 article focuses on judgment: as prediction gets cheaper, demand for judgment should rise. In a 2018 *McKinsey Quarterly* interview Agrawal explains the other two: a company's data becomes more valuable, and a yogurt demand forecast is worth nothing unless a grocer decides how much to buy. The 2016 article also admits the line between prediction and judgment is not sharp, and that some judgment may be reframed as a series of predictions.

*Why it evolves:* Judgment still matters most, but models trained on the public internet make a firm's own data less decisive than the book suggested.

### 4. The anatomy of a decision · Transcends

A prediction is not a decision: deciding means applying judgment to a prediction and then acting. Cheap machine prediction forces a look at the anatomy of a decision: data, prediction, judgment, action and outcome. Goldfarb's ICAEW adaptation of the book uses an umbrella: the forecast gives the chance of rain, judgment is how much you mind getting wet or carrying it, and together they decide. People once did prediction and judgment together, so the split hardly mattered. Now it shows where a machine helps and where a person is needed.

*Why it transcends:* Breaking a decision into these parts is still the clearest way to see what an AI system is doing for you and what it is not.

### 5. The AI canvas · Evolves

The book's Tools part offers an AI canvas for decomposing a decision before building a tool for it. In the authors' 2018 HBR article it has seven boxes: prediction, judgment, action and outcome, plus three kinds of data. Training data calibrates the model, input data feeds each live prediction, and feedback data comes from the machine at work and improves it over time. The article's worked example is a home security venture deciding how to respond to alarms, over 97 percent of which are false.

*Why it evolves:* The canvas still suits a single, high-stakes decision, but much AI use now spans many small tasks, where it fits one step at a time.

### 6. Tasks, not jobs · Evolves

Jobs are bundles of tasks. The book's Tools part has managers break workflows into tasks, estimate the return from adding AI to each, and rank them. AI tools can remove tasks and add new ones; *strategy+business* reads this as a new round of reengineering, in which machines replace people in some tasks but rarely in whole jobs. Radiology is one example: machines may soon read images better, while radiologists keep roles that machines cannot (yet) fill.

*Why it evolves:* Task-level analysis holds, but general-purpose AI touches far more tasks in a job at once, which makes the line between task and job thinner.

### 7. Turning the prediction dial · Transcends

As prediction improves, the authors picture turning up a dial, like the volume knob on a speaker. Amazon's recommendations, they wrote in HBR in 2017, are right about 5 percent of the time. Turn it far enough and Amazon could flip from shopping-then-shipping to shipping-then-shopping, sending predicted items and collecting returns. In the book's strategy chapters, AI rises to a strategic question when a trade-off exists and cheaper prediction can reduce uncertainty enough to tip it. Using AI also brings trade-offs of its own: more speed, less accuracy; more data, less privacy.

*Why it transcends:* Asking how fast the dial is turning in your industry, and what flips at a threshold, remains a sharp strategic question for any AI capability.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Prediction Machines* is a rare business book that can be tested on its own subject. Written in 2018, before large language models reached the public, it claimed AI is cheap prediction. That turned out more literal than anyone could show then: today's chat assistants work by predicting the next word.

What holds is the economics. Cheap prediction spread into tasks nobody had framed as prediction, writing among them, and the anatomy of a decision and the prediction dial remain sharp tools.

What changes is the shape of the machine. The book pictures narrow tools, each trained on a firm's own data for one decision. Current systems are general, bought rather than built, and reach individual workers directly. That loosens the book's data advantage and blurs its task-by-task method, and it retires the idea that AI offers prediction and little else. The judgment question, the book's best idea, now sits partly in the instructions people write for AI systems. The authors' own sequel takes up who holds that judgment.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend, 3 evolve and 1 expires.

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Break down a decision

Split one decision into its parts to see where AI helps and where a person must decide.

```text
In "Prediction Machines," Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb break every decision into data, prediction, judgment, action and outcome. AI makes the prediction cheap; people still supply judgment, which means deciding how much each possible outcome matters.

Ask me four questions about the decision, how often it is made and what goes wrong today. Then lay out the five parts, name the prediction that matters most, say what data would feed it, and spell out the judgment a person still has to make, including any trade-offs between outcomes.

[paste a description of the decision here]
```

### 2. Fill in the AI canvas

Plan an AI tool for one decision before anyone builds it.

```text
The AI canvas from "Prediction Machines" has seven boxes: prediction (what you need to know), judgment (how you value each outcome), action, outcome, training data (to build the model), input data (for each live prediction) and feedback data (to improve it over time).

Ask me three questions about the decision and the data I have. Then fill in all seven boxes, flag any box where I lack data or a clear owner, and suggest the smallest test that would show whether better prediction pays off.

[paste a description of the decision and your available data here]
```

### 3. Turn the prediction dial

Find where better prediction could flip your business model.

```text
"Prediction Machines" pictures AI improvement as turning up a dial. Amazon's recommendations were right about 5 percent of the time; turned up far enough, the authors argue, Amazon could ship first and let customers return what they don't want. AI becomes strategic when a trade-off exists, reducing uncertainty could resolve it, and a prediction tool can reduce it enough to change the balance.

Ask me what my business sells, how customers buy, and which trade-offs we live with today. Then name the predictions that matter most, describe what would flip at much higher accuracy, and say what signs would show the dial is turning.

[paste a short description of your business here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

In the *Journal of Economic Literature*, the economist Laurence Kotlikoff called it a must-read for economists but faulted it for giving no clear definition of AI, for a focus on prediction that passes over developments he thinks matter more, such as self-checkout and phone payments in China, where no prediction is involved, and for ignoring macroeconomic effects, such as what happens to saving and growth if wages stagnate. He also doubted its new roles for displaced workers. Reviewing it for *The Strategy Bridge*, the US Air Force officer Steven Fino praised its fresh, unhyped view but found that it sometimes oversimplifies, as when its self-driving example rests on a single prediction, what would a human do? Our own caveats: the Amazon scenario is a thought experiment, and the examples predate general-purpose AI.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want a clear, unhyped economic frame for AI, or plan AI tools decision by decision and want the canvas in full. **Skip it** if you have read *Power and Prediction* and want only the big picture: that book restates the frame and carries it into whole systems, and the anatomy of a decision and the canvas above cover most of the tools this one adds.
