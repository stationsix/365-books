## At a glance

**Mathematical models that score people are sold as objective, but when they are secret, widely used and harmful they punish the poor, protect the comfortable and hide their own mistakes.**

Cathy O'Neil · 2016 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Cathy O'Neil earned a PhD in mathematics at Harvard, taught at Barnard College, and in 2007 left academia to work as a quant at the hedge fund D. E. Shaw, just as the financial crisis began. The crash showed her that mathematics was tangled up in the world's problems. She moved on to risk management and data science, joined Occupy Wall Street's alternative banking group in 2011, and wrote about flawed models on her blog, mathbabe.

Crown published the book in September 2016; it was longlisted for the National Book Award and won the Mathematical Association of America's Euler Book Prize in 2019.

The book argues that models deciding who gets hired, insured, lent to or jailed carry their builders' choices and blind spots. When such a model is opaque, works at scale and does damage, O'Neil calls it a weapon of math destruction. After an opening chapter on how models work and one on her own Wall Street years, the book moves through case studies: college rankings, for-profit college ads, policing and sentencing, hiring, work schedules, credit, insurance and political campaigns.

## Key concepts

### 1. Opacity, scale and damage · Transcends

The book's test for a weapon of math destruction has three parts. Opacity: the people scored cannot see how it works, often because it is a trade secret. Scale: it touches thousands or millions of lives. Damage: it works against the people it judges. The opening case is Sarah Wysocki, a well-regarded fifth-grade teacher at MacFarland Middle School in Washington, DC, who was fired along with more than 200 others after a low score from IMPACT, the district's value-added model. She promptly found a job at a school in an affluent northern Virginia district. A healthy model, by contrast, is transparent and gets feedback that tells its builders when it is wrong.

*Why it transcends:* The three questions apply word for word to an AI system that screens resumes or flags fraud.

### 2. Feedback loops that prove themselves · Transcends

A weapon of math destruction rarely learns that it was wrong. Nothing told Wysocki's model it had misjudged her. Many models create the data that confirms them. Predictive policing sends officers to poor neighborhoods, where they record more minor crimes, which sends more officers back. Risk scores used in sentencing ask about things like prior run-ins with police, which can stand in for race, and longer sentences then raise the odds of reoffending. O'Neil's phrase is that such models define their own reality and use it to justify their results. "Instead of searching for the truth, the score comes to embody it."

*Why it transcends:* Any system trained on its own past decisions can lock in the same loop, which is why AI builders now worry about models learning from their own outputs.

### 3. Proxies for what cannot be measured · Transcends

Models chase what is easy to count. U.S. News began ranking colleges in 1983 and built its first data-driven ranking in 1988, leaning on proxies such as SAT scores, acceptance rates and alumni giving while leaving out cost. Colleges spent heavily to move the numbers; Texas Christian University climbed 37 places in seven years. The same habit shows up in credit. Credit reports are used to screen job applicants and set car insurance prices. In Florida, the book reports, adults with clean driving records but poor credit paid an average of $1,552 more than drivers with excellent credit and a drunk driving conviction.

*Why it transcends:* Every AI system optimizes some measurable target, so the gap between the proxy and the real goal is still where things go wrong.

### 4. Screened out by a test you never see · Evolves

The hiring chapter follows Kyle Behm, a student who took time off from Vanderbilt to get treatment for bipolar disorder and later applied for a part-time, minimum-wage job at a Kroger supermarket. A personality test in a selection system built by Kronos "red-lighted" him, and he never got an interview. By one Deloitte estimate, 60 to 70 percent of US job applicants take such tests, and O'Neil notes they are generally poor at predicting performance. Once hired, low-wage workers at chains such as Starbucks and Walmart face scheduling software that sets shifts on short notice.

*Why it evolves:* Personality quizzes are giving way to AI that ranks resumes and video interviews, which deepens the opacity problem.

### 5. Targeting the vulnerable · Evolves

Online ads let a model find people at their weakest. For-profit colleges aimed ads and recruiters at people in hard times, such as those on public assistance, and courted veterans partly for the federal money that came with them. The book reports that the University of Phoenix spent $2,225 per student on marketing and $892 on instruction. The last chapter turns to voters: microtargeting shows each slice of the public a different message, which O'Neil sees as splintering shared debate.

*Why it evolves:* Generative AI can now write a fresh, personal pitch for every reader, which makes this concern sharper while the defenses are still being built.

### 6. A Hippocratic oath for modelers · Evolves

The book asks the builders to take responsibility and calls for a Hippocratic oath for data scientists. A 2016 *IEEE Spectrum* article on the book set this beside the modelers' oath that Emanuel Derman and Paul Wilmott wrote after the 2008 crisis, which pledges not to give users false comfort about a model's accuracy and to state its assumptions openly. She also argues that, for the sake of fairness, some data should simply stay out of the model.

*Why it evolves:* AI teams now publish principles and model documentation, but a personal oath carries little weight against commercial pressure.

### 7. Audit the models, then regulate · Transcends

O'Neil wants outsiders to test the models. Researchers can audit a model as a black box, feeding it inputs and studying what comes out, as Princeton's Web Transparency and Accountability Project was doing. She holds up the regulated FICO score as proof that a model can be transparent, and calls on policymakers to regulate the rest.

*Why it transcends:* Testing a system's outcomes rather than its code is now a central approach to AI accountability, because even the builders cannot fully explain how large models work.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Weapons of Math Destruction* was written about big data scoring models, before generative AI, and it holds up well. Its three-part test, its warning about self-confirming loops and its distrust of proxies apply directly to AI systems that decide who gets a loan, an interview or a second look from the police.

What changed is the depth of opacity. The book's black boxes were hidden by trade secrecy; today's large models are hard to explain even for their builders. That turns the book's remedy, outside testing of results, from a proposal into a necessity.

O'Neil went on from the book. She founded ORCAA, an algorithmic auditing firm, in 2016. In a written statement for the US Senate's AI Insight Forum on November 1, 2023, she argued that there is no universal safety checklist for AI. An audit should start from a specific use and ask who the system could fail, then monitor it continuously, like gauges in a cockpit. Since nobody, even the inventors, can account for each word a large language model produces, she favors measuring outcomes.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve.

### 2. Find the proxy and the loop

Spot where a metric has replaced the real goal and started to prove itself.

```text
"Weapons of Math Destruction" shows how models use proxies, such as test scores, ZIP codes or credit reports, for things they cannot measure directly, and how feedback loops let a model create the data that confirms it, as when predictive policing sends officers back to the neighborhoods where they already recorded the most crime.

Ask me three questions about the metric and how people react to it. Then name what the metric is standing in for, list ways people could game it, describe any loop where the score shapes the outcome it predicts, and propose a way to check it against the real goal.

[paste the metric and how it is used here]
```

```text

Ask me four questions about the decision, the people affected and the data used. Then list each group of people who have a stake, what failure would look like for each, which failures matter most, the tests that would detect them, and what an appeal process should include.

[paste a description of the automated decision here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

In *The Scholarly Kitchen*, Kent Anderson found the solutions not entirely clear, and Charles Humble at InfoQ judged the fixes weaker than the diagnosis. Owen King, reviewing for Loyola University Chicago's Center for Digital Ethics, found the "weapons" label a bit misleading, since most of the harm is accidental, and said the three-part definition explains little about why such models arise. In *Plus* magazine, Stephen Kissler felt the early bravado made the claims sound exaggerated. In *Education Review*, Francis Schrag noted that human judgment is fallible too, and research cannot settle whether algorithms or experts decide better.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you build, buy or are judged by systems that score people and want a vivid guide to how they go wrong. **Skip it** if you know the cases or want hands-on methods: *Hello World* offers a more balanced view of algorithms, and *AI Snake Oil* applies the same skepticism to today's AI claims.
