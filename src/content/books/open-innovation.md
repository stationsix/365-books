## At a glance

**Useful knowledge is now spread far beyond any one company's lab, so firms should bring outside ideas in, send unused ideas out, and let the business model decide what a technology is worth.**

Henry Chesbrough · 2003 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Henry Chesbrough spent the 1980s in product management and marketing at the disk drive maker Quantum, then ran his own product and marketing consultancy before earning a Berkeley PhD. He wrote this book as an assistant professor at Harvard Business School and later moved to Berkeley Haas, where he founded a center on corporate innovation.

The puzzle that starts the book is Xerox PARC, the lab that produced Ethernet and the graphical user interface while Xerox, focused on copiers and printers, profited little from them. Chesbrough's answer is not that Xerox was badly run: it ran the lab by the best research practice of its era, a closed model in which a firm invents, develops and sells everything inside its own walls. That model, he argues, is breaking down, and the replacement is open innovation: internal and external ideas, taken to market by internal and external paths.

An introduction and ten chapters move from Xerox and the two paradigms through the business model, three company cases (IBM, Intel and Lucent), intellectual property, a practical chapter titled "What Do I Do Monday Morning?" and public policy.

## Key concepts

### 1. Xerox PARC and the false negative · Transcends

Chapter 1 uses PARC to show how a strong lab can still lose its best work. Many PARC projects lost internal funding because their results looked unclear and weakly tied to Xerox's market. Their value appeared mainly after researchers left for smaller companies or founded their own, such as 3Com. Unable to copy Xerox's vertical integration, they built models that worked with other companies' products. Chesbrough's companion research on 35 Xerox spinoffs (Research Policy, 2003) found that spinoffs governed by outside investors searched more widely for markets. The lesson is a new kind of error. Firms work hard to kill false positives, projects that look good and fail, and too little to catch false negatives, projects that look weak to them but are valuable elsewhere.

*Why it transcends:* Any organization that screens ideas against its current business will reject some that fit another one, whatever tools it uses.

### 2. The closed model and its erosion · Expires

Chapter 2 describes closed innovation, the model of the great twentieth-century corporate labs. Hire the smartest people, do the most research, get to market first, and guard the intellectual property so rivals cannot profit. It worked for decades. In a 2003 ACM Ubiquity interview about the book, Chesbrough named the forces that undid it: workers who carry knowledge when they change jobs, a college population that grew after the GI Bill, a venture capital industry vastly larger than 30 years earlier, universities closing the gap with industrial research, and the 1980 Bayh-Dole Act letting universities patent and license their work.

*Why it expires:* Code, papers and models now move between firms in days, so research kept fully in-house rarely stays an advantage for long.

### 3. The open innovation principles · Transcends

The book sets six open principles against six closed ones, on talent, R&D, speed, business models, ideas and intellectual property. The first is the best known: not all the smart people work for us. External R&D can create significant value, and internal R&D is needed to claim part of it. A firm does not have to originate research to profit from it. Winning comes from making the best use of internal and external ideas, not from having the most of them. And firms should profit when others use their IP, and buy others' IP when it advances their own model.

*Why it transcends:* The more knowledge sits outside any one firm, the more this holds, and AI spreads knowledge further.

### 4. The business model as focusing device · Transcends

The business model chapter argues that a technology has no fixed value on its own. Its worth depends on the business model that takes it to market, so the same invention can be worth little to one firm and a great deal to another. One open principle puts it plainly: building a better business model beats getting to market first. A 2002 paper by Chesbrough and Richard Rosenbloom on the Xerox spinoffs adds a warning: a successful model gives a firm a logic for judging technology, and that same logic narrows its search for other uses.

*Why it transcends:* AI capabilities spread fast, so the model wrapped around them decides who gets paid, which makes this the book's most durable idea.

### 5. Many paths to market for ideas and IP · Evolves

Chapters 7 and 8 treat intellectual property as something to trade, not just to defend. Ideas can reach market through the firm's own products, through licensing, through spinoffs or through sale, and each path can bring revenue. Chapter 7 studies Lucent's New Ventures Group, set up to build new ventures out of internal Bell Labs technologies. Chapter 8 links IP strategy to the business model: license in outside IP to keep pace, and license out the firm's own IP before it becomes obsolete.

*Why it evolves:* Open model weights, APIs and licensed data are new paths to market, but whether training on others' work is a use that must be licensed is still being argued.

### 6. Research as knowledge broker · Evolves

The IBM and Intel chapters show two routes to openness. IBM, after near-collapse in the early 1990s, pushed its research to market faster and opened up, investing in software it does not own, such as Java and Linux, and growing a services integration business far more open than its mainframe past. Intel, the largest semiconductor company, did remarkably little internal research for its size, funding university work and startups instead. In a Working Knowledge excerpt from the book, the research function expands from generating knowledge to brokering it in and out of the firm, with outside knowledge rewarded as highly as inside work.

*Why it evolves:* AI makes scanning outside knowledge cheap, so the scarce skill shifts to judging what is sound and fitting it into the firm.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book predates today's AI assistants. Chesbrough has since co-written a 2024 California Management Review article with Marcus Holgersson, Linus Dahlander and Marcel Bogers arguing that AI can enhance, enable or replace traditional open innovation practices, both for bringing ideas in and for sending them out. The rest is our reading.

The core claim holds more strongly than in 2003. An AI assistant lets a small team scan papers, patents and code at a scale that once needed a corporate lab. The false-negative idea also gains force: a firm can now prototype an idea that does not fit its model far more cheaply before deciding whether to license or spin it out.

What changes is the shape of the flows. Model providers and foundation models sit between firms and outside knowledge, so openness can mean dependence on a few suppliers. And the closed model is not entirely gone: some of the most valuable AI research happens inside a handful of well-funded labs that guard their work, which is a reminder that the open and closed labels describe a range, not a choice.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend, 2 evolve and 1 expires.

## Critiques and caveats

The strongest critique is that the book sets up a false dichotomy. Paul Trott and Dap Hartmann (*International Journal of Innovation Management*, 2009) accept the limits of closed innovation but argue that few firms ever followed the closed principles as starkly as the book suggests. In a later follow-up paper they cite older practice: research in the 1960s on gatekeepers who linked labs to outside knowledge, alliances such as Airbus, and licensing such as Pilkington's float glass. They also note that leakage and espionage cases argue for tight IP protection. A 2003 review by Rich Mironov, though positive, noted that the book is framed from a big company's point of view.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run R&D, corporate venturing, licensing or partnerships in a large firm and want the original case for open innovation with its Xerox, IBM, Intel and Lucent stories. **Skip it** if you want the idea in brief: the principles table and the Xerox story carry most of it, and *Democratizing Innovation* covers the user side of outside ideas, free online.
