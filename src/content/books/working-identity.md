## At a glance

**People do not find a new career by first working out who they really are; they find it by trying out possible selves in small experiments, new company and a story that keeps changing until it fits.**

Herminia Ibarra · 2003 · Mindset for Builders · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Herminia Ibarra was born in Cuba, came to the United States as a child, and earned her PhD in organizational behavior at Yale. She taught at Harvard Business School from 1989 to 2002, then moved to INSEAD, and since 2017 has held the Charles Handy chair at London Business School. *Working Identity* came out of a study of 39 managers and professionals who changed, or tried to change, careers, with an average of three interviews each over two to three years.

The book takes aim at the standard advice: look inward, take the tests, find your true self, then make the leap. Ibarra argues that this order is backward. People are made of many possible selves, and they learn which one to pursue by acting, not by thinking harder. Part One, "Identity in Transition," covers possible selves, the time between identities and deep change. Part Two, "Identity in Practice," sets out three practices: crafting experiments, shifting connections and making sense. An appendix explains the research. This summary follows the argument as the original edition set it out; the 2023 updated edition keeps the same chapter titles and adds a new preface, new examples and action exercises, which are not summarized here.

## Key concepts

### 1. Doing comes first, knowing second · Transcends

Ibarra calls the standard approach "plan and implement." In her 2002 *Harvard Business Review* article, "How to Stay Stuck in the Wrong Career," she boils it down to three steps: know thyself through introspection and assessments, consult trusted advisers, and think big with one decisive move. Each step, she argues, tends to keep people where they are. Her alternative is "test and learn": try things, see how they feel and how others respond, and adjust. Self-knowledge arrives as a result of action. As the article puts it, "Doing comes first, knowing second."

*Why it transcends:* When the jobs on offer keep changing shape, no amount of reflection can tell someone in advance what a new role will feel like. Testing is the only reliable way to find out.

### 2. Possible selves · Transcends

The idea of possible selves comes from the psychologist Hazel Markus, whose work Ibarra cites. In her companion paper of 2004, possible selves are the images of who we hope to become, think we should become or fear becoming. A career changer is not uncovering one hidden true self but sorting through several candidates, some vivid, some vague. Ibarra's evidence points to a further lesson: pain with the old job alone tends to freeze people, and they move when they have tangible, enticing alternatives to move toward. The work, then, is to make those alternatives concrete enough to judge.

*Why it transcends:* A list of options someone can actually picture and test beats a vague wish for change, whatever is driving the change.

### 3. Between identities · Evolves

The middle of a transition is messy. In a 2004 working paper, Ibarra describes a stretch in which a person's identity is multiple, ill defined and provisional, moving away from something without yet having left it. In the HBR article she says most career transitions take about three years, and in the companion paper she puts major ones at three to five. The danger that paper names is foreclosure: retreating to the old role, or grabbing the wrong next job just to end the discomfort. Ibarra treats this period as necessary, not as a sign of failure.

*Why it evolves:* The in-between period used to be something people chose to enter. When AI reshapes a role from outside, many people land in it without choosing, so the skill of living there well matters more.

### 4. Crafting experiments · Evolves

The first practice is to try new roles on a small scale before committing: side projects, freelance work, temporary assignments, courses. In the HBR article, Gary McCarthy, a former investment banker and consultant, drew up a business plan with his wife for a wine-tour company, qualified as a scuba instructor and took freelance projects with young companies before landing as an internal venture capitalist at a media company he admired. Susan Fontaine, a consulting partner, jumped to a near-identical strategy job, quit after two weeks, then freelanced while doing pro bono charity work, which led her into the nonprofit sector.

*Why it evolves:* AI makes many experiments faster and cheaper: a prototype, a sample portfolio or a market scan in an afternoon. The risk is mistaking a quick simulation for the real test, which still means doing the work with real people.

### 5. Shifting connections · Evolves

The second practice is about people. Ibarra argues that friends, family and long-time colleagues, however well meaning, tend to reinforce the identity a person is trying to leave. Gary's circle steered him toward finance roles. New identities take shape among new people: role models, communities and mentors in the field a person is moving toward. In the HBR article, Harris Roberts, a regulatory affairs director, became president and COO of a medical device company through a relationship with its founder, who had first come to him for regulatory advice.

*Why it evolves:* An AI assistant can help someone find communities and rehearse conversations, but it cannot vouch for them. The sponsor who opens a door is still a person in the new field.

### 6. Making sense · Transcends

The third practice is storytelling. Career changers build a story that links who they were to who they are becoming, and the story improves by being told and retold in public, even before it is fully formed. Ibarra notes that dramatic moments of truth tend to arrive late. In the article, John Alexander, an investment banker who wanted to write fiction, later wrote a newspaper column about the reading an astrologer gave him. Ibarra's point is that "the moment of insight is an effect, not a cause, of change."

*Why it transcends:* A clear account of why a winding path makes sense is what employers and clients ask for, and it matters more as careers become less linear.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Working Identity* was written about voluntary, mid-career reinvention by well-credentialed professionals. The AI era makes its method relevant to far more people, many of whom did not choose to change.

In a 2024 interview with Roland Berger's *Think:Act* magazine, Ibarra listed technological change that eliminates or reshapes jobs among the reasons nonlinear careers have become more common. In an April 2026 London Business School article she co-wrote, reporting her conversation with Michael Jacobides in an LBS masterclass, she framed AI as a leadership challenge about people's ability to use the technology, a change the article says unsettles people's sense of identity. She urged leaders to redefine their own jobs, and said experimentation needs psychological safety. Beyond those public remarks, what follows is our reading.

What holds is the order: act, then learn. What changes is the cost of each step. Experiments are cheaper, and an assistant can help draft the story and map new networks. What does not change is that identity forms through real work, real feedback and real relationships, and that the in-between period still takes time.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. List your possible selves

Turn a vague wish for change into options you can test.

```text
Herminia Ibarra's "Working Identity" argues that people don't find a new career by discovering one true self. We each have several possible selves (who we hope, think we should, or fear to become), and we move when an alternative is concrete and enticing, not just because the current job hurts.

Ask me five questions about what I find myself drawn to, what I envy in other people's work, and what I'd hate to become. Then list four or five possible selves in concrete terms (the work, the people, a typical week), rate how vivid each one is, and tell me which two are worth testing first.
```

### 2. Design three small experiments

Test a possible self without quitting your job.

```text
"Working Identity" says career change works by "test and learn," not "plan and implement." The first practice is crafting experiments: side projects, freelance work, temporary assignments or courses that let you try a new role on a small scale while keeping your current job. Each experiment should teach you something, even if it fails.

Ask me four questions about my time, money, current job and the possible self I want to test. Then design three experiments of increasing commitment, each with a clear question it answers, a time box, the real people involved, and a sign that would tell me to stop or go further.

[paste the possible self you want to test here]
```

### 3. Draft your transition story

Tell a story that links your past to where you're heading.

```text
In "Working Identity," Herminia Ibarra argues that career changers must make sense of their change by telling a story that connects who they were to who they are becoming, and that the story gets better by being told in public. Dramatic moments of insight usually come late, as an effect of change rather than its cause.

Ask me three questions about the threads that run through my past work and what I've tried recently. Then draft a two-minute version of my story for someone in the field I'm moving toward, flag any part that sounds like an excuse, and suggest who I should tell it to next.

[paste notes on your career so far and recent experiments here]
```

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my career transition coach, grounded in Herminia Ibarra's "Working Identity." Core ideas: doing comes first and knowing second, so test and learn instead of plan and implement; we each have several possible selves, and we move when one becomes concrete and enticing; the time between identities is messy, often lasts years, and is necessary; crafting small experiments, shifting connections toward people in the new field, and telling and retelling the story are the three practices that move a transition forward.

```

## Critiques and caveats

The evidence is qualitative and narrow: 39 people, about two-thirds of them men, mostly highly credentialed, and some interviews looked back on changes already made, so memory may tidy the story. In a 2021 interview with *The Psychologist*, Ibarra said the original research lacked racial diversity and drew on people with at least a university degree. The method also assumes slack. Side projects and years of exploration take time, savings and contacts that many workers, especially those displaced rather than restless, may not have. The stories are vivid, but they come from people who found their way, so the book cannot show how often test and learn fails.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you are thinking about a career change, or you coach or manage people who are, and you want a method grounded in research rather than personality tests. **Skip it** if you are happy in your field and want help growing inside it; *So Good They Can't Ignore You* by Cal Newport makes the case for building skill where you are.
