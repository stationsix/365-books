## At a glance

**Most explanations of why companies win are stories told after the fact: once you know a firm did well, its strategy, culture and leaders all look brilliant, and the "research" behind many bestsellers measures that glow, not its causes.**

Phil Rosenzweig · 2007 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Phil Rosenzweig is professor emeritus of strategy and international management at IMD in Lausanne. He was earlier an assistant professor at Harvard Business School, and before that worked at Hewlett-Packard. His PhD is from Wharton.

The book asks an unusual question. Most business writers ask what leads to high performance. Rosenzweig asks why high performance is so hard to understand. His answer is that managers, journalists and many researchers fall for a set of delusions, errors of judgment that distort how company performance is explained. He counts nine, and argues that many popular bestsellers, for all their claims of research, "operate mainly at the level of storytelling."

The tone is skeptical and witty, and the targets are named: *In Search of Excellence*, *Built to Last*, *Good to Great* and *What Really Works*, along with business journalism and parts of academic research. The book ends not with a formula but with an argument that strategy is a matter of choices made under uncertainty, and that execution is uncertain too.

## Key concepts

### 1. The halo effect · Transcends

The term comes from psychology: Edward Thorndike identified it in 1920. In Rosenzweig's article on the same research, it is "the basic human tendency to make specific inferences on the basis of a general impression." In business, that impression usually comes from financial results. His main case is Cisco. In the late 1990s the press praised its leadership, culture, customer focus and acquisitions. After the stock fell from about $80 in 2000 to $14 a year later, the same features were recast as failings, though, he argues, the company had changed little. ABB went through the same reversal. Experiments by the psychologist Barry Staw make the point: groups told they had performed well described themselves as more cohesive than groups told they had done badly, though all had done about equally well.

*Why it transcends:* Any judgment of a company, a team or a product launch is still colored by how the numbers came out. Analysts, investors and AI tools that read the coverage all inherit the glow.

### 2. Correlation, causality and the wrong end of the stick · Transcends

Two linked delusions. The first is assuming that because two things move together, one causes the other. The second is getting the direction backward. Low employee turnover may not drive high performance; a growing company can offer stimulating work and promotion, so people stay. Rosenzweig does not deny that leadership, culture and engagement matter. He questions which way the arrow points.

*Why it transcends:* Dashboards and analytics make it easier than ever to find things that move together. Telling which one causes the other still takes deliberate thought.

### 3. Connecting the winning dots · Evolves

This is the method at the heart of many classic business books: pick companies that did well, study them, and list what they share. Without a comparison group of firms that did poorly, the shared traits prove nothing, because failing companies may have the same traits. Worse, traits measured through press articles and interviews done after the success reflect the halo. Rosenzweig says *Good to Great* relied heavily on such sources, so much of what it found reflected the halo rather than causes. He also tested *In Search of Excellence*: tracking the 35 of its companies in the Compustat database, he found most had not kept pace with the market.

*Why it evolves:* Comparing winners with losers across thousands of firms is now cheap, so there is less excuse for studying only winners. But AI models trained on business writing absorb the same after-the-fact stories, so the bias can return in a new form.

### 4. Rigorous research and single explanations · Evolves

The delusion of rigorous research is the belief that a large volume of data makes a study valid. If the data is contaminated by the halo, more of it does not help. The delusion of single explanations is the habit of crediting success to one factor at a time, such as culture, customer focus or leadership, then adding up the effects. Because these factors are closely linked, their effects overlap, and adding them overstates how much any formula can deliver.

*Why it evolves:* AI can generate large, polished analyses in minutes, which makes volume even easier to confuse with rigor. The fix is the same one the book gives: ask where the data came from and whether it could have been shaped by the outcome.

### 5. Lasting success and absolute performance · Transcends

The delusion of lasting success is the belief that a recipe can keep a company on top for decades. Rosenzweig argues that high performance rarely lasts. The delusion of absolute performance is judging a company in isolation. Performance is relative: rivals act too, so following a formula cannot guarantee results. A firm can execute well and still fall behind if competitors do better.

*Why it transcends:* Fast-moving markets, including AI itself, show how quickly an edge can be copied. Measuring progress against rivals, not against last year, matters more than ever.

### 6. Organizational physics and strategy as choice · Transcends

The last delusion is that business follows predictable laws, like physics, so that the right inputs reliably produce the right results. Rosenzweig rejects this. Instead he offers a few working principles. Strategy is about choice, and good strategies involve risk, so no strategy is foolproof. Execution is uncertain as well, and practices that work in one company may fail in another. Chance plays a larger role than managers like to admit, so good decisions can turn out badly and bad ones can turn out well. A bad outcome does not prove a mistake, and a good one does not prove brilliance. Yet once the choice is made, the best managers act with persistence, as though chance did not matter.

*Why it transcends:* AI can sharpen estimates of the odds, but it cannot make business outcomes certain. Thinking in probabilities and judging decisions apart from results is a lasting habit.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Halo Effect* is less a strategy book than a guide to reading strategy books, which helps it age well: how people reason about success has not changed.

Four concepts transcend. The halo itself, the confusion of cause and effect, the relative nature of performance and the role of chance all apply to any company, product or team. They apply to AI companies too: praise and blame for AI firms can swing with valuations the way Cisco's did.

Two evolve. Studying only winners and confusing volume with rigor are easier to avoid now that comparative data is cheap, and easier to fall into, because AI tools produce confident analyses from business writing shaped by after-the-fact stories. An AI summary of why a company succeeded may be a summary of its press coverage.

The practical habit the book teaches, asking how you would judge a company if you did not know its results, is a good test to apply to any analysis, human or machine.

Verdict: 4 of 6 key concepts transcend and 2 evolve. None expire.

### 1. Test a success story

Check whether an explanation of why a company or team succeeded holds up.

```text
In "The Halo Effect," Phil Rosenzweig argues that once we know a company performed well, we judge its strategy, culture and leaders favorably, so explanations of success often describe the glow rather than the cause. He warns against studying only winners, confusing correlation with causation, getting cause and effect backward, and crediting success to a single factor.

Ask me two questions about where this explanation came from and when it was written. Then list each claimed cause, say whether it was measured independently of the results, suggest a comparison group of similar firms that did worse, and name any place where the arrow could point the other way.

[paste the success story, case study or article here]
```

### 2. Judge a decision apart from its outcome

Review a past decision on how it was made, not just how it turned out.

```text
Rosenzweig ("The Halo Effect") says strategy is about choice and choices involve risk, so good decisions can turn out badly and bad ones can turn out well. Managers should judge a decision by the information, assumptions and reasoning behind it, and recognize the role of chance.

Ask me three questions about the decision: what we knew at the time, what options we considered, and what we expected to happen. Then assess the quality of the decision separately from its result, say how much of the outcome looks like chance, and name one thing to do differently next time regardless of the result.

[paste a description of the decision and what happened here]
```

### 3. Measure performance against rivals

Find out whether you are actually gaining ground or just improving.

```text
"The Halo Effect" argues that performance is relative, not absolute: competitors act too, they imitate, and advantages erode, so a firm can improve on every measure and still fall behind. Lasting success is rare.

Ask me who my main competitors are and which measures matter most in my market. Then compare my recent progress with theirs on each measure, point out where I improved but lost ground, and suggest which advantages are most likely to be copied next.

[paste your recent results and anything you know about competitors here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Publishers Weekly called it a tart takedown but said Rosenzweig hedges his own principles so heavily that managers may find them of little practical use. The book is stronger at critique than at alternatives. Even the economist John Kay, who praised it, called *In Search of Excellence* and *Built to Last* great stories with genuine insights; their fault is the claimed scientific rigor. The book's own evidence is also a set of cases and reanalyses rather than a new study. Its examples come mostly from around the dot-com bust, and by its own argument, cases like these date quickly.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you read business books, case studies or consulting reports and want a reliable way to tell evidence from storytelling. **Skip it** if you want a step-by-step strategy method; *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* gives more practical guidance.
