## At a glance

**A firm's advantage lives in the separate activities it performs, so cost and differentiation are won or lost activity by activity, and the value chain is the tool for finding where.**

Michael E. Porter · 1985 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Five years after *Competitive Strategy*, Michael Porter, a Harvard Business School professor trained in engineering and business economics, turned from the industry to the individual firm. The earlier book explained which industries are attractive and named three generic strategies. This one asks how a firm actually achieves low cost or differentiation, and how it keeps the lead.

The answer is the value chain. Porter breaks a company into the discrete activities it performs to design, make, sell, deliver and support its product, and treats each activity as a possible source of cost or uniqueness. Advantage comes from the value a firm creates for its buyers beyond what that value costs to create.

Published by the Free Press, the book's 15 chapters open with a recap of the five forces and generic strategies, then cover cost, differentiation, technology and choosing competitors; segmentation and substitution; interrelationships among the units of a diversified company; and finally scenarios, defense and attacking a leader.

## Key concepts

### 1. The value chain and the value system · Transcends

Porter divides a firm into nine categories of activity. Five are primary: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Four are support activities that run across them: procurement, technology development, human resource management and firm infrastructure. Advantage seldom comes from one activity alone. Linkages within the chain matter, where the way one activity is performed changes the cost or performance of another. Each firm's chain also sits inside a larger value system of supplier, channel and buyer chains, and these vertical linkages are a source of advantage too.

*Why it transcends:* AI is adopted activity by activity, not company-wide, so a map of discrete activities is the natural place to ask where it lowers cost or adds value.

### 2. Cost drivers and reconfiguring the chain · Evolves

To understand cost, Porter assigns costs and assets to each activity and asks what drives them. He names ten cost drivers: economies of scale, learning, the pattern of capacity utilization, linkages, interrelationships with sister units, integration, timing, discretionary policies, location and institutional factors such as regulation. A firm gains cost advantage by controlling these drivers better than rivals or by reconfiguring the value chain, performing activities differently. Discretionary policies, such as choices about product features, delivery time and process technology, are the driver most directly in management's hands.

*Why it evolves:* The method holds, but AI changes the weights. Learning and scale in data and models can now outrun scale in plants, and some activities fall close to zero marginal cost.

### 3. Differentiation and buyer value · Evolves

Differentiation, for Porter, means being unique at something buyers value, enough to earn a price premium that exceeds the extra cost of being unique. Uniqueness has drivers much like those of cost, such as policy choices, linkages, timing, location and learning. Buyer value comes from lowering the buyer's cost or raising the buyer's performance, so the analyst studies the buyer's own value chain. Porter separates use criteria, which reflect that value, from signaling criteria such as reputation, advertising, packaging and even price, which buyers use to infer it.

*Why it evolves:* The distinction sharpens when buyers, or software acting for them, compare specifications directly. Signals may count for less, and proven performance for more.

### 4. Technology as a competitive lever · Transcends

Porter places technology throughout the value chain, not only in research labs, and argues that not every technological change is strategically beneficial. Its weight comes from how it changes a firm's cost or differentiation, how long rivals take to match it, and how it reshapes the structure of the industry. A firm chooses which technologies to develop, whether to lead or follow, since being first has costs as well as benefits, and whether to license.

*Why it transcends:* These questions fit AI investments closely: whether a lead can be sustained, whether it moves cost or uniqueness, and whether it leaves the industry better or worse for everyone in it.

### 5. Good competitors · Evolves

Porter argues that some rivals help. Good competitors can increase a firm's own competitive advantage, improve the structure of the industry, help develop the market and deter entry. The aim is not maximum share but the right configuration of rivals. That means keeping good competitors viable, taking care not to damage them while battling bad ones, and where possible turning bad competitors into good ones.

*Why it evolves:* The logic holds, but platform markets and rapid AI entrants make a stable set of well-behaved rivals harder to arrange or count on.

### 6. Segmentation and substitution · Transcends

Industry segmentation divides an industry along variables such as product variety, type of buyer, channel and geography, then asks which segments are attractive, how they are related, and whether a firm should focus or target broadly. Substitution treats a product that performs the same function from outside the industry as a competitor. Its threat depends on relative value compared with price, the buyer's switching costs and the buyer's propensity to substitute, and Porter traces the path a substitution follows over time.

*Why it transcends:* Substitution is the question AI forces on many products: whether a buyer gets more value for the price, how costly the switch is, and how quickly adoption will climb.

### 7. Interrelationships and horizontal strategy · Evolves

For diversified companies, Porter argues that the corporate level adds value by exploiting interrelationships among business units. Tangible interrelationships share value activities, at a cost as well as a benefit. Intangible ones transfer management know-how. Competitor interrelationships arise when the same rivals meet a company in several markets. Horizontal strategy is a coordinated set of goals across related units, and a full chapter covers the organizational impediments in its way.

*Why it evolves:* Shared data and models are now among the most valuable activities to share across units, but the same coordination costs and turf barriers still apply.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Competitive Advantage* reads well today because it works at the level where AI arrives: the single activity. A company does not "adopt AI" all at once. It changes how it handles inbound logistics, service, sales or technology development, and each change moves a cost driver or a driver of uniqueness. The value chain tells a manager where to look, and the chapter on technology asks whether a gain will last.

What changes is how long an activity-based edge holds. When the tools that reconfigure a chain are available to every competitor, a cost advantage from a better process can be copied quickly. The book's insistence on linkages points to what still protects a lead: a set of activities that is harder to copy as a whole than any one part.

The AI reading here is ours, not the book's.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

Omar Aktouf and colleagues (2005) argue that the value chain treats a firm as the sum of separable parts and leaves out corporate culture, worker morale and team spirit, and, following Jeremy Klein, they call Porter's definition of competitive advantage tautological. The resource-based view, set out by Jay Barney in 1991, places advantage in valuable, rare, hard-to-imitate resources rather than in activities and position. Writers on technology-intensive industries, such as Frank Rothaermel, stress that advantages there are temporary and must be renewed through continued innovation. Our own caveats: the book is long and dense, and much of its corporate strategy material assumes the large diversified firm of the 1980s.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you work on operations, cost or pricing strategy and want the full method for tracing advantage to specific activities, including the chapters on technology and substitution. **Skip it** if you want the gist; Porter's 1987 *Harvard Business Review* article "From Competitive Advantage to Corporate Strategy" covers the value chain and sharing activities across units in far fewer pages.
