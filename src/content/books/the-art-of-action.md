## At a glance

**Plans fail in contact with reality because of three gaps, in knowledge, alignment and effects, and the cure is not more detail but clear intent: tell people what to achieve and why, then let them work out how.**

Stephen Bungay · 2011 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Stephen Bungay is a military historian and management writer. He spent 17 years with the Boston Consulting Group in London and Munich and later became a director of the Ashridge Strategic Management Centre in London. His earlier books include *The Most Dangerous Enemy*, a history of the Battle of Britain, and *Alamein*.

*The Art of Action* starts from a problem every manager knows. Companies make sensible plans, yet what happens on the ground drifts away from what was intended, and results drift further still. Bungay argues this is not a failure of people or planning but is built into acting in an uncertain world.

To find a better answer, he goes back to the Prussian army of the 19th century. After its crushing defeat by Napoleon in 1806, it rebuilt how it commanded, and under Helmuth von Moltke the Elder it developed what is now called mission command. Bungay retells that history in detail, then translates it into a practical method for business leaders, with cases ranging from pharmaceuticals to Formula 1 racing.

## Key concepts

### 1. Friction and the three gaps · Transcends

Bungay borrows the idea of friction from the Prussian theorist Carl von Clausewitz: the many small, unpredictable things that make real action harder than plans assume. He breaks friction into three gaps. The knowledge gap is the difference between what we would like to know and what we actually know. The alignment gap is the difference between what we want people to do and what they actually do. The effects gap is the difference between what we hope our actions will achieve and what they actually achieve. Friction can be reduced but never eliminated. The question is how an organization behaves in spite of them.

*Why it transcends:* No amount of data removes uncertainty about competitors, customers or how people will respond. The three gaps are a useful checklist for any plan, in any era.

### 2. Why more detail makes it worse · Evolves

Bungay observes that managers respond to each gap in a natural but harmful way. Faced with the knowledge gap, they demand more detailed information. Faced with the alignment gap, they issue more detailed instructions. Faced with the effects gap, they impose more detailed controls. Each response adds work and pulls control upward, away from the people who can see what is happening. Pushed far enough, this leads to centralization, reliance on metrics and finally paralysis.

*Why it evolves:* AI makes detailed reports, instructions and monitoring almost free to produce, so the temptation the book describes grows stronger. The cost Bungay names, slower and less adaptive action, has not gone away.

### 3. Mission command: intent over instructions · Transcends

The Prussian answer, which Bungay recommends, inverts each response. Instead of more information, leaders limit direction to defining and communicating intent. Instead of detailed instructions, each level works out what it will do to realize that intent and reports back. Instead of tighter controls, individuals have freedom to adjust their actions as conditions change, as long as they stay within the intent. Intent means what is to be achieved and why, not how. Moltke's principle, as Bungay presents it, was to command no more than necessary and plan no further than the circumstances could be foreseen.

*Why it transcends:* Teams that understand the purpose behind an order can act sensibly when the order no longer fits. That holds for a sales team, a software team or a group of people supervising AI tools.

### 4. Directed opportunism · Transcends

Bungay's name for mission command in business is directed opportunism. Managers often assume alignment and autonomy pull against each other: control tightly and lose initiative, or let go and lose alignment. Bungay argues the opposite. Alignment should be built around intent and autonomy granted around actions, so the more alignment an organization has, the more autonomy it can grant. Linear plan-and-implement gives way to a loop he describes as do-and-adapt, in which people seize opportunities that serve the overall direction.

*Why it transcends:* The tension between central direction and local judgment is permanent in any organization that grows. Bungay's framing is one of the clearest ways to talk about it.

### 5. Briefing and backbriefing · Evolves

The method runs through a cascade. A leader briefs the next level on the higher intent (up to two levels up), the tasks it implies, the main effort, and the freedoms and constraints. That level then backbriefs: it explains back what it understands and what it plans to do. The backbrief exposes misunderstandings early and shows leaders the implications of their own direction, which they can then revise. Cascaded down the organization, the process aligns it up, down and across functions.

*Why it evolves:* AI tools can help draft a brief, compare backbriefs across teams and flag where plans drift from the stated intent. The human conversation that tests understanding still has to happen.

### 6. Strategy as direction, not a plan · Evolves

Bungay defines strategy as "a framework for decision making, a guide to thoughtful, purposive action": fundamentally an intent, not a fixed plan. Making strategy and carrying it out are not separate stages; decisions and actions evolve together. He links this to the military idea of a main effort: the single thing that will have the greatest impact or on which everything else depends. It gets priority for resources and gives people at every level a focus when they meet something unexpected.

*Why it evolves:* When tools change as fast as they do with AI, a strategy people can reinterpret on the spot beats a detailed roadmap, though teams may need to revisit it more often.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Art of Action* is about a problem AI does not solve, people acting together under uncertainty, so most of it ages well.

Three concepts transcend. Friction and the three gaps, intent over instructions, and directed opportunism describe how any organization turns a plan into results. Intent also maps onto a newer question, how to direct AI agents: stating the outcome and why, then checking the agent's plan before it acts, looks a lot like briefing and backbriefing.

Three evolve: the warning about piling on detail matters more now that reports and controls are cheap to generate, backbriefing can be supported by tools, and strategy as intent may need revisiting more often.

The main risk is the one Bungay names. Easy data and easy monitoring invite leaders to control more tightly, which slows the people closest to the work.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Find the three gaps in a plan

Spot where a plan is likely to break down before it does.

```text
In "The Art of Action," Stephen Bungay argues that plans run into three gaps: the knowledge gap (what we would like to know versus what we know), the alignment gap (what we want people to do versus what they do) and the effects gap (what we hope our actions will achieve versus what they achieve). Managers usually respond with more information, more instructions and more controls, which makes things worse.

Ask me three questions about the plan, who will carry it out and how we will know it is working. Then name the biggest risk in each gap, say where I am likely to over-respond with detail, and suggest a lighter response based on clear intent.

[paste your plan here]
```

### 2. Write a statement of intent

Turn a set of instructions into direction people can act on when things change.

```text
Bungay ("The Art of Action") recommends that leaders limit direction to intent: what is to be achieved and why, plus the main effort and key constraints, leaving the how to the people doing the work. Intent should be simple enough to guide decisions when the situation changes.

Ask me two questions about the goal and the people who will receive this direction. Then rewrite my instructions as a short statement of intent covering what, why, the main effort and the boundaries, and list any instructions I should drop because they dictate the how.

[paste your current instructions or task description here]
```

### 3. Run a backbrief

Check that someone understood the direction before they act on it.

```text
In "The Art of Action," briefing and backbriefing work as a cascade: a leader briefs the intent and context, then the next level explains back what it understands and what it plans to do. The backbrief exposes misunderstandings and often reveals problems in the original direction.

Ask me what I briefed and to whom. Then compare their backbrief with my intent, point out where they misread it, where their plan drifts, and where their questions suggest my direction was unclear, and draft three questions I should ask them next.

[paste your brief and their backbrief here]
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my execution coach, grounded in Stephen Bungay's "The Art of Action." Core ideas: action meets friction in three gaps, of knowledge, alignment and effects; piling on information, instructions and controls makes the gaps worse; leaders should define intent (what and why), let each level work out how and backbrief, and give people freedom to adjust within the intent; the aim is directed opportunism, high alignment with high autonomy; strategy is a framework for decisions with a clear main effort, not a fixed plan.

When I bring you a plan or a problem with execution: identify which gap is at work, push back when I reach for more detail or control, and help me sharpen the intent. Keep a running note in this project of each initiative's intent, main effort, and what the backbriefs revealed.
```

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing it for *strategy+business*, David K. Hurst found the book's fictional management sessions its least satisfying parts, and said it is unclear how directed opportunism works in companies without the rigorous training the German General Staff and Toyota used to instill it. In the RAF's *Air Power Review*, Neville Parton noted that much of it is not hugely original, as Bungay himself admits. Our own caveats: the approach assumes capable, trusted people at every level, and armies share a single clear purpose in a way that companies with many stakeholders often do not.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead a team or a function and keep seeing good plans drift in execution, or if military history helps ideas stick for you. **Skip it** if you want a quick framework; the three gaps and the briefing and backbriefing cascade above carry most of the practical method.
