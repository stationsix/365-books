## At a glance

**Strategy is not a plan you finish and hand to specialists; it is a leader's ongoing job of deciding why the company matters, building a system that delivers that difference, and renewing it as the world moves.**

Cynthia A. Montgomery · 2012 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Cynthia Montgomery is the Timken Professor of Business Administration at Harvard Business School and a former head of its Strategy unit. She has taught strategy for more than three decades, at the University of Michigan and Northwestern's Kellogg School as well as Harvard. The book grew out of a five-year stint teaching in Harvard's Entrepreneur, Owner, President (EOP) program for company owners and heads.

Its starting point is a confession. Montgomery argues that from the early 1980s, as economists and competitive analysis took over the subject, strategy drifted away from leadership and became an exercise in getting the analysis right. Her own research followed that wave. What leaders had learned about strategy, she tells readers, was not incorrect but incomplete.

The book is short and built around classroom moments. Chapters move from the myth of the super-manager to purpose, turning purpose into reality, owning the strategy and keeping it vibrant, then close with the essential strategist and a set of frequently asked questions. Running through it is the question she poses in all her classes: are you a strategist?

## Key concepts

### 1. Strategy belongs to the leader · Transcends

Montgomery's central claim is that strategy and leadership must be reunited at the top. Too often, she argues, the job has been outsourced to consultants. Because contingencies can never be fully anticipated, any strategy leaders treat as a finished product will eventually fail. In the excerpt Harvard published, she puts it this way: strategy "needs continuous, not intermittent, leadership. It needs a strategist." In a *strategy+business* interview she called the old debate of brilliant strategy versus good execution vacuous: you cannot execute well without knowing your aim.

*Why it transcends:* Tools can draft analyses and options, but someone still has to own the choice and live with it. That job stays with the leader.

### 2. The myth of the super-manager · Transcends

The book's sharpest case is Masco, best known for its faucets. In 1986 it set out to become the Procter & Gamble of consumer durables and bought ten furniture makers, becoming the world's largest furniture company. By 1989 net income had fallen 30 percent, and the furniture business was eventually sold at a loss of $650 million. Other well-run companies tried furniture and left too. Her 2012 *McKinsey Quarterly* article describes an industry with high transport costs, low productivity and eroding prices, where more than 2,500 manufacturers competed in the mid-1980s. By at least two to one, executives in her classes found those odds energizing rather than daunting. Her lesson: confidence can balloon into overconfidence.

*Why it transcends:* Industry economics do not bend to good management, with or without AI.

### 3. Begin with purpose · Transcends

For Montgomery, purpose comes first: why the company exists, the unique value it brings, what sets it apart, and why and to whom that matters. Harvard's *Working Knowledge* listed the questions she wants leaders to keep asking: what does the organization bring to the world, does that difference matter, is any part of it scarce and hard to imitate, and is the company doing today what it needs to matter tomorrow. IKEA is the model, built on a better everyday life for the many. A good purpose puts a stake in the ground. The *McKinsey Quarterly* version of the test is whether customers would prefer a world with your company rather than without it.

*Why it transcends:* When AI makes many products and services easier to copy, the question of what difference you make, and to whom, becomes harder to dodge.

### 4. Turn purpose into reality · Evolves

A purpose, she writes, gives you the right to play, but at root it is only an idea. Strategy turns it into a system of resources and activities that work together and reinforce each other. She draws it as a strategy wheel, with purpose at the hub and supporting activities as spokes. Gucci is her turnaround case: after heavy licensing had cheapened the brand, CEO Domenico De Sole positioned it as fashion-forward, high-quality and good value, then doubled advertising, modernized stores, upgraded customer support and improved the supply chain, all coordinated behind that choice.

*Why it evolves:* AI can now handle some spokes, such as service or supply planning. Rivals can copy single parts faster, so the fit between them carries more of the advantage.

### 5. Own your strategy · Transcends

Owning a strategy means making the choices that define what the organization is and whom it serves, and accepting responsibility for them. These cannot be delegated. Reviewer Bob Morris summed up the theme: you cannot be everything to everyone. In the *strategy+business* interview she recalled one executive who kept asking presenters what they were doing that was really distinctive.

*Why it transcends:* An assistant can list ten plausible strategies in seconds. Choosing one and saying no to the rest is still a human commitment.

### 6. Keep it vibrant · Evolves

Most managers, Montgomery observes, breathe a sigh of relief once a strategy exercise is done, as if the work were over. Speaking to *Working Knowledge*, she called a long-run sustainable advantage, for most companies, "a dangerous mirage"; any advantage is one frame in a motion picture. Apple under Steve Jobs is her example of renewal. Paul Sweeney's *Financial Executive* review notes that she compares Jobs's makeover of Apple, through the iPod and iTunes, to the ship of Theseus, the puzzle of a thing whose parts are all replaced.

*Why it evolves:* The idea holds, but the frames move faster. Review cycles built for annual plans may be too slow for markets AI is reshaping.

### 7. The strategist's three roles · Evolves

In her *McKinsey Quarterly* article, Montgomery described three roles. The meaning maker makes the choices that define the company's identity and purpose. The voice of reason confronts industry forces directly instead of backing bold plans that ignore them. The operator connects market economics and the company's core ideas to action, because a company delivers on its promises only if its strategists think like operators. In the *strategy+business* interview she added that these skills can be developed later in life.

*Why it evolves:* AI can sharpen the voice of reason by testing assumptions against data quickly. It can also produce confident analysis that flatters a bold plan, so the leader's judgment matters more.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

This is a book about who does strategy, not a framework, which is why most of it holds. AI makes analysis cheap, and Montgomery's argument was already that analysis was never the scarce part. The scarce part is a leader willing to decide what the company stands for, test that against the economics of its industry, and keep revisiting the answer.

Four concepts transcend: ownership of strategy, the super-manager warning, purpose, and identity-defining choices.

Three evolve. A system of reinforcing activities still needs designing, but rivals can copy individual parts faster. Keeping a strategy vibrant now runs on a shorter clock. And the voice of reason gains a strong new tool that can also mislead: a fluent AI forecast can feed the same overconfidence her executives showed about furniture. The AI reading here is ours, not the author's.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Test your difference that matters

Check whether your purpose is specific enough to guide choices.

```text
In "The Strategist," Cynthia Montgomery argues that strategy begins with purpose: why the company exists, the unique value it brings, what sets it apart, and why and to whom that matters. She asks leaders four questions: what do we bring to the world, does that difference matter, is any part of it scarce and hard to imitate, and are we doing today what we need to do to matter tomorrow? A good test is whether customers would prefer a world with us rather than without us.

Ask me four questions about my customers, my competitors and what we do differently. Then answer Montgomery's four questions for my business with an honest rating for each, point out where my purpose is too vague to guide a choice, and draft a one-sentence purpose that names who we matter to and why.

[paste your current mission, purpose statement or business description here]
```

### 2. Run a super-manager check

Pressure-test a bold move against the economics of the market you are entering.

```text
In "The Strategist," Cynthia Montgomery uses Masco's move into furniture as a warning. A well-run company assumed its management skill would win in an industry with high transport costs, low productivity and eroding prices, and it left at a loss. Her lesson: confidence can balloon into overconfidence, and a leader must be the voice of reason who confronts industry forces directly instead of backing a bold plan that ignores them.

Ask me three questions about the market I want to enter or expand in and why I think we will win. Then describe the industry's economics plainly (rivals, prices, costs, growth, returns), list the assumptions in my plan that depend on us managing better than everyone else, and tell me what would have to be true for the move to work.

[paste your plan or the opportunity you are considering here]
```

### 3. Draw your strategy wheel

Check that your activities reinforce your purpose.

```text
In "The Strategist," Cynthia Montgomery says a purpose only becomes real through a system of resources and activities that work together and reinforce each other. She draws it as a wheel, with purpose at the hub and supporting activities as spokes. Gucci's turnaround coordinated advertising, stores, customer support and supply chain behind one purpose: fashion-forward, high quality, good value.

Ask me three questions about my purpose and how we operate today. Then draw my strategy wheel as a list: the hub, six to eight spokes, how each spoke supports the purpose, which spokes work against it, and which spoke a rival could copy most easily.

[paste a description of how your business operates here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Substantive criticism is thin. The reviews we found were favorable: Bob Morris wrote that it exceeds his expectations, Paul Sweeney in *Financial Executive* called it fascinating, and getAbstract rated it good, saying it suits beginners and experienced professionals alike. We found no named reviewer who took issue with its argument. Our own caveats: its main cases (IKEA, Gucci, Apple) are well-known winners, so readers cannot see how often a clear purpose failed to save a company. Its industry analysis builds on Porter rather than adding new tools. And purpose-first leadership is a crowded field.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run a business or a unit and treat strategy as an annual document someone else prepares, or want a short, humane case that your own judgment is the core of strategy. **Skip it** if you want analytical tools or a step-by-step method; Porter's *Competitive Strategy* or Rumelt's *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* serve better, and Montgomery's 2012 *McKinsey Quarterly* article "How strategists lead" covers her core argument in a few pages.
