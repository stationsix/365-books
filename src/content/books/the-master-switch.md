## At a glance

**Every American information industry of the twentieth century began open and ended closed, held by a monopoly or cartel, and the question is whether the internet will go the same way.**

Tim Wu · 2010 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Tim Wu is a Columbia Law School professor best known for coining the term "net neutrality." After the book he served as a senior adviser at the Federal Trade Commission, worked for the New York Attorney General and, in 2021, joined the White House National Economic Council as a special assistant for technology and competition policy.

The master switch of the title is the central control a corporate power gains over a new medium once the early chaos ends. Wu tells the history of the telephone, radio, film, television and cable as one recurring story: an invention opens a free-for-all of tinkerers and upstarts, then a single firm or cartel closes it, often with government help. Each story is built around a strong personality, from Theodore Vail to David Sarnoff. The final chapters turn to Apple, Google and a resurgent AT&T, ask whether the internet is next, and propose a remedy.

## Key concepts

### 1. The Cycle · Transcends

The book's organizing idea is what Wu calls the Cycle, "the oscillation of information industries between open and closed." A new technology starts in a golden age of openness and utopian hopes. Then a firm consolidates it, the open system becomes a closed one, and the closed system holds until a newer technology cracks it open again. Wu draws on Joseph Schumpeter's creative destruction and on Clayton Christensen's disruptive innovation, and stresses the cost of the long closed stretches, when better technologies wait.

*Why it transcends:* Any new technology, AI included, can be checked against one simple question: is this field getting more open or more closed, and who is closing it?

### 2. The Kronos effect · Transcends

Named for the Titan who ate his children to stop them overthrowing him, the Kronos effect is "the efforts undertaken by a dominant company to consume its potential successors in their infancy." Western Union first passed up an 1877 offer of Bell's telephone patents, then fought the telephone as a threat to the telegraph. The book's sharpest case is Sarnoff at RCA, who saw FM radio as a threat to AM, his NBC network included, and used government to hold it back for decades. Edwin Armstrong, the inventor of FM, is the tragic figure of that chapter.

*Why it transcends:* Buying, copying or regulating away a young rival is a standing temptation for any incumbent, and the pattern is easy to spot once it has a name.

### 3. The enlightened monopoly · Transcends

Theodore Vail, who built the Bell system with J.P. Morgan's capital, gets one of the book's fullest portraits. In Wu's telling Vail saw competition as wasteful and presented Bell's monopoly as a public duty, run in close cooperation with the state, with the promise of universal service. In the 1913 Kingsbury Commitment, Bell made modest concessions to the government that headed off harsher action, and Wu reads it as a step toward monopoly rather than away from it. Vail's bargain, public duty and close ties to the state in exchange for protected dominance, shaped American telephony until the 1980s breakup.

*Why it transcends:* A dominant firm offering to work closely with regulators, in return for rules newcomers struggle to meet, is a move worth watching for in any industry.

### 4. Structure sets the limits of speech · Evolves

Wu's larger claim is that in the United States industry structure determined the limits of free speech in each medium. His film chapters follow the independents who fled Edison's patent trust to Los Angeles, then built a centralized studio system of their own, which lived under an industry-run censorship regime, the Production Code.

*Why it evolves:* The few gatekeepers are now the firms that host, rank and generate content, and their choices about what an AI system will or will not say are a new version of the same question.

### 5. Open versus closed · Evolves

The book's later chapters recast the old fight as a choice between two models. Wu contrasts Steve Wozniak's commitment to openness in the Apple II with Steve Jobs's drive for a closed, tightly designed machine in the Macintosh, a line that runs on to the iPhone and its Apple-approved apps. Apple stands for curated convenience; Google, in 2010, for an open and messier web. Wu grants that closed devices offer a seamless experience; his concern is that curation should be the user's choice, not something imposed from a master switch.

*Why it evolves:* The choice is real but the line has moved: the open-versus-closed argument now runs through AI models themselves, where "open" can mean published weights, shared research or neither.

### 6. The internet's turn · Expires

Wu's question is whether the internet will be the exception. He traces AT&T's breakup and its return, reads the failure of the AOL and Time Warner merger as the internet's openness defeating an old-style empire, and names AT&T, Apple and Google as the contenders for the switch. In a Fortune response to a critic, he granted three reasons the internet might differ (its design, capital markets and changing attitudes to size) but held that network effects and scale still favor dominant firms.

*Why it expires:* The 2010 cast of contenders and the focus on who owns the network no longer match where power concentrated in the years since.

### 7. The Separations Principle · Evolves

Wu's remedy is a Separations Principle, which he presents as more a constitutional than a regulatory framework. Those who develop information, those who own the networks it travels on, and those who control the tools or venues of access should be kept apart, and government's proper role is as a check on private power, never as an aid to it. In a reply to a critic he noted that the internet was the first communications system to build a version of this separation into its design.

*Why it evolves:* The three layers still help, but AI blurs them: one firm can own the chips, the cloud, the model and the assistant you talk to.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Master Switch* was written in 2010 and says nothing about generative AI; this lens applies its Cycle to AI and does not report Wu's own view.

What holds is the pattern. Read with Wu's frame, AI looks like a technology still deciding which way to turn. There are many startups, published research and freely downloadable models, all signs of an open phase. There are also signs of closing: huge costs for computing power, a few firms supplying the chips and cloud capacity, and partnerships between model makers and established giants. The Kronos effect and Vail's bargain give useful names to moves worth watching, from acquisitions of young rivals to calls for licensing rules.

What changes is the map. Wu's master switch sat with whoever owned the wires or the device. In AI it may sit with whoever owns the model that answers your questions, which also makes the speech question sharper. His three-way separation is harder to apply when one firm can span every layer.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend, 3 evolve and 1 expires.

Judge whether your industry is opening or closing, and what that means for you.

```text

Ask me four questions about my industry: who the main players are, what it costs to enter, who controls the key infrastructure, and how that has changed in five years. ```

```

## Critiques and caveats

In *Fortune*, Scott Woolley argued that digital technology had broken the Cycle and that Wu forces history to fit his theory. Reviewing it in *The American Prospect*, the sociologist Paul Starr found no real causal account of why the Cycle happens, called the pattern specific to twentieth-century American information industries, and faulted the book for skipping the Defense Department's role in building the internet. In the *University of Chicago Law Review*, Peter Decherney, Nathan Ensmenger and Christopher Yoo called it a great-man theory of history and argued that bundling content can increase the quantity and variety of programming. Milton Mueller, in the journal *info*, found the Separations Principle sketched in about five pages.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you enjoy business history told through vivid characters, or work on technology policy and want the case histories behind today's antitrust debates. **Skip it** if you want a rigorous economic model of market power: the stories are strong, the theory is thin, and *Information Rules* covers the economics more tightly.
