## At a glance

**"AI" is a label stuck on very different technologies; generative AI is real progress with real flaws, while much of the AI that claims to predict people's futures does not work and likely never will.**

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor · 2024 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Arvind Narayanan is a professor of computer science at Princeton and director of its Center for Information Technology Policy. Sayash Kapoor, then a Princeton PhD candidate, had worked as a software engineer at Facebook, where he helped build AI for content moderation; he is now an assistant professor at the UC Berkeley School of Information.

The book grew out of Narayanan's 2019 talk "How to recognize AI snake oil," whose slides went viral, and Kapoor joined after taking Narayanan's course on the limits of prediction. Princeton University Press published it on September 24, 2024.

Its target is the word AI. The book's definition: "AI snake oil is AI that does not and cannot work as advertised." Across eight chapters it takes predictive AI, generative AI, existential risk, content moderation and the sources of hype in turn, then asks what to do next.

## Key concepts

### 1. One word for many technologies · Transcends

The introduction imagines a world with a single word, "vehicle," for bikes, buses, cars and spacecraft. People would argue past each other about whether vehicles are good for the environment. The authors argue that "artificial intelligence" causes the same confusion, so a claim about a chatbot gets read as a claim about a hiring tool. Their fix is vocabulary: separate generative AI, which produces text, images and sound, from predictive AI, which forecasts outcomes to guide decisions about people. Generative AI's progress, they write, is genuine and remarkable, though the product is still immature and unreliable.

*Why it transcends:* The first question about any AI claim is still which kind of AI it means, and the word has only grown baggier.

### 2. Predictive AI is an extraordinary claim · Transcends

The book argues that most snake oil sits in predictive AI, sold to governments and companies for decisions about bail, hiring, insurance and care. An 85-year-old Medicare patient was judged ready to leave in 17 days; when that time ran out she still could not push a walker unaided, and her payments stopped. Hiring tools claim to read warmth or openness from a 30-second video without published evidence. In Maryland, an Allstate pricing tool produced a list of customers whose rates would rise sharply, and seniors over 62 were drastically overrepresented on it. Pretrial risk tools, the authors write, are only slightly more accurate than random guessing.

*Why it transcends:* Vendors still make these claims, and demanding strong evidence first costs nothing.

### 3. A good prediction is not a good decision · Transcends

The book explains why even accurate predictions can lead to bad choices, the theme of chapter 2. One pneumonia model learned from its training data that asthma patients were at lower risk, a pattern that would have sent them home early. A kidney transplant system that estimated how long recipients would live could have discouraged patients from protecting their kidney function; a deliberative process with patients, doctors and other stakeholders led to it being abandoned. Opaque systems invite gaming, human overseers rubber-stamp the output, and a Cook County pretrial tool built on national data, *Publishers Weekly* notes, ignored the county's lower crime rate.

*Why it transcends:* Moving from forecasts to decisions about people is where AI projects fail, whatever the model underneath.

### 4. The limits to prediction · Transcends

Chapter 3 asks whether better data could fix this, and leans on the Fragile Families Challenge. In that Princeton-led study, 160 teams tried to predict six outcomes at age 15, such as a child's grades or a family's eviction, from a rich dataset on more than 4,000 families. Even the best models were not very accurate, and simple models often beat complex ones. The book treats the result as a sign that individual life outcomes may be partly unpredictable, not merely under-measured. It contrasts weather forecasting, which has improved, with the success of cultural products, which it treats as a lottery.

*Why it transcends:* The point does not depend on model size: much of a life turns on luck and choices no dataset records.

### 5. Automating bullshit · Evolves

The authors call themselves enthusiastic users of generative AI and think most knowledge industries can benefit from chatbots in some way. A section titled "Automating Bullshit" explains the catch. Chatbots learn to produce plausible text, not true text, so they can sound convincing on any subject, and the book argues they would make things up even if trained only on true statements. The chapter also covers deepfakes, artists' labor taken for training data, and image classifiers turned to surveillance.

*Why it evolves:* *Science News* warned at publication that some details may be out of date by the time readers get the book, and this chapter is the most exposed. The warning about fluent text with no source of truth still applies.

### 6. The ladder of generality · Evolves

Chapter 5 rejects a sharp line between narrow and general AI. The authors describe a ladder on which each rung makes systems more general; current technology is on the seventh rung, and no one knows how many rungs remain. They doubt a rogue superintelligence would overpower people, arguing that humans are powerful mainly because of their technology. Rather than banning powerful models, they favor defending against specific threats, such as better lab security against engineered viruses, and shoring up weak points like democracy, public health and financial systems.

*Why it evolves:* The authors returned to these arguments in their 2025 essay "AI as Normal Technology," and the existential risk debate remains open.

### 7. Why AI can't fix social media · Transcends

Kapoor helped build content moderation AI at Facebook, and chapter 6 lists seven shortcomings of AI for content moderation. They include missing context and cultural nuance, learning from past content while new speech keeps arriving, users who outwit filters, life-and-death stakes, regulatory pressure, and the hardest part, drawing lines, which is a value judgment rather than a technical task. A section titled "A Problem of Their Own Making" turns to the platforms; as one review summarizes it, recommendation algorithms actively amplify problematic content.

*Why it transcends:* No model settles what a platform should allow; that stays a human choice about values.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*AI Snake Oil* is about AI itself, so this lens checks its 2024 claims two years on.

The predictive AI warnings have held. In May 2025 a federal court in California let a lawsuit against Workday go forward, preliminarily certifying a nationwide collective action, on the claim that its AI screening tools disadvantage applicants over 40. Workday says the suit lacks merit and nothing is decided, but the book's hiring worries are now argued in court.

The authors pushed their argument further. Their essay "AI as Normal Technology," published by the Knight First Amendment Institute on April 15, 2025, treats AI as a transformative general-purpose technology like electricity, spreading over decades as institutions adapt. It calls superintelligence incoherent as usually conceived, treats catastrophic misalignment as the most speculative risk, expects harms more like those of past technologies, such as inequality, and opposes nonproliferation policies that would concentrate power. A paperback followed in September 2025; this summary did not review its additions.

What is most likely to date is the generative AI material. The toolkit for judging claims is not.

Verdict: 5 of 7 key concepts transcend and 2 evolve.

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Test a vendor's AI claim

Check whether a product you are being sold could be snake oil.

```text
In "AI Snake Oil," Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that "AI" covers unrelated technologies, so the first step is to ask which kind a product is. Generative AI makes content and is useful but unreliable. Predictive AI forecasts outcomes about people, such as job performance or reoffending, and often does not work. Predictive claims are extraordinary and need strong evidence.

Ask me four questions about the product, what it claims to predict or produce, and what evidence the vendor has shared. Then say which kind of AI it is, list the claims that need proof, name the evidence that would convince a skeptic, and rate the risk that it does not work as advertised.

[paste the product description or sales claims here]
```

### 2. Turn a prediction into a decision check

Find where an accurate forecast could still lead to a bad choice.

```text
"AI Snake Oil" warns that a good prediction is not a good decision. Models can learn misleading patterns, such as a pneumonia model that rated asthma patients as low risk and would have sent them home early. People game opaque systems, human reviewers rubber-stamp outputs, and tools built on one population fail on another.

Ask me three questions about the decision, who it affects and how the model's output is used. Then describe how the prediction could be right while the decision goes wrong, how people might game it, whether the training data matches the people it is used on, and what a meaningful human review and appeal would look like.

[paste a description of the model and decision here]
```

### 3. Read an AI headline skeptically

Sort hype from substance in a news story or announcement.

```text
"AI Snake Oil" argues that companies, researchers and journalists all feed AI hype: companies have few reasons to be transparent, AI research has a reproducibility crisis, and news outlets often echo industry claims. The book also says we should worry more about what people do with AI than about what AI does on its own.

Ask me what the story claims and who is making the claim. Then identify which kind of AI is involved, separate what was measured from what is predicted, list what is missing (independent tests, failure rates, who benefits), and rewrite the headline so it says only what the evidence supports.

[paste the article or announcement here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

*Publishers Weekly* found little new in the sections on facial recognition bias and the conditions of data annotators. In *The New Republic*, Edward Ongweso Jr. argued that the book neglects the military's role in AI's history, tells that history in depoliticized terms, and offers only vague calls for antitrust, labor organizing and some regulation. As Wikipedia summarizes them, Joshua Rothman in *The New Yorker* argued that AI's power to replicate complexity is already here and well funded, and Alexya Martinez in *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly* faulted its Western focus. Simon Boulter, in the *Journal of Ethics and Emerging Technologies*, faulted it for not investigating AI's role in discouraging critical thinking.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you buy, build or are judged by AI systems and want a clear test for which claims to believe, especially claims to predict people. **Skip it** if you want hands-on help using chatbots: *Co-Intelligence* is the practical, enthusiastic counterpart, and *Weapons of Math Destruction* covers the older scoring models in more depth.
