## At a glance

**Groups that welcome crazy ideas turn on them as they grow, the way water freezes, and the fix is structure, not culture: separate inventors from operators, keep them talking, and reward the project over the promotion.**

Safi Bahcall · 2019 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Safi Bahcall took a physics degree at Harvard and a PhD at Stanford, then co-founded Synta Pharmaceuticals in 2001 and ran it for 13 years. In 2011 he worked with President Obama's science advisory council on the future of American research. The book opens with Alex, a 33-year-old cancer patient, the first to receive his company's new drug.

Why do good teams kill great ideas? Bahcall's answer borrows from physics: teams, companies and even nations can flip suddenly from nurturing new ideas to rejecting them, the way a small drop in temperature turns water to ice. Culture gets the blame, he argues, but small changes in structure control the transition.

Part one tells five lives, among them Vannevar Bush, Akira Endo, Juan Trippe and Edwin Land, to show that loonshots and franchises are different phases of a group. Part two supplies the science, including an equation for a magic number near 150. A final chapter applies the ideas to nations; an afterword sets loonshots against disruption.

## Key concepts

### 1. Loonshots and franchises · Transcends

The most important breakthroughs, Bahcall writes, come from loonshots, "widely dismissed ideas whose champions are often written off as crazy." Unlike a moonshot, an ambitious goal everyone applauds, a loonshot is ridiculed. Franchises are the proven lines whose certainties keep the high failure rates of loonshots from bankrupting a company. Every organization needs both, and they demand opposite things: tolerance for repeated failure on one side, discipline and reliable execution on the other. As groups grow, the franchise side tends to win.

*Why it transcends:* The tension between new bets and proven earners is about incentives and attention, and no tool removes it.

### 2. Separate the phases · Transcends

The first Bush-Vail rule is to keep the people who invent apart from the people who execute; Bahcall calls them artists and soldiers. Vannevar Bush, head of the Carnegie Institution and a former MIT engineering dean, met Franklin Roosevelt in June 1940 and won approval for the National Defense Research Committee. A year later the new Office of Scientific Research and Development absorbed it, with Bush answering directly to the president. Bush, in Bahcall's account, did not try to change military culture; he built fragile ideas a separate home. The second name on the rules belongs to Theodore Vail, who returned to lead AT&T in 1907 and backed the research effort that became Bell Labs.

*Why it transcends:* Exploratory work still needs protection from the metrics that run the core, whatever the technology.

### 3. Dynamic equilibrium and the gardener · Transcends

Separation alone fails. The second rule asks for constant two-way exchange between lab and field, because the greatest failure point is the handoff, not the idea. Bahcall's wartime example: when pilots could not work the complicated radar controls under combat pressure, scientists built a simpler display. The leader's job is to be a gardener, not a Moses: to tend both sides, manage the transfer and step in only where it breaks down, rather than choose winning ideas personally.

*Why it transcends:* The handoff between builders and operators is still where good projects die.

### 4. The Three Deaths and the False Fail · Evolves

Big ideas, Bahcall argues, usually get killed several times before they succeed, the three deaths of the loonshot. Some deaths are False Fails: a result that looks like failure but reflects, as he put it to Tim Ferriss in 2019, "a flaw in the experiment, rather than the idea." The chapter's case is Akira Endo at Sankyo. After screening about 3,800 fungal strains, Endo isolated compactin, the first statin, in 1973. It did nothing to cholesterol in rats, yet in 1976 it cut cholesterol by about half in laying hens. Sankyo dropped it in 1980; Merck's lovastatin reached the market in 1987. The remedy is to ask, with curiosity, why a test failed.

*Why it evolves:* AI can rerun analyses and spot flawed setups faster, but it can also produce confident negatives at scale, so questioning a failed test matters more.

### 5. P-type and S-type loonshots · Evolves

P-type loonshots are products or technologies everyone says will not work. S-type loonshots are small changes in strategy or business model that everyone says are crazy or will not work. The book's pairing is Juan Trippe and Bob Crandall. Trippe's Pan Am led the jet age with launch orders in 1955 and later the Boeing 747, then folded in 1991. Crandall's American Airlines won with strategy: the Sabre reservation system and yield management. A group good at one type can be blind to the other.

*Why it evolves:* AI tools are an obvious P-type story, but much of the value may come from S-type moves in pricing, workflow and business model that look dull from outside.

### 6. The Moses trap and the system mindset · Transcends

The Moses trap is the myth of the great leader who anoints a chosen project: a founder with a run of hits starts deciding alone which ideas advance. Edwin Land is the book's case: Polaroid's instant camera (1948) and SX-70 (1972) were triumphs, but his Polavision home movie system, launched in 1977 as videotape arrived, flopped, and Polaroid wrote off $89 million. Land left by 1981; Polaroid went bankrupt in 2001. The escape is a system mindset, which Bahcall takes from Garry Kasparov: after a loss, ask not only what went wrong but how the decision was made.

*Why it transcends:* Reviewing how decisions get made, not just which ones failed, is a habit no tool supplies.

### 7. Stake, rank and the magic number · Evolves

Why do groups freeze? Two forces compete: each member's stake in the outcome and the perks of rank. In a team of three, each person owns a third of the result; in a firm of 100, perhaps 1 percent, while a promotion might raise pay by 30 percent. Once rank outweighs stake, politics beats projects. Bahcall's equation for that tipping size uses four parameters: equity fraction (pay tied to outcomes), management span, the fit between people's skills and projects, and salary growth with promotion. Typical values put the magic number near 150. Wider spans and outcome-linked rewards raise it; steep promotion raises lower it.

*Why it evolves:* If AI lets small teams do more, more work can stay below the threshold, though the formula itself is barely tested.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

When Bahcall discussed AI in a 2020 interview with Barry Ritholtz, his concern was national security: rival states weaponizing machine learning. Nothing we read applies the Bush-Vail rules to AI tools inside companies, so what follows is our reading, not his view.

The book maps cleanly onto how most firms are handling AI. The pilot team is the loonshot group, the core business is the franchise, and the usual failure is at the transfer, when a prototype meets a division measured on this quarter. Separating the phases while keeping them in contact matches the separate-then-integrate design in *Lead and Disrupt*, reached here through structure and incentives rather than leadership.

Two ideas gain weight. False Fails multiply when experiments are cheap, since a badly designed test can kill an idea in an afternoon. And S-type loonshots may matter more than the headline technology.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve.

### 2. Separate the phases without cutting the line

Design a home for a new project that protects it and still connects it to the core.

```text
"Loonshots" says organizations need two phases: loonshot groups (artists) working on fragile, often-dismissed ideas and franchise groups (soldiers) running proven products. Rule one: keep them separate so the franchise side does not crush new ideas. Rule two: keep them in constant two-way exchange, because the handoff is where most breakthroughs die. Leaders should act as gardeners managing that transfer, not as a Moses choosing winners.

Ask me five questions about the new project, the core business and who sponsors it. Then propose how to separate the project (people, budget, metrics), how ideas and feedback will flow both ways, who owns the handoff, and the earliest warning signs that the core is starving or smothering it.

[paste a description of the project and your organization here]
```

### 3. Find your magic number

See whether your team's incentives reward projects or politics.

```text
"Loonshots" argues that groups flip from backing projects to playing politics once the perks of rank outweigh each person's stake in the outcome. Bahcall's equation for the size where this happens uses four parameters: equity fraction (pay tied to outcomes), management span (direct reports per manager), fit between people's skills and projects, and salary growth with promotion. Wider spans, outcome-linked rewards and better fit raise the threshold; big promotion raises lower it. Typical values put it near 150 people.

Ask me five questions about our team size, layers, pay and promotion, and how people are matched to projects. Then estimate whether stake or rank dominates for a typical member, name the parameter doing the most damage, and suggest three changes we could make within six months.

[paste a description of your team or company here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Reviews were split: the *Financial Times* found it ambitious and entertaining, *Publishers Weekly* less than game-changing. In the *Wall Street Journal*, David Shaywitz noted that most innovative ideas do not work out. The phase-transition model is an analogy from physics, and its equation is illustrative rather than tested; a 3 Quarks Daily review called the 150 result mostly inspired numerology and argued that the chapter on Europe overlooks factors such as religion. The cases were chosen with hindsight.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run or fund new projects in a growing organization and want vivid history plus a vocabulary for why good ideas stall. **Skip it** if you want a researched design for running new ventures beside a core business: *Lead and Disrupt* covers that ground with more evidence and fewer anecdotes.
