## At a glance

**A business that solves a problem nobody has solved before ends up inventing a set of interlocking solutions, and that whole stack, not any single idea, is what lets a small company survive a giant that copies it.**

Jim McKelvey · 2020 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Jim McKelvey is a glass artist, inventor and serial entrepreneur who has started at least seven companies. In 2009 he lost a sale of his glass work because he could not take an American Express card. He called his friend Jack Dorsey, and together they founded Square, the payments company that let small sellers take cards on a phone. When the book came out in 2020 he still sat on Square's board, had founded Invisibly to rework the economics of online content, had started the nonprofit LaunchCode to teach people to program, and was deputy chair of the board of the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis.

The book is built around one puzzle. In 2014 Amazon launched a card reader that copied Square's product and undercut its price, and Square survived while Amazon retreated. McKelvey studied why. Told in the first person, the book mixes the Square story with histories of the Bank of Italy, IKEA and Southwest Airlines.

## Key concepts

### 1. Copy what works, until you can't · Transcends

McKelvey argues that most businesses are built by copying, and that this is sensible. Shake Shack took its burgers and fries from Steak 'n Shake and its frozen custard from a St. Louis stand, Ted Drewes. He pictures established markets as medieval Edinburgh inside its stone wall: confining, but safe, because everything inside has been tried. He put the rule plainly in a 2021 interview: "Copy when you can; invent when you must." Inventing is riskier, and worth it only when there is nothing to copy.

*Why it transcends:* AI makes copying faster than ever, which makes the line between copying a proven model and inventing a new one even more worth drawing.

### 2. True entrepreneurs, and perfect problems · Transcends

The word "entrepreneur" now covers almost anyone who starts a business. McKelvey wants it back in the older sense the economist Joseph Schumpeter popularized: someone doing something truly new. He draws the line with the Wright brothers: running a bicycle shop made them businesspeople, and building an airplane made them entrepreneurs. Their starting point is a **perfect problem**: one that can be solved, but only with a solution that does not exist yet. In a later interview he added that what makes a problem perfect is the person who cares enough to take it on.

*Why it transcends:* The definition rests on novelty, not on any tool. Whatever the technology, someone still has to choose an unsolved problem and stick with it.

### 3. The Innovation Stack · Evolves

Solving a new problem creates new problems, and each one needs its own invention. The result is a stack: a set of interlocking solutions, each depending on the others. McKelvey says stacks are not designed in advance; they evolve as a business fights to survive. The interlocking is the defense: a rival can copy one element, but one piece of a system that only works as a whole gets it very little.

*Why it evolves:* AI lowers the cost of building any single element, so stacks made of features erode faster. Stacks that include operations, culture and customer relationships still resist copying.

### 4. Square's stack and the Amazon attack · Evolves

The book describes 14 elements in Square's stack, among them simple pricing, free sign-up, cheap hardware, no long-term contracts and, at first, no live support. Amazon's Local Register, launched in August 2014, offered an introductory rate of 1.75 percent against Square's 2.75 percent. Square did not match the price; McKelvey has said Square could never have become profitable at Amazon's rate. Amazon announced in October 2015 that it was closing the service, ended it on February 1, 2016, and offered its users free Square readers.

*Why it evolves:* The specific elements belong to 2010s card processing and have since been widely copied. The lesson that a system beats a feature holds up better than the list.

### 5. Look below the bottom of the market · Transcends

McKelvey argues that the opportunity often lies below the bottom of an existing market, and draws pyramids to show it. Square went after small sellers whom card processors overlooked or charged far more than large retailers. The Bank of Italy, founded by A.P. Giannini in San Francisco in 1904 and later renamed Bank of America, focused on "the little fellow," the small depositor and borrower other banks ignored. His summary: if you want to be big, go low.

*Why it transcends:* Every cost collapse opens up a group of customers who were too small to serve before. Finding them is the same move in any era.

### 6. Stacks from history · Transcends

Southwest Airlines built its stack on one idea: planes make money in the air, not at the gate. Getting more flying out of each plane required a ten-minute turnaround, a standardized fleet, batch boarding, open seating, a single class and outlying airports, each supporting the others. The Bank of Italy stacked 16 ideas, among them banking for women, low rates and low minimums. McKelvey argues that a company with a full stack can largely ignore a rival's attack, as Southwest ignored competitors' pricing for decades, instead of fighting on the attacker's terms.

*Why it transcends:* The examples span banking, furniture and airlines, so the pattern is about how systems defend themselves, not any one industry.

### 7. No experts in the unknown · Evolves

Nobody is an expert in a problem that has never been solved. Expertise matters for copying, McKelvey argues, but not for inventing, and the first person to do anything is always unqualified. Square's founders were newcomers to payments. What the people in his stories share is stubbornness and perseverance, and the book promises no guarantees.

*Why it evolves:* AI gives newcomers instant access to existing expertise, which helps with the copied parts of a business, but it also carries the industry's assumptions.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Innovation Stack* asks a question AI makes sharper: what can a rival not copy? When software can reproduce a feature in days, an advantage built on one clever idea is fragile. McKelvey's answer, a system of interlocking choices that only work together, is close to what strategists call fit, and it holds up well.

Four concepts transcend: the copy or invent rule, the narrow definition of entrepreneurship, looking below the market, and the pattern of stacks surviving attack. Three evolve. Stacks built mostly of features are easier to copy now, Square's own list belongs to its moment, and freedom from expertise is harder to keep when every assistant has absorbed the industry's conventional wisdom.

The stacks that last will lean on what is slow to copy: operations, trust and culture.

The AI reading here is ours, not the book's.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

### 2. Map your stack

See whether your advantages interlock or stand alone.

```text
"The Innovation Stack" says that solving a new problem creates further problems, each needing its own invention, and that the resulting set of interlocking solutions is hard to copy because each piece depends on the others. Square's stack included simple pricing, free sign-up, cheap hardware, no contracts and no live support; Southwest's included ten-minute turnarounds, a standardized fleet, open seating and a single class, all serving one idea: planes earn money in the air.

Ask me three questions about how my business operates and what customers value. Then list my stack elements, draw the dependencies between them, mark which elements a rival could copy alone, and say which element would hurt most if it were removed.

[paste a description of how your business works here]
```

### 3. Plan for a giant copying you

Test how your business would hold up if a much larger rival copied your product and cut the price.

```text
In "The Innovation Stack," Jim McKelvey describes how Amazon copied Square's card reader in 2014 at a lower rate, and Square survived by refusing to match the price and sticking to its whole stack. He argues that a company with a full stack, like Square or Southwest Airlines, can largely ignore an attacker and keep doing what makes it different.

Ask me three questions about my product, my pricing and my likely competitors. Then describe the most plausible copycat attack, which parts of my business it could match, which it could not, and what I should do and not do if it happened.

[paste a description of your product and competitors here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Shortform's summary notes counterarguments: many successful businesses improve on existing solutions rather than solve new problems, and a complex stack can create problems of its own. The UXmatters review, though positive, says the book gives direction but not a map. Our own caveats: the core evidence is four companies, one of them the author's own, all chosen because they survived, and the book does not look at companies that built stacks and failed anyway. The Amazon story is told from Square's side, and Amazon's decision to quit may have had reasons that had little to do with Square's stack.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you are starting something genuinely new or fear a much larger rival, and want a lively account from someone who did it. **Skip it** if you want a rigorous framework for competitive advantage; *7 Powers* covers defensibility with more structure, and *Zero to One* makes a similar case for doing something new.
