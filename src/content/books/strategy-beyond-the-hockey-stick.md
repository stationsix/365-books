## At a glance

**Most strategies fail because the people in the room plan from the inside, so judge a plan by the outside odds and back it with a few big moves.**

Chris Bradley, Martin Hirt and Sven Smit · 2018 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

All three authors were McKinsey partners when the book came out. Chris Bradley, in Sydney, led the firm's Business Strategy Service Line. Martin Hirt, a senior partner in Greater China, led its Global Strategy and Corporate Finance Practice. Sven Smit, a senior partner in Amsterdam, led McKinsey in Western Europe and co-wrote *The Granularity of Growth*.

The book argues that strategy rarely fails for lack of frameworks. It fails because of the "social side of strategy": politics, incentives and biases in the strategy room. The cure is an outside view built from data on thousands of large companies, which shows the real odds of a plan and the few levers that change them. Bradley described the approach as more big data and empiricism, less theorizing.

The book moves from the games played in the strategy room, through the odds and the levers, to big moves and eight shifts in how leaders run strategy. Cartoons by Jeremy Banks and Mike Shapiro keep the tone light.

## Key concepts

### 1. The social side of strategy and the hockey stick · Transcends

In the strategy room, smart people make predictable mistakes. Following Daniel Kahneman, they describe the "inside view": teams extrapolate from their own experience and fall prey to overconfidence, anchoring, loss aversion and politics. Presenters define their markets to flatter their units and sandbag to avoid risk. The emblem is the hockey-stick projection: flat results now, a confident upturn just ahead. One company drew a fresh upturn every year from 2011 to 2015 while actual results stayed roughly flat, then trailed off.

*Why it transcends:* People and incentives have not changed. A tool that makes forecasts faster and prettier can make a hockey stick more convincing, not more likely.

### 2. The power curve and the 1-in-12 odds · Evolves

The outside view starts with economic profit, profit after the cost of capital, for 2,393 of the world's largest companies from 2010 to 2014. It follows a power curve. The bottom fifth suffers deep losses, the middle three fifths earn little, and the top fifth captures nearly 90 percent of the economic profit, about 30 times the middle's average. The curve is getting steeper. Over ten years only about 8 percent of middle companies reach the top fifth, just 1 in 12. A strategy should be judged against that base rate.

*Why it evolves:* Base rates are more useful than ever, and easier to build. But this curve is a snapshot of large companies before the current AI wave, so readers should refresh the numbers for their own industry.

### 3. Ten levers: endowment, trends and moves · Evolves

The authors examined 40 levers and kept ten that they say explain more than 80 percent of the up-drift and down-drift in corporate performance. Endowment, what a company starts with, is revenue size, debt level and past R&D spending, and accounts for about 30 percent of the odds. Trends, the industry trend and exposure to growth geographies, add about 25 percent. Moves, what management actually does, explain almost half. The odds come from combined influences, not simple addition.

*Why it evolves:* The split separates luck from choice. Which variables matter, and how much, may shift as AI changes what scale and R&D buy.

### 4. Ride the trend, or move to a better one · Transcends

Industry drives about half of a company's position on the curve. As the authors put it in a *McKinsey Quarterly* article adapted from the book, "it's better to be an average company in a great industry than a great company in an average industry." Nine of the 12 tobacco companies in the data sit in the top fifth; none of the 20 paper companies does. Hirt calls the industry trend the single most important of the ten. A strong tailwind lifts a middle company's odds of reaching the top to 24 percent; a strong headwind cuts them to 4 percent.

*Why it transcends:* Where you compete still matters more than how hard you try. AI is now one of the biggest tailwinds or headwinds a company can face, which makes this lever more pressing, not less.

### 5. Five big moves, measured against rivals · Evolves

The moves that matter are programmatic M&A (a steady stream of deals adding at least 30 percent of market value over ten years), dynamic reallocation (shifting at least half of capital spending among units over a decade), strong capital spending (top fifth of the industry on capex to sales), productivity (top 30 percent of the industry) and differentiation (top 30 percent on gross margin). What counts is how a company stacks up against competitors; keeping pace with the industry gives no lift. One or two big moves raise a middle company's odds from 8 to 17 percent, and three raise them to 47 percent. Heavy capex is the exception that also raises the risk of falling. The article concludes: "In general, making no bold moves is probably the most dangerous strategy of all."

*Why it evolves:* The logic of relative, persistent moves holds. The thresholds were set on pre-AI data, and spending on data centers or models may not behave like the capex in the study.

### 6. Eight shifts to unlock strategy · Transcends

The book closes with eight shifts in how leadership teams run strategy. Replace the annual plan with a strategy journey. Debate real alternatives instead of approving one plan. Stop spreading resources thinly, the "peanut butter" habit, and fund the one-in-ten opportunities that can break out. Make big moves before approving budgets, starting from a momentum case of where the business is heading on its current path. Keep part of the budget open to reallocation. Hold separate conversations on improvement, growth and risk. Judge people on how results were pursued, not just the numbers. And force the first step by breaking big moves into near-term goals.

*Why it transcends:* These habits target human behavior, whatever tools supply the numbers.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book replaces stories with base rates, and that ages well. An AI assistant can now gather peer data or test a plan against a reference class in an afternoon. The outside view has become cheap.

The social side of strategy is untouched. A team can ask an AI to build a polished hockey stick as easily as an honest momentum case, and a confident chart is still a chart. The book's discipline of naming the odds before debating the plan matters more when slides cost nothing.

Three concepts evolve: the power curve, the ten levers and the big-move thresholds were all fitted to large-company data from before 2015. The method holds; the numbers need refreshing. Three transcend: the social side of strategy, the weight of industry trends, and the eight shifts.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

Independent critical reviews are scarce. We found none in major trade or academic outlets, and much of the coverage comes from McKinsey or author interviews. Stuart Reynolds, reviewing it in 2020 for the Australian advisory firm Fullstack, liked the book but warned the research may not apply directly to startups, especially the size factor. Johannes Etzelmüller of the strategy software firm Workpath called the relatively short time frame of the analysis a possible limiting factor. Goodreads readers said it is written for very large companies, offers little actionable advice, and could have been a McKinsey Quarterly article. Our own caveats: the levers describe patterns in data, not tested causes; a sample of the largest companies leaves out firms that never got there; and the authors' firm sells this kind of strategy work.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run or advise strategy at a large company, sit through hockey-stick planning sessions, or want the data and the eight shifts in full. **Skip it** if you run a small business or startup; the McKinsey Quarterly articles "Strategy to beat the odds" and "Eight shifts that will take your strategy into high gear," both adapted from the book, cover the core in two short reads.
