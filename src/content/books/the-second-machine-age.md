## At a glance

**Digital technology is now doing for mental work what the steam engine did for muscle, and it will make the economy far richer while handing most of the gains to a few unless people and policy adapt.**

Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee · 2014 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

When they wrote the book, Brynjolfsson was a professor at MIT Sloan and McAfee a principal research scientist at MIT's Center for Digital Business; together they co-directed MIT's Initiative on the Digital Economy. Their 2011 book was *Race Against the Machine*.

The book opens by asking which developments in human history mattered most. Using the historian Ian Morris's index of human development, the authors show the curve bending sharply in the late 1700s, when James Watt's improved steam engine began the first machine age, an era of machines that multiplied physical power. The second machine age does the same for mental power. Its signs included a 2012 ride in a Google driverless car that handled traffic on Highway 101, eight years after no entrant finished the 2004 DARPA race, and IBM's Watson beating two champions on *Jeopardy!* in 2011.

Chapters 1 to 6 explain why the technology is advancing so fast, chapters 7 to 11 weigh its economic effects under two headings, bounty and spread, and chapters 12 to 15 offer advice for individuals and policy.

## Key concepts

### 1. The second half of the chessboard · Transcends

The authors retell the legend of the inventor of chess, who asked his emperor for one grain of rice on the first square, two on the second, and double on every square after. By the 32nd square the pile reaches about 4 billion grains; by the 64th it would dwarf Mount Everest. Following the inventor Ray Kurzweil, they argue that exponential growth starts to feel strange only after about 32 doublings, and that Moore's law has carried computing that far. "One of the things that sets the second machine age apart is how quickly that second half of the chessboard can arrive."

*Why it transcends:* Doubling still beats intuition, and the habit of asking where a technology sits on its curve is as useful for AI capability as it was for chips.

### 2. Digital and recombinant · Transcends

Alongside exponential growth, the authors name two more forces: digitization and combination. Digital information can be copied and sent at close to zero cost, so a good idea can reach millions at almost no extra expense. Chapter 5, "Innovation: Declining or Recombining?", answers economists who feared the great inventions were behind us. The authors see innovation as mostly the recombining of existing ideas, and every new digital building block multiplies the possible combinations rather than using them up.

*Why it transcends:* Today's AI systems are built from cheap digital copies of text, code and images, and recombining them is close to what they do.

### 3. Bounty and spread · Transcends

The book's economic chapters run on two words. Bounty is the growing abundance technology delivers, much of it free online and so missed by GDP, which the authors find inadequate as a measure ("Beyond GDP" is a chapter title). Spread is the widening gap in income and wealth. They trace it to three shifts: technology that favors skilled workers, a move of income from labor to capital, and superstar effects. In a 2014 *McKinsey Quarterly* interview, Brynjolfsson called the split between rising productivity and stagnant median income and employment the Great Decoupling.

*Why it transcends:* Every debate about AI and work still splits into these two questions: how much bigger the pie gets, and who gets which slices.

### 4. Stars and superstars · Transcends

Chapter 10, "The Biggest Winners: Stars and Superstars," argues that digital markets reward the best product far more than the second best. When copies cost almost nothing, a slightly better app can take most of a market. In a 2014 *Foreign Affairs* article with the economist Michael Spence, the two authors described the result as a power-law economy: a few winners take most of the rewards, a long tail shares the rest, and most people earn less than the average. That article also argued that people with ideas, more than workers or investors, would become the scarcest input.

*Why it transcends:* AI tools let one person or a small team serve a huge market, which strengthens the superstar effect rather than softening it.

### 5. Racing with machines · Evolves

Chapter 12 turns to individuals with a title that reverses the earlier book: "Learning to Race with Machines." Its emblem is freestyle chess, where human and computer teams compete. "The key insight from freestyle chess is that people and computers don't approach the same task the same way." The advice follows: build skills that complement machines, and pair human strengths with computer speed. In a 2014 PBS NewsHour interview, Brynjolfsson said public education had set up a race between technology and education that America won for over a century.

*Why it evolves:* Teaming with machines is now an everyday habit, but the human role in the team keeps shrinking as the machine's share of the work grows.

### 6. Where humans keep the edge · Expires

The authors see ideation, large-frame pattern recognition and the most complex forms of communication as areas where people keep the advantage for some time to come. In 2004 Frank Levy and Richard Murnane had named the last two as human ground; the authors note machines were already making inroads.

*Why it expires:* Current AI assistants converse, translate, draft ideas and summarize at length, so the list no longer marks a safe boundary, even if judgment and leadership still do.

### 7. Econ 101 now, bigger ideas later · Evolves

The policy chapters start with what the authors call Econ 101 measures: better schools, support for startups and other standard steps toward growth. For the longer term they consider a basic income or a negative income tax and expansions of the earned income tax credit, along with contests and other incentives for technology that augments labor and a national mutual fund that would pay dividends to everyone.

*Why it evolves:* The menu is still the one policymakers reach for, but faster AI moves the long-term ideas closer to the short term.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Second Machine Age* is a 2014 forecast that thinking machines were about to arrive at scale. On direction, it was right. The chessboard, digitization and recombination help explain how fast general AI assistants arrived, and bounty and spread still sort the arguments about them.

What changed is where the line between people and machines falls. The book's human edge rested on communication and ideation, the very tasks language models now handle. Racing with machines still holds as advice, but each lap the machine runs more of the course.

In a 2022 *Dædalus* essay, "The Turing Trap," written after he moved to Stanford, Brynjolfsson warned that AI built to imitate people tends to replace them and weaken workers' bargaining power, and argued for AI that augments them. A 2023 NBER working paper he wrote with Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond found that a generative AI assistant raised support agents' issues resolved per hour by 14 percent on average and 34 percent for novice and low-skilled workers. Inside one job, at least, that cuts against the book's worry that technology mostly rewards the already skilled.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend, 2 evolve and 1 expires.

## Critiques and caveats

In *Issues in Science and Technology*, Robert D. Atkinson of the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation doubted that Moore's law would keep holding, argued that innovation was no faster than in the late 1800s, said near-free digital goods were probably under 5 percent of the economy, and estimated that only about 20 percent of US jobs could be easily automated within a decade or two. In *The New Republic*, the sociologist Paul Starr noted that productivity gains had trailed off after 2004, so the case rested on gains still to come, and called the policy chapters a menu of options rather than a program. In *Prospect*, John McDermott found the argument built on anecdote, theory and extrapolation and called the proposals tiny and naive for the change predicted.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want the clearest popular account of why digital progress compounds and how its gains get shared. **Skip it** if you want practical AI tactics: the authors' follow-up, *Machine, Platform, Crowd*, covers more business ground, and *Co-Intelligence* is closer to how people work with AI today.
