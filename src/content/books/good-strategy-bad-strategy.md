## At a glance

**Most of what organizations call strategy is not strategy at all, and the real thing has a simple, demanding core: diagnose the challenge, choose a guiding policy, and take coherent action.**

Richard Rumelt · 2011 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Rumelt, a professor emeritus at UCLA's Anderson School who has advised companies and governments for decades, wrote this book because he kept running into the same problem. Leaders produce documents full of ambition, values and targets, and call them strategy. None of it says how the organization will overcome the obstacle actually in its way.

His argument is blunt. A good strategy is a coherent response to a specific challenge. It is not a vision statement, a financial goal or a list of things everyone already wanted to do. Good strategy is rare because it requires choosing, and choosing means saying no to people, projects and ideas. Bad strategy is common because it avoids that pain.

The book splits into three parts: how to recognize bad strategy, where good strategy gets its power, and how to think like a strategist. Rumelt teaches through stories, from business turnarounds to military campaigns, and he is consistently impatient with fluff.

## Key concepts

### 1. The four hallmarks of bad strategy · Transcends

Rumelt names four warning signs. **Fluff** dresses up the obvious in jargon. **Failure to face the challenge** means the document never names the problem, so nobody can judge whether the plan addresses it. **Mistaking goals for strategy** treats "grow 20%" as a plan. **Bad strategic objectives** list everything leaders want, or set targets as hard as the original problem.

*Why it transcends:* AI makes fluff nearly free to produce. A model can generate a polished strategy deck in seconds, which makes Rumelt's test more valuable, not less.

### 2. The kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action · Transcends

Every good strategy has a three-part kernel. The **diagnosis** explains what is going on and names the critical challenge. The **guiding policy** is the overall approach chosen to deal with it, ruling some options in and others out. **Coherent actions** are coordinated steps that carry out the policy and reinforce each other.

The book opens with a classic example. When Steve Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, the company was losing money and had a sprawling product line. His diagnosis was that Apple had too many products and no clear position. His guiding policy was radical focus, and his coherent actions cut the line down to a handful of products.

*Why it transcends:* AI can speed up diagnosis by pulling in more data, faster. Deciding what the data means and what to give up is still the strategist's job.

### 3. Leverage · Evolves

Power comes from concentrating effort where it produces an outsized effect. Rumelt calls these pivot points: anticipating where things are heading, finding the spot where a small push moves a lot, and putting resources there instead of spreading them thin.

*Why it evolves:* The principle holds, but the pivot points move. In AI-driven markets they often sit in data, distribution and workflow position rather than in product features alone.

### 4. Proximate objectives · Evolves

A good leader sets objectives close enough to achieve, which turns a fuzzy challenge into a problem the organization can actually solve. A proximate objective absorbs ambiguity so the team can act.

*Why it evolves:* Faster build-and-test cycles let organizations set smaller, quicker objectives and adjust weekly instead of yearly. The idea is the same, but the clock runs much faster.

### 5. Chain-link systems · Transcends

Some systems are only as strong as their weakest link. Improving one link does nothing if another is holding everything back, and that is why stuck organizations stay stuck. Breaking out usually means improving several links at once, under strong leadership.

*Why it transcends:* This is one of the most useful ideas for anyone automating work. Speeding up one step with AI produces nothing if the approval, the data quality or the handoff downstream stays broken.

### 6. Riding the waves of change · Transcends

Rumelt urges strategists to watch for big shifts (in technology, costs, regulation or buyer behavior) and to think through second-order effects before rivals do. The biggest opportunities appear when a wave changes the rules faster than incumbents can adjust.

*Why it transcends:* Generative AI is exactly the kind of wave he describes. The book gives a way to ask who gains and who loses, rather than just "how do we use AI?"

### 7. Inertia and entropy · Transcends

Organizations resist change, and left alone they drift toward disorder. Rumelt treats both as strategic facts. Your own inertia is a risk, and a rival's inertia can be your opportunity.

*Why it transcends:* Incumbents slow to adopt new tools face the same dynamic Rumelt described. Entropy now includes sprawling, uncoordinated AI pilots that nobody owns.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

This book has aged into something close to a field manual for the AI era. Its central warning, that most strategy is fluff, matters more when fluff costs nothing to generate. Its central tool, the kernel, fits any team asking what to do about AI. Diagnose the challenge AI creates for you, choose a guiding policy (lead, follow or deliberately abstain), and line up coherent actions behind it.

The weaker spots are about speed. Rumelt's examples move at the pace of quarters and years, and AI compresses that. Proximate objectives and pivot points still apply, but they shift faster, so the kernel needs revisiting more often than his stories suggest.

Verdict: 5 of 7 key concepts transcend and 2 evolve. If a leader reads one strategy book before writing an AI strategy, this is a strong candidate.

### 1. Run the fluff test

Check your current strategy or AI plan against Rumelt's four hallmarks of bad strategy.

```text
I just read a summary of Richard Rumelt's "Good Strategy Bad Strategy." Rumelt says bad strategy has four hallmarks: fluff (jargon that hides a lack of substance), failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives. A good strategy has a kernel: a diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy, and coherent actions.

I'll paste my team's current strategy below. Before reviewing it, ask me two questions about our situation and the obstacle we face. Then: (1) quote each sentence that shows one of the four hallmarks and name which one, (2) tell me whether it contains a real diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent actions, and (3) rewrite the diagnosis in one paragraph. Be direct.

[paste strategy here]
```

### 2. Sharpen your diagnosis

Write a one-paragraph diagnosis of your biggest challenge, then have the AI attack it.

```text
Richard Rumelt ("Good Strategy Bad Strategy") argues the most important part of a strategy is the diagnosis: a clear explanation of what is going on that names the critical challenge.

Ask me three questions about my situation, one at a time. Then draft a one-paragraph diagnosis that names the single biggest obstacle in my way. Next, argue against that diagnosis as a skeptical board member would. Finally, help me revise it until it is specific enough that it rules some actions out.
```

### 3. Find your weakest link

Map one workflow and find the step that limits everything else, before automating any of it.

```text
Richard Rumelt ("Good Strategy Bad Strategy") describes chain-link systems: performance is limited by the weakest link, so improving one step does nothing if another step holds everything back.

I'll describe an important workflow on my team. Ask clarifying questions until you can list each step in order. Then identify the most likely weakest link, explain why, and tell me what else would have to improve alongside it before automating any single step with AI.
```

## Critiques and caveats

The book is built on cases and stories, not data, so readers have to trust Rumelt's judgment about why each strategy worked. It's stronger at spotting bad strategy than at explaining how to produce a good diagnosis, a gap Rumelt took up more fully in *The Crux* (2022). Some examples draw on hindsight that's clearer than anything leaders had in the moment.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you write, approve or critique strategy, or if you need words for why a plan feels hollow. **Skip it** if you want templates or step-by-step frameworks; this book teaches judgment, not fill-in-the-blanks.
