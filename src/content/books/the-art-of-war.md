## At a glance

**The best victory is the one you never have to fight for: calculate before you act, know the other side and yourself, move fast, and strike where the opponent is weak.**

Sun Tzu · c. 5th century BC (Giles translation, 1910) · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

*The Art of War* is a Chinese military treatise in 13 short chapters, traditionally credited to Sun Tzu ("Master Sun"), a general said to have served the state of Wu. The historian Sima Qian recorded that King Helü of Wu read his work. Scholars have questioned Sun Tzu's existence since about the 12th century, and Britannica judges the text more likely to date from the early Warring States period (from 475 BC) than from his lifetime. Bamboo slips found at Yinqueshan in 1972, in Han tombs sealed between 134 and 118 BC, cover about a third of the chapters and match the received text very closely.

The book treats war as a matter of life and death for the state, so it is about avoiding waste and defeat as much as about winning. It moves from planning and cost, through stratagem, positioning and maneuver, to terrain, fire and spies.

This summary follows the 1910 translation by the British scholar Lionel Giles, which is public domain and free online, so every claim here can be checked against the full text. Modern translations, such as Samuel Griffith's (1963) and John Minford's (2002), differ in wording.

## Key concepts

### 1. Win first, then fight · Transcends

The book opens with calculation. A general should weigh five constant factors before any campaign: the Moral Law (whether the people are in accord with their ruler), Heaven (night and day, cold and heat, times and seasons), Earth (distances, danger and security, open ground and narrow passes), the Commander (wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage and strictness) and Method and discipline (the army's subdivisions, ranks, supply roads and spending). The winning general makes many calculations in the temple before the battle. Chapter IV sharpens the point: the victorious strategist seeks battle only after victory is already secured, while the one destined to lose fights first and hopes to win afterwards.

*Why it transcends:* Deciding whether a fight can be won before committing to it is sound in any market. AI can run the numbers faster; choosing which factors matter stays a human call.

### 2. Win without fighting · Transcends

Sun Tzu ranks the ways to prevail. The highest form of generalship is to thwart the enemy's plans. Next best is to stop the enemy's forces from joining up, then to attack its army in the field, and worst of all to besiege walled cities. Winning every battle is not the ideal: "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting." A good general knows when to fight and when not to.

*Why it transcends:* Head-on fights such as price wars and feature races drain both sides. Winning through position and choice of battlefield still underpins most strategy books on this list.

### 3. Speed over long campaigns · Transcends

Chapter II is about cost. An army in the field costs a thousand ounces of silver a day, and a protracted campaign strains the state's resources, dulls the troops' weapons and ardour, and lets other chieftains take advantage. Sun Tzu observes that there is no instance of a country benefiting from prolonged warfare: stupid haste is known in war, but cleverness has never been seen with long delays. His remedy is to make victory, not lengthy campaigns, the goal, and to bring war material from home but forage on the enemy.

*Why it transcends:* Long, expensive initiatives that never quite end are still one of the most common ways organizations lose. Faster tools make the cost of delay more visible, not smaller.

### 4. Deception and appearances · Evolves

"All warfare is based on deception." When able to attack, seem unable; when active, seem inactive; when near, make the enemy believe you are far away, and the reverse. Sun Tzu wants the opponent to misread strength, position and intent, so that it prepares in the wrong place.

*Why it evolves:* Shaping what competitors expect, through timing and what you reveal, still works. Deceiving customers, partners or regulators does not: business is a repeated game, and AI makes fakes cheap to produce but also patterns easier to spot.

### 5. Know the enemy and know yourself · Evolves

Chapter III ends with the book's best-known line: if you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. Know yourself but not the enemy, and each victory comes with a defeat; know neither, and you will lose every battle. The last chapter adds that such foreknowledge cannot come from spirits, experience or calculation, only from other people. Sun Tzu names five kinds of spies and calls it the height of inhumanity to stay ignorant of the enemy's condition to save a hundred ounces of silver.

*Why it evolves:* AI can digest public filings, reviews and job posts in minutes, so knowing the field is cheaper than ever. The edge moves to what is not published: what customers, partners and your own staff actually say, and an honest view of your own weaknesses.

### 6. Be like water · Evolves

Military tactics, Sun Tzu says, are like water, which runs away from high places and hastens downwards and shapes its course to the ground. In the same way, an army should avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak. Water has no constant shape, and war has no constant conditions, so the general who modifies his tactics to the opponent and wins may be called a heaven-born captain. Chapters V and VI add: use the direct method to join battle and indirect methods to secure victory, and march swiftly to places where you are not expected.

*Why it evolves:* Attacking the gaps a larger rival leaves holds as well as ever. What changes is the pace: when competitors can copy a move in weeks, weak points close faster and adaptation has to be continuous.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The text has lasted because it is about conflict between thinking people, which has not changed. Most of it is advice on judgment, which AI supports but does not replace.

Three concepts transcend. Calculating before committing, winning through position instead of collision, and avoiding long, draining campaigns fit a product launch as well as a campaign. AI makes the calculation faster, so there is less excuse to skip it.

Three evolve. Deception is cheaper to produce with AI and easier to catch, and in business it backfires with anyone you deal with repeatedly. Knowing the enemy gets easier for public information, so advantage shifts to what only people can tell you. Adapting like water matters more as rivals copy moves faster.

The risk is unchanged: it is a book about war, and treating customers or colleagues as enemies distorts business decisions. Its lasting value is restraint, preparation and self-knowledge.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

The book was written for rulers and generals, not companies, and most business uses of it rest on metaphor rather than evidence. Business is rarely zero-sum: customers, partners and even competitors are often not enemies, and reading them that way can do harm. Its aphorisms are easy to bend to whatever a reader already believes. Authorship and dating are uncertain, so it is safer to treat the text as a compiled tradition than as one general's testimony. Translations also vary enough that a single line can read quite differently across editions.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you like going to the source, want a short classic you can finish in an evening, or face real rivals. It is free online. **Skip it** if you want a modern business method; *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* covers the same ground of diagnosis and focused action with business cases.
