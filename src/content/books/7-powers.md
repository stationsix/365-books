## At a glance

**A business is only as valuable as its Power: a benefit that raises cash flow, paired with a barrier that stops competitors from taking it away, and there are just seven kinds.**

Hamilton Helmer · 2016 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Hamilton Helmer holds a PhD in economics from Yale and worked at Bain & Company. He founded his own strategy consultancy, where he led more than 200 projects for companies including Netflix, Adobe and Spotify, co-founded the investment firm Strategy Capital, where he is chief investment officer, and has taught business strategy in Stanford's economics department. Netflix's Reed Hastings wrote the foreword.

Helmer defines Power as "the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns," and a strategy as "a route to continuing Power in significant markets." He sums the idea up in what he calls the Fundamental Equation of Strategy: a business's value equals its market size and growth multiplied by its long-run market share and its long-run margin above the cost of capital. Share and margin are what Power protects.

At under 200 pages, the book has two parts: statics, which describes the seven Powers, and dynamics, which explains how and when a company acquires them. It deliberately looks at a single business, not a whole corporation; Helmer leaves corporate strategy for later work.

## Key concepts

### 1. Power needs a benefit and a barrier · Transcends

Every Power has two parts. The benefit is any improvement in cash flow: higher prices, lower costs or less investment. The barrier is what prevents competitors from copying the move and competing the benefit away. Power is also relative. It has to be judged against each competitor, actual and potential, because a single rival able to match the benefit is enough to drive the margin down. For any claimed advantage, the question is what stops a capable rival doing the same.

*Why it transcends:* When AI lets any team build a good product quickly, a benefit without a barrier fades faster than ever.

### 2. Scale and network economies · Evolves

With scale economies, cost per unit falls as volume rises. The barrier is the prohibitive cost of gaining share: a smaller rival that cuts prices to grow invites retaliation from a leader with lower costs. The book's running example is Netflix: once it paid for exclusive and original shows such as *House of Cards*, a fixed content cost was spread across a far larger subscriber base than any rival had. With network economies, a product is worth more to each customer as the installed base grows, as with a professional network like LinkedIn. A follower would need a discount so large it becomes unthinkable, so these markets often end with one winner.

*Why it evolves:* Both still apply, but AI changes the math. Training a frontier model is a classic fixed cost, yet every request also costs compute. Network effects are weaker for many AI tools, where users switch to whichever model performs best.

### 3. Counter-positioning · Transcends

A newcomer adopts a new, superior business model that the incumbent does not copy, because copying it would damage the incumbent's existing business. Helmer's barrier here is collateral damage. His main case is Vanguard's low-cost index funds: Fidelity, built on actively managed funds and their fees, held back because matching Vanguard would cannibalize its core business. Netflix played the same role against the store-based Blockbuster. Holding back is rational for the incumbent, which is what makes the position strong.

*Why it transcends:* This is the clearest lens on AI disruption. When an AI service threatens to replace billable hours, seats or headcount, the incumbent's hesitation is collateral damage at work.

### 4. Switching costs · Evolves

Switching costs are the value a customer expects to lose by moving to another supplier, and Helmer sorts them into financial, procedural and relational kinds. The benefit is the ability to charge existing customers more, which pays off only through follow-on sales to them. The barrier is that a rival must compensate customers for those costs to win them,. Helmer's example is enterprise software such as SAP, where implementation, customization and training make replacement daunting.

*Why it evolves:* AI tools can cut switching costs by migrating data and rewriting integrations quickly, while AI products that learn a team's work and history build new ones.

### 5. Branding · Transcends

Helmer defines branding as the durable attribution of higher value to an objectively identical offering, arising from what customers know of the seller's history. Customers pay more because of positive feelings toward the brand or because it reduces uncertainty, as with Bayer aspirin against a chemically similar store brand. His main example is Tiffany: buyers pay a premium over a comparable diamond from an online seller like Blue Nile. The barrier is hysteresis, his word for advantages that only build up over a long period of reinforcing actions. He also warns that off-brand products can dilute a brand and reset that clock.

*Why it transcends:* When AI makes polished content cheap, buyers lean more on reputation. A long track record is one thing AI cannot fake quickly.

### 6. Cornered resource and process power · Evolves

A cornered resource is preferential access, on attractive terms, to a coveted asset that independently adds value: a patent, a mineral deposit or a rare team. Helmer points to Pixar's tight group of creative veterans, its Brain Trust, and calls the barrier "fiat," meaning ownership or choice rules out copying. Process power is embedded organization and activity sets that lower costs or improve the product and can be matched only by an extended commitment. The Toyota Production System is the example: rivals struggled to copy it for decades even after studying it closely, because the barrier is again hysteresis.

*Why it evolves:* Proprietary data and scarce research talent are today's cornered resources. Process power may weaken where AI can document and copy what a firm does, or strengthen where an organization learns to work with AI faster than rivals.

### 7. The Power Progression · Transcends

All Power starts with invention, of a product, process, brand or business model, that delivers compelling value. Helmer describes three paths there: capabilities-led (Adobe Acrobat, which built on Adobe's graphics expertise), customer-led (Corning's fiber optics) and competitor-led (Sony's PlayStation). Each Power is then most likely to be established at a particular stage. In origination, before a business takes off, a firm can secure a cornered resource or counter-positioning, as Intel did when it won back rights to its microprocessor from the Japanese firm Busicom. In takeoff, the period of rapid growth, scale economies, network economies and switching costs are decided,. In stability, only branding and process power remain, since both need years to build, with rare exceptions.

*Why it transcends:* The progression tells a founder which Powers are still possible now and which have already been decided.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*7 Powers* was written before the current AI wave, but its main question is the one AI forces on every company: if competitors can now build almost anything quickly, what keeps your returns from being competed away?

The benefit and barrier test, counter-positioning, branding and the Power Progression transcend: they filter out AI strategies that rely on being first or slightly better, and they explain why firms with seat-based or hourly pricing hesitate. Scale, switching costs, cornered resources and process power still hold but evolve, as compute costs, fast migration and copyable processes change the arithmetic.

The AI reading here is ours, not the book's, though its framework makes it easier.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Test your advantage

Check whether what you call an advantage is really Power.

```text
Hamilton Helmer's "7 Powers" says lasting value needs Power: a benefit (higher prices, lower costs or less investment) plus a barrier that stops competitors from copying it. The seven Powers are scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource and process power. Power is relative, so it must hold against each capable rival.

Ask me four questions about my business, my customers and my strongest competitors. Then list each advantage I claim, name the benefit and the barrier for each, match it to one of the seven Powers or say it is none of them, and tell me which rival could copy it soonest.

[paste a description of your business and its advantages here]
```

### 2. Find your counter-position

Look for a business model the market leader would hurt itself by copying.

```text
In "7 Powers," counter-positioning means adopting a new, superior business model that the incumbent does not copy because doing so would damage its existing business (collateral damage). Vanguard's index funds against Fidelity's actively managed funds is the classic case.

Ask me three questions about the leading incumbents in my market and how they make money. Then propose two business models that would be better for customers and costly for the incumbents to copy, explain the collateral damage each incumbent would suffer, and say how long that hesitation might last.
```

### 3. Place yourself on the Power Progression

See which Powers are still available to you at your stage.

```text
Helmer's "7 Powers" says Power starts with an invention that delivers compelling value, then each Power can only be won at a certain stage. Origination (before takeoff): cornered resource and counter-positioning. Takeoff (rapid growth): scale economies, network economies and switching costs, a window that closes once growth slows. Stability: branding and process power, which take years to build.

Ask me three questions about my product, growth rate and market. Then tell me which stage I am in, which Powers are still open, which are probably already won or lost, and the two moves that matter most before my current window closes.
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my strategy adviser, grounded in Hamilton Helmer's "7 Powers." Core ideas: value depends on market size times Power, where Power is a benefit plus a barrier that keeps rivals from competing it away; there are seven Powers (scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource, process power); Power is judged against each competitor; counter-positioning works because incumbents fear collateral damage; Powers are won at different stages, from origination through takeoff to stability.

When I bring you a plan or decision: name the Power it builds or relies on, state its benefit and barrier, and challenge any advantage that lacks a barrier. Keep a running note in this project of my current Powers, my stage on the Power Progression, and the competitors each Power must hold against.
```

## Critiques and caveats

The strategy writer Jeff Towson praises the framework as, in Helmer's own phrase, "simple but not simplistic," yet lists four problems: it does not say where it stops working, it fits great companies in fast-changing markets rather than ordinary ones like restaurants, it helps little in stable, predictable industries, and it says little about linked platforms and ecosystems. Two caveats of our own: the book measures everything in shareholder value, so it may read more naturally for investors than for managers deciding what to do on Monday, and its cases are mostly companies that won, analyzed after the fact. By design it also leaves out corporate-level strategy across several businesses.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you invest in companies, found or fund startups, or need a compact way to judge whether a competitive advantage will last. **Skip it** if your business competes mostly on execution in a stable local market; *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* offers more practical help with making a plan.
