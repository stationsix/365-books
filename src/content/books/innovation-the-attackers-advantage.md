## At a glance

**Every technology improves along an S-curve until it nears a physical limit, and leaders lose not because they stop working but because they keep pouring effort into the old curve while an attacker climbs a new one.**

Richard N. Foster · 1986 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Richard Foster trained as an engineer, earning three degrees at Yale including a PhD in engineering and applied science, and joined McKinsey & Company in 1973. He became a director in 1982 and founded and led the firm's technology and healthcare practices. *Innovation: The Attacker's Advantage* (Summit Books, 1986) was his first book, and the Wall Street Journal named it one of the year's best business books.

The book asks why established, well-run companies lose markets quickly to smaller newcomers with faster-advancing technology. Foster's answer is that leaders misread where their technology stands. Progress on any technology follows an S-curve, slow, then fast, then slow again as it nears a limit. Defenders keep investing in a curve that is running out, while attackers bet on the next one. In the attacker's view, risky as innovation is, not innovating is riskier.

The book works through cases from old and new industries, from sailing ships and tire cords to transistors and detergents, and ends by putting the burden of change on the chief executive.

## Key concepts

### 1. The S-curve: performance against effort · Transcends

Foster's S-curve plots the effort put into improving a product or process against the results that effort buys. Early on, money and engineering produce little, because the basic knowledge is still forming. Then progress takes off. Then each extra dollar buys less, until the curve flattens. The key move is the horizontal axis: progress comes from applying effort, not from the passage of time. The book's chapter on the idea calls it a new forecasting tool, and Foster argues it lets a company compare what it spends with what it gets, and see a slowdown coming.

*Why it transcends:* Plotting results against effort, not dates, is still the right way to ask whether a technology, an AI model family included, is speeding up or running out of road.

### 2. Limits and the people who spot them · Evolves

Curves flatten, Foster argues, because they approach natural limits. Ships under sail stopped getting much faster, and steam took over. Near a limit, progress gets steadily more expensive, so knowing where the limit sits helps a firm anticipate change or stop pouring money into something that cannot improve. Foster describes "limitists," people unusually good at recognizing limits and the ways around them. A limit can also be one of perception. When clothes were about as clean as detergent could get them, Procter & Gamble added optical brighteners so they looked cleaner, and Tide sold well.

*Why it evolves:* The idea holds, but limits now move faster and are harder to see. AI performance can stall on one approach and jump on another, so a limit is closer to a working guess than a fixed wall.

### 3. Discontinuities between curves · Transcends

Technologies rarely improve forever; they get replaced. Foster calls the break between one S-curve and the next a technological discontinuity, the point where a new knowledge base takes over from the old one. Vacuum tubes gave way to solid-state electronics, and steamships carried sails for a time as hybrids. Tire cords are another of the book's cases. As Tom Peters told it in a 1986 column, DuPont dominated nylon tire cord for years, while Celanese, with no nylon business to protect, backed polyester and took the market.

*Why it transcends:* The pattern of a new curve arriving from people with nothing to lose fits many AI transitions, and so does the hybrid phase of old products with new parts bolted on.

### 4. Effectiveness versus efficiency · Transcends

Foster separates two choices. Effectiveness is set when a company decides which S-curve to pursue, vacuum tubes or solid state. Efficiency is the slope of the curve it is on. A manager who works hard on efficiency near the top of an old curve may raise it by about 50 percent, the book argues, which is little help against an attacker whose productivity on a new curve may be climbing ten times faster. Defenders also tend to believe their productivity is higher than the attacker's, because the market still treats the new product as a curiosity.

*Why it transcends:* Teams that use AI only to speed up an existing process are working on efficiency. The larger question, which curve the work should be on, stays the same.

### 5. The defender's financial blind spot · Evolves

Foster argues that the numbers mislead incumbents. It always looks more economic to protect the old business than to feed the new one, and conventional financial analysis has no practical way to count the opportunity cost of not investing in the next technology. As Peters summarized the book, financial tools push firms to over-invest in incremental improvements to profitable, predictable technology. The remedy is uncomfortable: leaders must give up the skills and products that made them successful and cannibalize their current products while they are still at their most lucrative.

*Why it evolves:* The bias is unchanged, but cheaper experiments lower the cost of feeding the new business alongside the old, which weakens the excuse even if it does not remove the pull.

### 6. The attacker's advantage · Evolves

The title claim, as Clayton Christensen summarized it, is that attackers often seize the advantage when a new technology invades a market, and leaders lose because they reinforce and refine maturing approaches. Attackers, with no old business to defend, move first and treat not innovating as the bigger risk. The book puts the burden on leadership: the change will not happen unless the chief executive swaps a search for efficiency for a quest for competitiveness.

*Why it evolves:* Later research found attackers win some kinds of shifts and not others, so the advantage is better treated as a question to test than a rule.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

Foster's public statements we read do not address AI, so what follows is our reading of his framework, not his view.

The S-curve may be the most useful single picture for thinking about AI progress, because it asks the right question: what does the next unit of effort buy? If returns to more compute and data are flattening on one approach, Foster's framework predicts that the next jump comes from a different curve, and quite possibly from a different company.

Effectiveness versus efficiency is the sharpest test for managers. Most AI rollouts raise the slope of an existing process. Foster's warning is that a better slope on a maturing curve can still lose to a rival who has moved to a new one. The defender's financial blind spot also holds: the case for protecting current revenue always looks stronger in a spreadsheet than the case for a product that does not exist yet.

What needs updating is the confidence about limits and attackers. Limits in AI shift quickly, and some recent shifts came from large incumbents, not small entrants.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve.

## Critiques and caveats

The sharpest test came from Clayton Christensen, whose 1992 studies of disk drive components found S-curves useful at the industry level but ambiguous for a single firm's planning. Limits looked like moving targets rather than fixed barriers, plateaus were partly self-fulfilling when forecasts pulled engineers away, and entrants that pioneered new component technologies rarely won, though first movers, attackers among them, did well in architectural shifts. Lecture notes from an MIT innovation course that teach the book's S-curve chapter ask whether the curve has ever forecast accurately. Foster's own focus later shifted. *Creative Destruction* (2001, with Sarah Kaplan) argued that companies built on an assumption of continuity underperform the market, and must design in the creation of new businesses and the removal of weak ones. We read it as a sign that switching curves proved harder for incumbents than the 1986 book implied.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want the original S-curve argument behind later disruption theory, or you manage a technology that may be nearing its limit. **Skip it** if you want a current guide: its examples are from the 1980s, and *The Innovator's Dilemma* covers the incumbent's problem with stronger data.
