## At a glance

**A product that gets better as more people use it starts out at its worst, so the job is to build one small network that works on its own, then copy it, and keep fighting the forces that stall growth later.**

Andrew Chen · 2021 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Andrew Chen is a general partner at Andreessen Horowitz, where he invests in consumer technology. Before that he led rider growth at Uber while its active users grew from about 15 million to 100 million. His tech and business newsletter has a large following.

The book, published by Harper Business in December 2021, draws on what Chen describes as more than a hundred interviews with founders and teams, from Slack and Airbnb to Twitch. Its starting point is that network effects, where a product becomes more useful as more people use it, work in reverse at launch: with few users, the product has little to offer, and people leave.

Chen organizes the answer as a life cycle in five stages: the Cold Start Problem, the Tipping Point, Escape Velocity, Hitting the Ceiling and the Moat. Each stage gets its own tactics, and short chapters built around case studies carry the argument, with Uber, Tinder, Slack, Zoom, Dropbox, LinkedIn and Wikipedia among them.

## Key concepts

### 1. The cold start problem · Transcends

A new networked product begins with almost no one on it, and an empty network is a bad product. Chen's opening chapter puts it bluntly: "This is the Cold Start Problem, and if it's not overcome quickly, a new product will die." The goal is the magic moment, when a user finds the network already working. Chen also tracks its opposite, which Uber called a Zero: a rider opens the app and no drivers are nearby, and that rider may not come back.

*Why it transcends:* Any product whose value depends on other people being there, from a chat app to a marketplace for AI agents, faces the empty-room problem on day one.

### 2. The atomic network · Transcends

Chen defines the atomic network as "the smallest network needed that can stand on its own." His point is that it is usually smaller and more specific than founders expect. Slack's was the team around one user, possibly under ten people in a company. Uber's first networks were not cities but moments, such as 5pm at the Caltrain station at 5th and King in San Francisco. Credit cards needed a whole city: in 1958 Bank of America mailed BankAmericards to 60,000 residents of Fresno, California. Once one atomic network works, the company copies it into the next one nearby.

*Why it transcends:* Starting dense and narrow instead of broad and thin is a lesson about how networks form, not about any one technology.

### 3. The hard side · Transcends

Most networks have an easy side and a hard side: a small group that does most of the work and creates most of the value. Wikipedia has hundreds of millions of users but only about 4,000 volunteers who make more than 100 edits a month, and at Uber the power drivers were 20% of drivers but provided 60% of trips. The hard side is also the hardest to win and keep, so the product must solve a real problem for them. In Chen's telling of Tinder, swiping kept its most sought-after users from feeling overwhelmed, since they matched only with people they had chosen.

*Why it transcends:* Every network still depends on its few heavy contributors, and they now have more places than ever to take their work.

### 4. Tactics for the tipping point · Evolves

Invite-only launches, which LinkedIn used early on, give new users a warmer welcome because they arrive already connected to someone. "Come for the tool, stay for the network," a phrase Chen borrows from investor Chris Dixon, hooks users with something useful alone, as Instagram's photo filters did, then pulls them into the network. Reddit's founders filled the early front page with posts themselves, using dummy accounts. Some companies simply pay, as Uber did with heavy driver incentives and referral bonuses.

*Why it evolves:* AI makes a good single-player tool cheap to build and easy to copy, so the tool buys less time before the network has to carry the product.

### 5. Escape velocity and the three effects · Evolves

Chen splits network effects into three forces. The acquisition effect brings users in cheaply through virality and referrals, as with PayPal, whose payments and badges spread the product from user to user. The engagement effect raises use as the network fills in, moving users up a ladder of new uses; Uber riders went from airport trips to dining out to daily commutes. The economic effect improves monetization as the network grows.

*Why it evolves:* The split holds, but AI changes each force: it lowers the cost of acquisition for everyone, and engagement measured in clicks says less when machine-made content fills a feed.

### 6. Hitting the ceiling · Evolves

Growth stalls for many reasons: saturation, waves of newcomers who change a community's culture (Usenet's "Eternal September" is his cautionary tale), trolls and spam. Chen's Law of Shitty Clickthroughs, first set out on his blog in 2012, says every marketing channel decays over time as users tune it out. Context collapse sets in when a post meant for one audience is seen by every audience. The hard side can revolt: drivers, sellers and creators protest, as in the Reddit blackout. The fix is new products, formats and adjacent networks, as eBay restarted growth with Buy It Now.

*Why it evolves:* Spam, fraud and channel decay arrive faster when AI can produce content and fake accounts at almost no cost.

### 7. The moat: network against network · Evolves

Chen's final stage is competition between networks, and he doubts that winner takes all. Upstarts cherry-pick an incumbent's underserved niches and build an atomic network there. Incumbents can use their scale, and the simplest route for a large company, Chen writes, is to buy startups that have hit escape velocity. Network effects are often local: Uber's lead in New York did not carry to San Diego, and it pulled out of China and Southeast Asia.

*Why it evolves:* The logic holds, but when software agents can work across several networks for one user, the cost of leaving a network falls and moats need more than size.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Cold Start Problem* is a practitioner's playbook, and its core holds. The cold start itself, the atomic network and the hard side describe how people come to rely on each other in a product, which no technology has changed. These three concepts transcend.

The other four evolve, mostly because AI changes costs. It is now cheap to build a useful tool, so "come for the tool" protects less. It is cheap to make content, fake profiles and spam, so the ceiling arrives sooner and the hard side needs more protection from noise.

A question the book leaves open is whether AI products have network effects at all. A model that improves with usage data may look like a network, but users do not see or rely on each other, so the book's tactics may apply only to the parts of an AI product where people interact. That reading is ours, not Chen's.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve. None expire.

### 2. Win the hard side

Work out who does most of the work on your network and what would keep them.

```text

The product must solve a real problem for them, and good places to look are where they are already active but underserved, such as hobbies and side hustles.

```

Find out why growth has slowed and what could restart it.

```text
In "The Cold Start Problem," Chen says growth stalls because of market saturation, decaying marketing channels (every channel's response rate falls over time), waves of newcomers who change the community's culture, context collapse, trolls and spam, and revolts by the hard side. Growth restarts with new products, formats and adjacent networks.

Ask me four questions about my growth curve, my channels and how my community has changed. Then rank which of these forces is most likely behind my slowdown, say what evidence would confirm it, and suggest one new product, format or adjacent network that could restart growth.

[paste your growth metrics and a description of recent changes here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

A review in TIME's Charter newsletter praised the framework but said the book underplays the downsides of the companies it holds up, such as Uber's workplace controversies and YouTube's tendency to promote extremist and misleading videos, and that it does not examine how Web3 might change its argument. The rest are our caveats. The case studies are mostly winners, and several are companies connected to Chen's own firm, which invests in networked products, so the evidence leans toward stories that confirm the framework. The book offers benchmarks and examples rather than tests of how often its tactics fail.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you are launching a marketplace, a social or messaging product, or workplace software that spreads team by team, and want tactics for each stage. **Skip it** if your product works the same for one user as for a million; *7 Powers* treats network economies as one advantage among several, more briefly.
