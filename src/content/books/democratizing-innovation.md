## At a glance

**Many new products are first built by the people who need them, not the companies that sell them, and as design tools get cheaper and sharing gets easier, firms that learn to work with these user-innovators gain an edge.**

Eric von Hippel · 2005 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Eric von Hippel, born in 1941, is a professor at the MIT Sloan School of Management. He introduced the idea of lead users in 1986, and his earlier book, *The Sources of Innovation* (1988), first made the case that users often innovate before manufacturers do.

This book pulls that research into one argument. Users, in his terms, expect to benefit from using a product; manufacturers expect to benefit from selling one. Innovation is being democratized because users are increasingly able to innovate for themselves, helped by better design tools and by what others share freely. The book argues that this is good for society and that firms and policy makers should adapt.

MIT Press published it in 2005, with a free electronic version under a Creative Commons license. Its twelve chapters move from evidence (chapters 2 to 7) through policy (8) and the broader trend (9) to two applications for firms (10 and 11) and links to other fields (12).

## Key concepts

### 1. Users as innovators · Transcends

The book's starting fact is that many users build or modify the products they use. Across the studies collected in chapter 2, from circuit design software to surgical equipment and extreme sports gear, 10 percent to nearly 40 percent of users had done so. The opening example is Hawaiian windsurfers who built experimental boards with footstraps for jumping. The motive is direct. "Users that innovate can develop exactly what they want, rather than relying on manufacturers to act as their (often very imperfect) agents."

*Why it transcends:* People who feel a problem every day still see it first and care most about fixing it, whatever tools they use.

### 2. Lead users · Transcends

Lead users share two traits: they are ahead of most users on an important market trend, and they expect high benefits from solving the needs they meet there. The book reports that the stronger these traits, the more commercially attractive a user's innovation tends to be. Chapter 10 turns this into a method. Small teams pick a trend, then use pyramiding, asking each expert to name someone more expert, to find lead users in the target market and in analogous fields, such as antilock brakes first built for aircraft. At 3M, lead user projects were forecast to earn about eight times the sales of conventional ones.

*Why it transcends:* The edge of any market, including AI tools, is where mainstream needs show up first.

### 3. Custom needs and the innovate-or-buy decision · Evolves

Chapter 3 argues that needs vary far more than market segments suggest. In a study of security features for the Apache web server, users showed almost no clustering, and those who customized their software were more satisfied than those who did not. Chapter 4 explains why users build instead of buying: a manufacturer serving a custom need is an imperfect agent. It wants to cut its costs by reusing solution elements it already has, and contracts cannot fully specify what the user wants. In one study of stressed-skin panels for buildings, users developed 82 percent of the key innovations, at an average cost of $153 each.

*Why it evolves:* AI assistants cut the cost of building a custom fix, which should push more users toward building, though the agency problem returns whenever the assistant's defaults are not the user's.

### 4. Sticky information and low-cost niches · Evolves

Information is sticky when it is costly to move to where it is needed. Users hold sticky knowledge of their needs and contexts; manufacturers hold sticky knowledge of solutions. So each innovates where its own information is cheap. In a study of scientific instruments, users made 82 percent of the innovations that added a new functional capability, while manufacturers made 87 percent of the improvements in convenience or reliability.

*Why it evolves:* Explaining needs to an AI assistant in plain words lowers some transfer costs, but tacit know-how about a job or a body still sticks to the person who has it.

### 5. Free revealing and innovation communities · Transcends

Chapter 6 shows that user-innovators often give their work away. Libraries freely revealed 56 percent of their search software modifications, and rival firms shared improvements in nineteenth-century English iron furnaces and in embedded Linux. Protection is often impractical, and revealing brings reputation and improvements. Chapter 7 describes innovation communities, networks of people or firms linked by information transfer, with open source software as the main case and kitesurfers swapping kite designs as CAD files as a physical one. A private-collective model explains why people invest privately and still share.

*Why it transcends:* Open model weights, shared prompts and public code repositories run on the same logic of private effort and free sharing.

### 6. Toolkits for user innovation · Evolves

Chapter 11 proposes that manufacturers stop guessing at custom needs and hand users a toolkit instead, splitting the work so users do the need-heavy design and the firm does the solution-heavy production. A good toolkit supports rapid trial and error, offers a large enough solution space and produces designs the factory can make without rework. In custom chips, development time fell by two thirds or more, and in 2000 toolkit-designed custom chips worth more than $15 billion were sold. The book notes the limits: toolkits suit users who share a common solution approach, not products that need peak performance.

*Why it evolves:* A 2026 paper by Julian De Freitas and colleagues says generative AI can hand users solution information, a mode von Hippel's toolkit work foreshadowed. The solution space is far larger, and so is the need for checks.

### 7. Policy for an information commons · Evolves

Chapter 8 cites modeling by Henkel and von Hippel suggesting that social welfare is likely to be higher when users and manufacturers both innovate. Because patents, copyright and trade secrets are costly or weak for small user-innovators, the book warns about patent thickets and laws such as the DMCA that restrict modification. It recommends better data on user innovation, considering separate ownership of content and distribution channels, and tying R&D subsidies to free revealing.

*Why it evolves:* Fights over who may modify and share AI models make this chapter more pressing, though the laws have moved on.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

Von Hippel wrote this before today's AI assistants, and this summary draws on no view of his about them, so what follows is our reading.

The book's central claim, that better software, hardware and a richer innovation commons let more users innovate, fits AI closely. An AI assistant adds all three at once: it can write code, draft designs and explain a field to a user who knows the need but not the solution. That moves solution knowledge toward the user, the reverse of the sticky-information split.

What holds is the user's advantage. The person with the problem still sets the goal and judges the result. What changes is scale and risk: more people build, and more half-tested tools get shared. The lead user method gains a new kind of target, the people already building their own AI workflows ahead of the market.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve.

### 1. Find your lead users

Locate the people already solving tomorrow's version of your customers' problem.

```text

Teams find them by pyramiding (asking each expert who is more expert) and by searching analogous fields that face a harsher version of the same need.

Ask me four questions about my product, the trend I think matters and who uses the product hardest. Then name the trend precisely, describe what a lead user would look like in my market and in two analogous fields, list where I could find them, and draft three pyramiding questions to ask the first people I contact.

[paste a short description of your product and market here]
```

### 2. Spot the workarounds

Turn the hacks your customers already build into product ideas.

```text
"Democratizing Innovation" reports that 10 to nearly 40 percent of users build or modify the products they use, because needs vary more than segments suggest and a manufacturer is an imperfect agent for a custom need. Users tend to invent new functions; manufacturers tend to improve convenience and reliability.

Ask me three questions about my customers and the support tickets, forum posts or feedback I see. Then read the material below, list the workarounds and modifications customers describe, sort them into new functions versus convenience fixes, and rank the three most promising to build into the product, noting which users should help test each one.

[paste customer feedback, forum threads or support notes here]
```

### 3. Design a user toolkit

Decide which part of a custom job to hand to customers.

```text
In "Democratizing Innovation," von Hippel argues that firms can hand users a toolkit, splitting the work so users do the design that depends on their needs and the firm does production that depends on its know-how. A good toolkit supports trial and error, offers a large enough solution space, is easy to use, includes standard modules, and turns out designs the firm can make without rework. Toolkits fit when users share a common solution approach, not when peak performance matters.

Ask me four questions about the custom work my team does and where it gets stuck going back and forth with clients. Then say whether a toolkit fits, which tasks to give users, what modules and limits it needs, and how to test it with five customers.

[paste a description of your custom work and its usual delays here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

The book is candid about its own limits: the 3M comparison was quasi-experimental, the sample was small and the sales were forecasts, and lead users "typically have no reason to lead, mislead, or even contact manufacturers," so firms must go looking. About half the studies behind its 10 to 40 percent range do not establish how common user innovation is across whole populations. Paul Trott, Patrick van der Duin and Dap Hartmann (*Prometheus*, 2013) found conceptual, methodological and empirical weaknesses in lead user theory and cited earlier work finding that the most radical innovations of recent decades did not come from users. A 2014 study of LEGO's user ecosystem by Christoph Hienerth and colleagues notes costs: lead user ideas can meet not-invented-here resistance, firms lose some control to user communities, and toolkits confine users to a closed solution space.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run product, research or open source work and want the evidence for user innovation, lead users and toolkits; it is free from MIT. **Skip it** if you want a quick playbook: chapters 1, 10 and 11 hold most of the practice, and the HBS Working Knowledge summary covers the core.
