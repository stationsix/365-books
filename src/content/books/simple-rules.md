## At a glance

**Complex problems rarely need complex solutions: a handful of concrete rules, tailored to the people using them and aimed at the one decision that matters most, often works better.**

Donald Sull and Kathleen M. Eisenhardt · 2015 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Donald Sull is a senior lecturer at the MIT Sloan School of Management and the author of *The Upside of Turbulence*. Kathleen M. Eisenhardt is the S. W. Ascherman Professor of Strategy at Stanford and co-director of the Stanford Technology Ventures Program; her research focuses on strategy and decision making in highly uncertain markets.

The pair first set out the idea in a 2001 *Harvard Business Review* article, "Strategy as Simple Rules," which argued that companies in fast-moving markets do best with a few rules of their own: how-to rules, boundary conditions, priority rules, timing rules and exit rules. *Simple Rules* widens that idea well beyond business, with examples that run from interest rate decisions at the Federal Reserve to climbers on Everest and burglars choosing which house to rob.

The authors explain why simple rules work, sort them into rules for decisions and rules for action, show where good rules come from, and apply a three-step method to strategy and to personal goals, before turning to improving and breaking rules.

## Key concepts

### 1. Simplicity as the answer to complexity · Transcends

Complexity, in Sull and Eisenhardt's account, arises when a system has many interdependent parts. People tend to answer it with complex solutions, which can overwhelm the people expected to follow them. "Meeting complexity with complexity can create more confusion than it resolves." The alternative is a handful of guidelines tailored to the user and the task. Effective rules are few, fit the person or organization using them, apply to a well-defined activity or decision, and give clear guidance while leaving room for judgment.

*Why it transcends:* More tools and data add to complexity rather than remove it. A short list people can remember is as useful as ever.

### 2. Why simple can beat sophisticated · Evolves

Simple rules work, the authors argue, for three reasons. They let people pursue new opportunities while staying consistent. They can produce better decisions when information is limited and time is short. And they let members of a group synchronize their actions. Simple rules can match or beat complex models, partly because a model fitted too closely to past data hardwires error into it (overfitting). The authors acknowledge, though, that companies such as Netflix pair simple rules with large amounts of sophisticated analytics.

*Why it evolves:* Where data is plentiful and the past is a good guide, AI models can beat rules of thumb. Where data is thin or the situation is new, the case for a few robust rules still holds.

### 3. Rules for decisions: boundary, prioritizing and stopping · Transcends

The first family structures choices. "Boundary rules guide the choice of what to do (and not do) without requiring a lot of time, analysis, or information." Many burglars, for instance, skip houses with cars in the driveway. Prioritizing rules rank alternatives competing for scarce money, time or attention, as the Brazilian freight railway América Latina Logística did when managers requested far more capital than it had. Stopping rules say when to stop or reverse course. The investor Gerald Loeb's rule was to sell any investment that lost 10 percent of its initial value. On Everest in 1996, expedition leader Scott Fischer's rule was to turn around if not at the top by 2:00; he and most of the party ignored it, and eight people died. Such rules matter most when people tend to double down on a losing hand.

*Why it transcends:* What to do, what comes first and when to quit are the core choices in any strategy.

### 4. Rules for action: how-to, coordination and timing · Evolves

The second family, process rules, helps people carry things out when flexibility matters more than consistency, steering between chaos and rigidity. How-to rules, the most widely used, guide the basics of a task. Coordination rules let many actors work together using local information, like Napoleon's instruction to march toward the sound of gunfire. Timing rules tie action to events or to the clock and calendar: Pixar, for example, after *Toy Story* took four years, moved to a rhythm of one film a year.

*Why it evolves:* Instructions for AI agents are a new use for these rules. A few clear how-to, coordination and timing rules may guide agents better than a long manual, though the book could not test that.

### 5. Strategy as simple rules: needles, bottleneck, rules · Transcends

The authors' method has three steps. First, figure out what will move the needles: the few critical choices that drive results. Second, choose a bottleneck, a recurring decision or activity where opportunities exceed resources. The German machine maker Weima received about 10,000 requests for proposals a year but could fill orders for only about 1,000 machines, so choosing which to pursue was its bottleneck. Third, craft the rules with a small team that includes the people who will use them, rather than from the top down. Rules can draw on personal experience, others' experience, scientific evidence and negotiation. The method also works for personal goals, such as a St. Louis lawyer who crafted rules for coping with depression.

*Why it transcends:* Finding the bottleneck and involving the people who do the work are durable habits. AI can gather evidence, but choosing what matters is still a human call.

### 6. Improving rules, and knowing when to break them · Evolves

A first set of rules is unlikely to be perfect. Because a bottleneck recurs, its rules can be tested and refined against feedback. Rules also go stale: rules that evolved over time can become entrenched and outlive their usefulness. And simplicity has barriers, including the effort of writing good rules and people who benefit from complexity. Netflix is the authors' example of cutting back, replacing thick expense and HR manuals with a few rules after concluding that most of its employees could be trusted.

*Why it evolves:* AI makes it cheaper to track how rules perform and spot when they drift. Revising them deliberately is still the discipline.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Simple Rules* holds up well because AI adds to the complexity it describes. More tools and options mean more choices to filter, and a few memorable rules remain one of the best filters people have.

Three concepts transcend: meeting complexity with simplicity, the decision rules, and the three-step method for writing rules with the people who use them.

Three evolve. The claim that simple rules beat sophisticated models needs a qualifier where data is rich and the future resembles the past. Process rules gain a new use in instructing AI agents. And refinement gets easier when tools show which rules are working.

The risk runs both ways. Teams may hand rule-making to an AI assistant and lose the buy-in that comes from users writing their own rules, or trust a model where a simple stopping rule would have saved them.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Find your bottleneck

Pick the one recurring decision where a few rules would help most.

```text
In "Simple Rules," Donald Sull and Kathleen Eisenhardt say to start by figuring out what will move the needles (the few choices that drive results), then choose a bottleneck: a recurring decision or activity where opportunities exceed resources and better choices would add real value.

Ask me four questions about my goals, my team and the decisions we make repeatedly. Then list three candidate bottlenecks, rate each on impact, how often it recurs and how scarce the resources are, and recommend one.

[paste a description of your team, goals and recurring decisions here]
```

### 2. Draft simple rules for a decision

Turn a messy decision into a handful of rules people can remember.

```text
Sull and Eisenhardt ("Simple Rules") describe decision rules of three kinds: boundary rules (what to do and not do), prioritizing rules (how to rank options competing for scarce resources) and stopping rules (when to stop or reverse). Good rules are few, tailored to the people using them, aimed at one well-defined decision, and leave room for judgment.

Ask me three questions about the decision and who makes it. Then draft no more than six rules, label each as boundary, prioritizing or stopping, and explain how I could test them with the people who will use them.

[paste the decision and any past examples here]
```

### 3. Audit an existing rulebook

Cut a long policy or process down to what matters.

```text
"Simple Rules" by Sull and Eisenhardt argues that meeting complexity with complex rules creates confusion and can make people stop following them, and that rules can become entrenched and outlive their usefulness. Good rules are few, tailored to their users, aimed at one well-defined activity, and are tested and refined over time.

Ask me two questions about who uses this policy and what problems it causes. Then mark which rules are essential, which are too vague to guide anyone and which are out of date, and propose a short replacement set with a plan to review it.

[paste your policy, checklist or process here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

A *Sydney Morning Herald* review judged the book neither a silver bullet nor a self-help book. A review in the *Press and Journal* found parts of it tiring because the same point is explained in several ways, while calling it a solid guide overall. Writing on Oxfam's *From Poverty to Power* blog, Duncan Green noted that the success stories in the authors' 2001 article included Yahoo and Enron, and that in the aid sector unpredictability tends to bring more rules, not fewer. Our own caveats: many of the examples are stories of rules that worked, so readers cannot tell how often similar rules failed, and the authors' own Netflix example shows simple rules often sit on top of heavy analytics.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you manage a team or a process buried in policy, or want a practical method for turning strategy into a few rules people will follow, with plenty of examples from outside business. **Skip it** if you want only the method; the three steps and six rule types above cover the core, and the 2001 article "Strategy as Simple Rules" gives the original business version in a few pages.
