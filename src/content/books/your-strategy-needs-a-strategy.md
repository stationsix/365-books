## At a glance

**There is no single right way to make strategy: first read how predictable, how shapeable and how harsh your environment is, then pick the approach that fits, and be ready to run several at once.**

Martin Reeves, Knut Haanaes and Janmejaya Sinha · 2015 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

When the book came out, all three authors were senior partners at the Boston Consulting Group. Martin Reeves directed the BCG Bruce Henderson Institute in New York, Knut Haanaes was global leader of BCG's Strategy practice in Geneva, and Janmejaya Sinha was chairman of BCG Asia Pacific and a member of its Executive Committee in Mumbai.

The book opens plainly: "Strategy is a means to an end: favorable business outcomes." Strategy usually means planning, the authors note, but an oil company's multi-decade plan would make no sense to a software firm facing new rivals every day. Their answer to a crowd of contradictory strategy ideas is a strategy palette of five approaches, each matched to a type of environment.

Reeves described the research base as ten years of work at BCG, including a survey of 150 multinationals, performance data for all US public companies since 1950, case studies, CEO interviews and a strategy simulator. After the five approaches come chapters on combining them and on leadership, an epilogue on personal mastery, and appendices with a self-assessment and a simulation model.

## Key concepts

### 1. Read the environment before choosing the approach · Transcends

The core diagnostic asks three questions about a business environment. Predictability: can you forecast it? Malleability: can you shape it, alone or with others? Harshness: can you survive it? The answers point to one of five approaches. Predictable but hard to shape calls for classical strategy. Unpredictable and hard to shape calls for adaptive. Predictable and shapeable calls for visionary. Unpredictable but shapeable calls for shaping. Harsh conditions, where resources are severely constrained, call for renewal. An earlier *Harvard Business Review* article of the same title, by Reeves, Claire Love and Philipp Tillmanns, found that about three in four surveyed executives relied on classical and visionary styles, and nearly 90 percent planned annually however fast their markets moved.

*Why it transcends:* Asking what kind of game you are in before choosing how to play is a habit no tool replaces. AI is changing the answers in many industries.

### 2. Classical: be big · Evolves

The classical approach is the familiar one: analyze, plan, execute. It seeks a lasting advantage by positioning the firm well in an attractive market, where the bases of competition are established and stable. Advantage comes from scale, differentiation or superior capabilities. The chapter's opening case is Mars, which the authors credit with earning and keeping market leadership on long-lived brands such as Snickers, M&M's and Twix. In an interview with Knowledge at Wharton, Reeves called this approach fine for some situations but "no longer a panacea."

*Why it evolves:* AI makes forecasts and analysis cheaper and faster, which helps classical players. It can also make planners more confident than their markets justify.

### 3. Adaptive: be fast · Transcends

When the environment can be neither predicted nor shaped, a lasting advantage gives way to what BCG calls serial temporary advantage. The adaptive approach runs a loop: vary (try many options), select (keep what works) and scale (expand the winners fast). In this mode, Reeves told Knowledge at Wharton, strategy emerges continuously from experiments rather than from a plan. The 2012 article added that the goal is flexibility rather than efficiency, and that cultures that punish failure block it.

*Why it transcends:* Many markets touched by AI change faster than an annual plan. A disciplined loop of cheap experiments is close to a default for them.

### 4. Visionary: be first · Evolves

The visionary approach fits an environment that is predictable because the firm itself can shape it. Its steps are envisage, build and persist: spot an opportunity early, such as a megatrend, a new technology or customer dissatisfaction; be first to build the company and product that realize it; and stick to a fixed goal while staying flexible about the means. Timing matters: winners exploit the gap before incumbents react. Reeves cited 23andMe as an example.

*Why it evolves:* AI can test a vision faster and cheaper than before. The gaps between opportunity and incumbent reaction may also be shorter, so being first lasts less long.

### 5. Shaping: be the orchestrator · Transcends

When an environment is unpredictable but can be influenced, often because an industry is young and its rules are still being written, a firm can work with others to steer the market. Its ecosystem spreads risk and brings in complementary capabilities. The steps are engage other players around a shared vision, orchestrate a platform for their collaboration, and keep evolving the platform and ecosystem. The firm must influence partners it cannot control. Reeves pointed to marketplaces such as Alibaba and ecosystem-based companies such as Red Hat and Amazon.

*Why it transcends:* Platforms, model providers and developer communities make orchestration a central question in AI markets.

### 6. Renewal: be viable · Transcends

Renewal is for harsh conditions, whether from an outside or inside shock or a long mismatch between the firm and its market. The firm notices the decline early, changes course to preserve and free up resources, then redirects them toward growth; the aim is to thrive again, not just survive. The book's case is American Express in the 2008 financial crisis, when missed payments rose and funding markets tightened. CEO Ken Chenault responded quickly with an aggressive cost-cutting and restructuring program. Reeves told Knowledge at Wharton that 75 percent of renewal projects fail and that a company cannot cut its way to success. Renewal ends in a pivot to one of the other four approaches.

*Why it transcends:* Cutting back to survive, then reinvesting in growth, is how firms get through any shock, including one that AI brings to their business model.

### 7. Ambidexterity and the leader as animator · Evolves

Large firms face several environments at once, so they must run several approaches, together or in sequence, by separating units with their own approaches, switching shared resources between approaches over time, letting each unit choose for itself and sourcing approaches from outside specialists in an ecosystem. An earlier BCG article by Reeves and Haanaes gave Haier, with some 2,000 self-governing units, as the self-organizing case and Apple as the ecosystem case. The leader animates this mix through eight roles; Reeves highlighted diagnosing which approach fits where, segmenting the organization, disrupting an approach that no longer fits, coaching and deploying people, and asking the right questions.

*Why it evolves:* AI agents may take on routine work in some units and not others. That makes segmenting and switching more frequent decisions for leaders.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book's central move, diagnosing the environment before picking an approach, suits a period when AI is changing how predictable and shapeable many markets are. A firm that was safely classical may now face adaptive or shaping conditions in parts of its business.

Four concepts transcend: the diagnostic itself, the adaptive loop of vary, select and scale, shaping through ecosystems, and renewal as a bridge back to growth.

Three evolve. Classical planning gains from better forecasting tools but risks the misplaced confidence a 2012 BCG survey found. Visionary bets may pay off for shorter periods. And leaders running several approaches will face more frequent calls about where each one applies.

The trap to watch is the one the 2012 survey named: executives consistently overestimated how predictable and malleable their environments were. Fluent AI forecasts can strengthen that bias. The AI reading here is ours, not the authors'.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Diagnose your environment

Find which of the five approaches fits a business or unit.

```text
In "Your Strategy Needs a Strategy," Martin Reeves, Knut Haanaes and Janmejaya Sinha ask three questions about a business environment: how predictable is it (can I plan it?), how malleable is it (can I shape it?) and how harsh is it (can I survive it?). The answers point to one of five approaches: classical (be big), adaptive (be fast), visionary (be first), shaping (be the orchestrator) or renewal (be viable). A 2012 BCG survey by one of the authors found that executives tend to overestimate how predictable and malleable their environment is.

Ask me four questions about my market, how fast it changes, how much influence we have over it and our financial position. Then rate predictability, malleability and harshness as low, medium or high with a reason for each, name the approach that fits, and point out where I may be overrating predictability or control.

[paste a description of your business or unit here]
```

### 2. Design an adaptive experiment loop

Turn a plan into a vary, select and scale cycle.

```text
In "Your Strategy Needs a Strategy," the adaptive approach fits environments that are neither predictable nor shapeable. Advantage is serial and temporary, so the firm runs a loop: vary (try several options at once), select (keep what works, using clear tests) and scale (expand the winners quickly). Plans become rough hypotheses, and a culture that punishes failure blocks the loop.

Ask me three questions about my goal, my budget for experiments and how quickly I can get results. Then propose four small experiments, the measure and deadline that decides each one, the rule for scaling a winner, and one cultural obstacle I should remove first.

[paste your current plan or initiative here]
```

### 3. Map your strategy palette

See which approach each part of your organization needs.

```text
"Your Strategy Needs a Strategy" argues that large organizations face several environments at once and must run different approaches in different units, either together or in sequence. Options include separating units, switching shared resources between approaches over time, letting small units choose their own approach, and relying on outside partners. Leaders diagnose which approach fits where, segment the organization, disrupt approaches that no longer fit, and coach people for the approach they work in.

Ask me three questions about my organization's units, markets and how they are run today. Then list each unit with the approach it uses now, the approach its environment calls for and the gap, and recommend which of the four combining options suits us.

[paste a list of your units or business lines here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing for *Business Standard*, Alokananda Chakraborty found the first five chapters thought-provoking but the later ones a potpourri, some helpful and some not. She said the authors leaned on hackneyed examples such as Toyota, Novo Nordisk, Pepsi and Apple, over-emphasized single-leader organizations, and gave only a small part to leadership skills such as inspiring people and developing talent. In *Dialogue Review*, Piers Cain of the Chartered Management Institute was won over, finding the archetypes clearly explained, but noted that the mathematical modelling behind the reasoning has to be more or less taken on trust, though it seemed plausible. Our own caveats: the evidence comes mostly from the authors' firm, and real environments rarely sort cleanly into five boxes.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead a business or unit and suspect your planning process no longer fits your market, or you run a portfolio of businesses that face very different conditions. **Skip it** if you want only the framework; the three questions, five approaches and the BCG strategy palette pages cover the core, and the 2012 *Harvard Business Review* article gives an earlier version in a few pages.
