## At a glance

**Formal planning can program a strategy that already exists, but it cannot create one, because strategy comes from synthesis and planning is built on analysis.**

Henry Mintzberg · 1994 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

From the mid-1960s, strategic planning spread through large companies and governments on a simple promise: break strategy making into formal steps, staff it with planners, and good strategies will come out the other end. Igor Ansoff's *Corporate Strategy* (1965) gave the movement much of its early momentum. By the early 1980s the best-known planning system in America, at General Electric, had been scuttled when Jack Welch became chief executive.

Henry Mintzberg, a management professor at McGill University and a former president of the Strategic Management Society, set out to explain what went wrong. His answer is that planning was never strategy making in the first place. It was strategic programming: the codifying and elaborating of strategies that managers already had. Planning rearranges established categories, he argues, while real strategic change requires inventing new ones.

The book runs over 450 pages, with more than 25 pages of references. It traces planning's rise and its pitfalls, then takes apart the assumptions underneath it, and ends by proposing new roles for planning, plans and planners. Mintzberg condensed the argument into two 1994 articles in *Long Range Planning* and one in *Harvard Business Review*.

## Key concepts

### 1. The grand fallacy: analysis is not synthesis · Transcends

Mintzberg's central claim is that analysis breaks a problem into parts, while strategy requires putting things together into a new whole. Planners assumed that analysis encompasses synthesis, so a formal sequence of analytic steps would produce the whole. That is the grand fallacy. In his HBR summary of the argument, he states it flatly: "Planning cannot generate strategies." Strategies come from managers who synthesize what they learn, often informally. In the *Long Range Planning* version, he concludes that "the term 'strategic planning' has proved itself to be an oxymoron."

*Why it transcends:* AI makes analysis close to free, which makes the distinction sharper, not weaker. A tool that produces a hundred well-reasoned analyses still leaves someone to decide what they add up to.

### 2. The fallacy of prediction · Transcends

Formal planning assumes the world will hold still, or follow a predictable course, while the plan is made and carried out. Mintzberg argues that the events that matter most, discontinuities such as a technological breakthrough, are the ones that can't be forecast. He singles out Ansoff's "planning horizon," defined in 1965 as the period a firm can forecast to within about 20 percent, and calls the claim extraordinary.

*Why it transcends:* Forecasting models have improved enormously, but they still learn from what has already happened. The breaks that reshape a market remain the hardest thing to see coming.

### 3. The fallacy of detachment · Evolves

Planning separated thinkers from doers. Senior managers were supposed to make strategy at a distance, from aggregated "hard" data supplied by planners. Mintzberg argues that hard data arrives late, is often thin, and is aggregated until the important detail disappears. The most effective managers, he observes, rely on soft information, down to gossip and hearsay. Strategies also form far from headquarters, as when a salesperson's experiment with one customer grows into a new market.

*Why it evolves:* AI delivers data faster and in finer grain, and can summarize soft sources such as call notes. That narrows the gap but does not close it: a summary of the front line is not being there.

### 4. The fallacy of formalization · Evolves

The third assumption was that the strategy process itself could be formalized into procedures. Mintzberg argues that formal systems can process more information, at least hard information, but cannot internalize, comprehend or synthesize it. Formal planning tends to produce incremental, generic or copied strategies, and its ties to budgeting pull it toward the short term. In a literal sense, he concludes, planning never learned.

*Why it evolves:* AI is the most powerful formalization tool yet, and more adaptable than a fixed procedure. The risk shifts rather than disappears: a well-structured process can still stand in for asking whether the categories are right.

### 5. Deliberate and emergent strategy · Transcends

Mintzberg distinguishes strategies that are intended and then carried out (deliberate) from patterns that form through action and learning without anyone planning them (emergent). Viable strategies usually have both qualities: a purely deliberate strategy signals an unwillingness to learn, a purely emergent one an unwillingness to think ahead. And strategy can grow from anywhere in the organization.

*Why it transcends:* When experiments are cheap and fast, more strategy will emerge from what teams try and what customers respond to. Spotting which of those patterns to back is a leadership skill AI supports but does not replace.

### 6. Planning as strategic programming · Evolves

The book's reconstruction gives planning a narrower, real job. Once a strategy exists, planning can program it in three steps: codify it clearly enough to act on, elaborate it into substrategies and action plans, and convert it into budgets, objectives and procedures. Plans also coordinate people inside the organization and inform outsiders such as financiers and suppliers. Programming suits stable settings that need tight coordination, such as airline scheduling, and can stifle learning in fast-changing ones.

*Why it evolves:* Codifying, elaborating and converting are tasks AI handles quickly. The job stays; the time and staff it takes shrink sharply.

### 7. Planners as finders, analysts and catalysts · Expires

Mintzberg proposes that planners work around strategy making rather than inside it. As finders, they look for emerging strategies in the organization's own activity and in competitors'. As analysts, they run quick studies and hand managers the results as inputs to weigh, posing the right questions more than supplying answers. As catalysts, they challenge conventional assumptions and provoke strategic thinking. He describes two types: "right-handed" planners who are analytic and suit large, stable bureaucracies, and "left-handed" planners who are creative and suit project-based and professional organizations.

*Why it expires:* The functions hold up. A specialist planning staff split into two types looks dated, since much of the finding and analysis can now sit with managers and their tools.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

Written for an age of planning departments, this book reads as a warning for an age of AI tools. The AI reading here is ours, not Mintzberg's.

His core distinction, between analysis and synthesis, gets sharper when analysis costs almost nothing. A team can now produce market sizes and competitor profiles in an afternoon. Mintzberg's question is what comes next: who turns all that into a strategy, and are they close enough to the work to know what the numbers leave out?

What changes is the programming side. The tasks he assigned to planning, codifying a strategy, breaking it into plans and turning it into budgets, are exactly the kind of structured work AI does well. That makes planning faster, and raises the risk he warned about: a polished, formal process that feels like strategy without producing any.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend, 3 evolve and 1 expires.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Separate the analysis from the strategy

Check whether your strategy document actually contains a strategy, or only analysis.

```text
Henry Mintzberg argued that analysis breaks things into parts while strategy requires synthesis: combining what you learn into a new direction. Planning, he said, can program a strategy that already exists but cannot create one.

Ask me 3 questions about my organization and what this document is meant to decide. Then go through the document and sort its contents into three groups: analysis (facts, forecasts, breakdowns), programming (budgets, targets, action plans), and actual strategic choices. Tell me plainly whether a real strategy is in there, and if not, what choice the analysis is circling around without making.

[paste your strategy or planning document here]
```

### 2. Find the strategy that is already emerging

Look for patterns in what your team is actually doing that might be a better strategy than the official one.

```text
Henry Mintzberg distinguished deliberate strategies (intended, then carried out) from emergent ones (patterns that form through action and learning). He argued that one of a planner's useful jobs is to find emerging strategies in the organization's own activity and in competitors'.

Ask me 4 questions about what my team has tried in the past year, what worked unexpectedly, and where customers surprised us. Then identify up to three emerging patterns, say which ones conflict with our stated strategy, and suggest which is worth backing deliberately and how we would test it.

[paste notes on recent projects, experiments or customer feedback here]
```

### 3. Stress-test a forecast

Check how much a plan depends on the future behaving like the past.

```text
Henry Mintzberg warned that formal plans assume the world will hold still or follow a predictable course, while the changes that matter most, such as technological breakthroughs, can't be forecast.

Ask me 3 questions about my market and how this plan was built. Then list the assumptions in the plan that depend on recent trends continuing, name two or three plausible discontinuities that would break them, and suggest what we could watch for early and how the plan could stay flexible enough to respond.

[paste your plan or forecast here]
```

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my strategy thinking partner, grounded in Henry Mintzberg's "The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning." Core ideas: analysis is not synthesis, so formal planning cannot create strategy; discontinuities can't be forecast; strategists need soft, close-up information, not only aggregated data; strategies can emerge from action as well as be planned; planning's real job is to program strategies once they exist.
```

## Critiques and caveats

Writing in the *Financial Times*, Christopher Lorenz suggested the title should be reversed to the fall and rise of planning, and saw a mellowing Mintzberg. Andrew Campbell, co-author of *Corporate-Level Strategy*, defended planning as worthwhile when the corporate center has a value-creating strategy and processes to carry it out. Igor Ansoff, whose planning ideas the book challenges, had already answered Mintzberg's 1990 article on the design school in the *Strategic Management Journal* in 1991, judging it unsound in method and fact and concluding that Mintzberg's own model held only in a limited, if important, context. Mintzberg replied the same year with "Learning 1, Planning 0." A 1999 study of 656 firms by Peter Brews and Michelle Hunt found that formal planning and incremental learning both belong to good planning, especially in unstable environments. Reviewing it in 2018, Daniel Good found it still relevant but dense. Our caveat: the book's case rests largely on planning at large corporations and governments of the 1960s to 1980s.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run or sit inside a planning process, teach strategy, or want the full case for why formal planning so often disappoints. **Skip it** if you want a quick read; Mintzberg's 1994 HBR article "The Fall and Rise of Strategic Planning" carries the core argument in a few pages (HBR reversed his title, Mintzberg notes, without his consent).
