## At a glance

**Some of the world's strongest companies are midsize firms nobody has heard of, and they win by owning a narrow market worldwide, staying close to customers and relying on their own strengths.**

Hermann Simon · 1996 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Hermann Simon taught business at the universities of Bielefeld and Mainz and founded the pricing consultancy Simon-Kucher & Partners in 1985, becoming its CEO in 1995. He has written that the research began with a question Harvard's Ted Levitt asked in 1987: why are German companies so successful at exporting? Simon's answer was not Siemens or Mercedes-Benz but quiet midsize firms. He first used the term "hidden champions" in a German journal in 1990, and his 1992 *Harvard Business Review* article, "Lessons from Germany's Midsize Giants," named firms such as Krones, Körber/Hauni, Weinig, Webasto and TetraWerke.

The 1996 book, published by Harvard Business School Press, draws on about 500 such companies, mostly German. In a German summary of the study that year, Simon wrote that the project had visited more than a hundred of them, set out the findings as three circles and nine lessons, and concluded there is no secret formula, only common sense. The 1996 criteria, as the CEEMAN research network later quoted them, were first or second in the world market and first in Europe, small or midsize with sales normally under $1 billion, and a low public profile. The sequels moved the bar: the 2009 *Hidden Champions of the 21st Century* covers more than a thousand firms with revenues under $4 billion, and by 2012 Simon counted the top three in a world market, with revenues under 5 billion euros.

## Key concepts

### 1. Aim for leadership in a market you define · Transcends

Simon's first lesson is that hidden champions want market leadership and nothing less, and some say they want to be the psychological leader of their market, not just the largest. The second lesson explains how a small firm gets there: define the market narrowly, and treat that definition as part of the strategy rather than a given. Many of these firms created the markets they lead. The payoff can be dominance large companies rarely reach. Of the German midsize leaders he studied, Simon wrote in 1992: "They have world market shares in the range of 70% to 90%."

*Why it transcends:* Choosing a market you can own outright is a timeless move, and it matters more when general-purpose AI tools make broad, shallow offerings easy to copy.

### 2. Narrow focus, global reach · Evolves

A narrow niche is small in any one country, so the champions pair deep product focus with selling everywhere, through their own subsidiaries rather than middlemen. In his 1996 German article, Simon reported that nearly all of the firms had their own company in the United States, more than 80 percent had subsidiaries in Britain, France and Italy, and more than half had one in Japan.

*Why it evolves:* The logic holds, but AI help with translation, support and sales is cutting the cost of reaching foreign customers. Global reach may no longer require a subsidiary in every major market.

### 3. Close to the customer, selling value not price · Transcends

The champions stay very close to their customers, especially the most demanding ones, yet Simon-Kucher's description of the book notes they have no large marketing departments. Julian Schenkenhofer's 2022 literature review argues the bond rests on mutual dependence, since narrow markets offer few substitutes, more than on physical nearness. The firms sell on value rather than price, compete on quality and service, and often sit in the same region as their strongest rivals.

*Why it transcends:* Knowing a small group of customers better than anyone else is hard to automate and hard to copy, whatever tools rivals buy.

### 4. Continuous innovation from market and technology · Evolves

Simon-Kucher's summary calls the champions great innovators. Simon's 1996 article stresses steady improvement of both products and processes, with market and technology as equal drivers. A later figure comes from the 2009 sequel: Simon reported that the firms patent far more per employee than Germany's largest listed companies, a measure Schenkenhofer questions because patent counts are not a reliable gauge of innovation.

*Why it evolves:* AI can speed up design and testing in many niches. It also raises the risk that a niche technology gets displaced, a danger Simon illustrated in 2009 with a slide projector maker that kept building fine projectors as digital projection took its market.

### 5. Rely on your own strengths · Evolves

The seventh lesson is self-reliance. Simon-Kucher's description says the champions "avoid outsourcing, diversification, and strategic alliances, instead creating unmatchable internal competencies." They keep core know-how inside; in Simon's 1996 article, one firm reported that about a tenth of its workforce built its own machines. A 2009 Knowledge at Wharton report on the sequel cites wind turbine maker Enercon, which made 80 percent of its equipment in-house against 20 percent at other wind power firms.

*Why it evolves:* Depth still protects know-how, but few firms can build their own AI. Deciding what to keep inside and what to rent from model providers is the new version of this choice.

### 6. More work than heads · Evolves

Simon's eighth lesson is to keep more work than people. The champions hire selectively and keep everyone fully occupied. Simon-Kucher's summary says employee turnover is almost nonexistent, and Simon's 1996 article reported sickness rates typically below 4 percent.

*Why it evolves:* Small teams with heavy workloads are where AI assistance pays off quickly. The lesson shifts from stretching people to deciding which work people should keep.

### 7. Leaders who stay · Transcends

The ninth lesson concerns leadership. The heads of hidden champions identify personally with their firms, lead in an authoritarian way on core values and a participative way on details, and stay a very long time: more than two decades on average (22 years in Simon's 1996 German article; Simon-Kucher's description of the book gives 24).

*Why it transcends:* Long, committed leadership lets a firm pursue a niche for decades. That patience counts for more when technology cycles are short.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Hidden Champions* is unusual on this list: a book about firms that win by being small, specialized and patient. Its central bet is that a company can own a narrow world market through focus, closeness to customers and depth no rival can match. Those advantages look stronger when broad capabilities become cheap. If any competitor can use the same AI tools, what remains scarce is deep knowledge of a few demanding customers, proprietary processes and decades of accumulated craft.

Three concepts transcend: choosing a market you can lead, closeness to customers, and long, committed leadership. Four evolve. Global reach gets cheaper. Innovation speeds up, but so does the risk of a niche being made obsolete. Self-reliance meets a world where few can build their own AI. And "more work than heads" becomes a question of which work to give to people.

The main risk is the one the champions' strength creates: dependence on one product and one market. Simon's own slide projector example shows how a focused leader can lose its market to a new technology. The AI reading here is ours, not Simon's.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing the German edition, *Die heimlichen Gewinner*, in *Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft* in 1998, Elisabeth Beer doubted the firms were really hidden, at least from an Austrian view, and said she could not share the author's big moment of discovery, though she found the book useful for practitioners. Julian Schenkenhofer's 2022 review in *Management Review Quarterly* notes that world market leadership is self-assessed by the companies, market shares are at best estimates, niches are sometimes defined too narrowly, and the low-awareness criterion has never been measured. He flags survivorship bias, since firms that went insolvent or were acquired drop out of the samples, and reports a debate, in work by Bernd Venohr and Klaus E. Meyer (2007) and later authors, over whether "hidden champions" is a meaningful category. Our caveat: like many success studies, the book learns from winners, mostly from one country in one era.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run or advise a specialized small or midsize company, especially an industrial one, and want a detailed picture of how niche leaders think about markets, customers and people. **Skip it** if you want current data; read *Hidden Champions of the 21st Century* (2009) for the updated research, or Simon's 1992 *Harvard Business Review* article for the core argument in a few pages.
