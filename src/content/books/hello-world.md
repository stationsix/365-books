## At a glance

**Algorithms are neither saviors nor villains: they are flawed tools built and used by flawed people, and the best results come when humans and machines cover each other's weaknesses.**

Hannah Fry · 2018 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Hannah Fry is a British mathematician. She took her degree and a PhD in fluid dynamics at University College London, then joined UCL's Centre for Advanced Spatial Analysis in 2012, rising to professor in the Mathematics of Cities. In 2018 interviews she traced her interest in the ethics of algorithms to a talk in Berlin, where an audience told her that her work with police modeling the 2011 London riots could be used to suppress peaceful protest.

The book came out in September 2018, from Doubleday in the UK and W. W. Norton in the US, and was shortlisted for the Baillie Gifford Prize and the Royal Society Science Book Prize. After a note on the title and an introduction, it runs through seven chapters: Power, Data, Justice, Medicine, Cars, Crime and Art. Each asks what the algorithm can do, where it fails, and whether it beats the human it replaces.

## Key concepts

### 1. Overtrust and the power we hand over · Transcends

The book opens its Power chapter with a chess match. In May 1997, IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov 3½ to 2½. Fry's reading is that the machine won partly through psychology: its engineers had it hold off announcing moves it had already chosen, and Kasparov began second-guessing it. The chapter sets this beside a driver who followed his satnav so faithfully he nearly went over a cliff. Fry finds people at once dismissive of algorithms, intimidated by them and in awe of them. "We have a tendency to overtrust anything we don't understand."

*Why it transcends:* People still swing between blind faith and total dismissal with AI, often after a single impressive answer or a single error.

### 2. Your data is the price · Evolves

The Data chapter follows what happens to personal information. Data brokers gather and sell personal information with little regulation or disclosure. The retailer Target inferred from a teenager's shopping that she was pregnant and sent baby coupons before she had told her father. The chapter also explains the Cambridge Analytica scandal. Fry sets the detailed data that companies sell against the mess of public records such as the NHS's.

*Why it evolves:* Personal data now also trains AI models, and assistants that read your email and files gather far more context than a shopping basket.

### 3. Consistency against individual justice · Transcends

The Justice chapter weighs judges against risk scores. A 2017 study of New York City bail decisions estimated that an algorithm could have jailed 41.8 percent fewer defendants before trial without raising crime. Yet every tool makes two kinds of mistake, which the criminologist Richard Berk frames as releasing a Darth Vader or jailing a Luke Skywalker. In Wisconsin in 2013, a COMPAS score helped persuade a judge to reject Paul Zilly's plea deal for stealing a lawnmower and double his sentence. In Virginia, a risk score counted 19-year-old Christopher Drew Brooks's youth against him and raised his recommended sentence. In a 2018 National Geographic interview she also cited ProPublica's finding that such a tool wrongly labeled Black defendants high risk more often than white ones.

*Why it transcends:* The trade between consistent rules and judgment of the individual case is the central question for any AI that decides about people.

### 4. Machines that see, people who decide · Evolves

The Medicine chapter finds more to welcome. Even pigeons can be trained to spot cancerous cells in breast tissue, and neural networks learn to find cancer in medical images. In one contest with pathologists, an algorithm caught 92.4 percent of cancers on slides but produced many false positives. Fry's conclusion is a partnership: the machine screens tirelessly and the doctor makes the call. In a 2018 National Geographic interview Fry was warier of diagnostic chatbots such as Babylon Health, which she said still made many mistakes.

*Why it evolves:* Medical AI has moved from reading images toward answering patients' questions directly, the open-ended job the book thought hardest.

### 5. Driving is a problem of uncertainty · Evolves

The Cars chapter traces driverless cars from a 1939 World's Fair exhibit through the DARPA Grand Challenge of 2004, when no vehicle finished, to 2005, when five completed the course. Fry explains the Bayesian reasoning a car needs to make sense of an uncertain world. She poses the trolley problem: should a car protect its passenger or the pedestrians? In a 2018 National Geographic interview she said such dilemmas show that some questions have no single right answer, and doubted that driverless cars would ever share the road with all the disorder of ordinary traffic.

*Why it evolves:* Driverless taxis now carry paying passengers in some cities, but mostly in mapped, limited areas, which supports her caution more than it refutes it.

### 6. Predicting where, not just who · Evolves

The Crime chapter turns to policing. Kent police used PredPol software to forecast where crimes were likely, and Durham police used an AI tool estimating whether suspects would reoffend. *The Times* judged the crime and justice chapters the book's best. Facial recognition gets harder scrutiny. In Denver, the financial adviser Steve Talley was arrested after software wrongly matched him to two bank robberies. On *Science Friday* in 2018 she warned that systems trained on historical arrest records encode old biases.

*Why it evolves:* Face matching and pattern-finding are far more accurate and widespread than in 2018, so the question has shifted from whether they work to who checks them.

### 7. Can a machine be creative? · Transcends

The Art chapter asks what algorithms can tell us about taste. It describes an experiment with an artificial music market that tested how strongly social influence decides which songs become hits. It then turns to programs that compose music in the style of Bach. The chapter's question is whether a machine can be creative, and what listeners value when they judge a piece of art.

*Why it transcends:* With AI now writing, painting and composing on demand, the question of what human authorship adds has moved from a seminar topic to a daily one.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Hello World* predates large language models, and most of its cases are scoring, sorting and recognition systems. It holds up because its method fits any AI: ask what the system does well, how it fails, who bears the errors and what the human alternative really is. Fry described her own book in a 2018 National Geographic interview: "this isn't a book about algorithms; this is a book about humans."

Its balance is the point of including it beside *Weapons of Math Destruction*. O'Neil's book indicts opaque models; Fry's weighs each system against flawed human judges and doctors. Both call for regulation, and in a 2018 Nautilus interview Fry proposed something like an FDA for algorithms, with experts weighing each system's benefits against its harms.

Fry has stayed close to the field. She has hosted the Google DeepMind podcast since 2019. In a 2021 Raconteur interview she dismissed some AI products, such as a tool claiming to read pain from faces, as bad science, and pointed to narrow uses such as DeepMind's rainfall forecasting. Introducing her BBC Two series *AI Confidential* in February 2026, she told BBC Science Focus that using AI as a tutor extends her abilities, but warned that chatbots used as therapists can reinforce what users already believe, since they are not designed to tell people what they don't want to hear.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve.

### 1. Judge the system against the human

Decide whether an algorithm should make, assist or stay out of a decision.

```text
In "Hello World," Hannah Fry argues that an algorithm should be judged against the real humans it replaces, not against perfection: people are inconsistent and biased, machines make different mistakes, and control should go to whichever side proves it predicts better, with humans keeping the final say where stakes are high.

Ask me four questions about the decision, who makes it today and how errors are caught. Then compare the current human process with the proposed system on accuracy, consistency and kinds of error, and recommend whether the system should decide, assist or stay out.

[paste a description of the decision and the proposed system here]
```

### 2. Weigh the two kinds of error

Choose which mistake you can live with before you set a threshold.

```text
"Hello World" shows that every predictive tool makes two kinds of mistake: false negatives, such as releasing a dangerous defendant, and false positives, such as jailing a harmless one. Reducing one usually increases the other, and the errors can fall unevenly across groups.

Ask me three questions about what the tool predicts and who is affected. Then describe each type of error in my setting, who pays for it, how to check whether errors differ by group, and what threshold and review process would be fair.

[paste a description of the prediction and its uses here]
```

### 3. Design against overtrust

Build in checks for the moment people stop questioning the machine.

```text
Hannah Fry argues in "Hello World" that people overtrust what they don't understand, yet dismiss a machine after one error, so systems should be designed for how people really behave: show options and confidence rather than one answer, keep humans meaningfully in the loop, and make it easy to challenge and correct mistakes.

Ask me four questions about who uses the tool and how they act on its output. Then list where users are likely to overtrust it, the design changes that would keep them thinking, and a simple process for appeals and corrections.

[paste a description of the tool and its users here]
```

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

You are my algorithm-sense adviser, grounded in Hannah Fry's "Hello World." ## Critiques and caveats

Oliver Moody in *The Times* called the book stylish and scrupulously fair-minded, but at barely 200 pages wished it had said more about politics, advertising and social media. In *Physics World*, Anna Demming found it less an information-dense text than an anthology of entertaining anecdotes, weighted toward algorithms failing rather than succeeding. Readers wanting firm answers may find the balance frustrating, since several chapters raise hard questions, such as the trolley problem, and leave them open. Many specifics describe 2018 technology.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want a clear, funny and fair introduction to how algorithms make decisions about people, and what they get right as well as wrong. **Skip it** if you already know the cases or want today's AI: *Weapons of Math Destruction* is the sharper warning, and *Co-Intelligence* covers working with large language models.
