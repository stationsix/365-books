## At a glance

**Product teams decide better when the people building the product talk to customers every week, work toward a clear outcome, and test the assumptions behind their ideas instead of debating them.**

Teresa Torres · 2021 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Teresa Torres studied symbolic systems at Stanford and later earned a master's in learning and organizational change at Northwestern. She held product and design roles at HighWire Press and Become.com, was CEO of Affinity Circles, an online community provider for university alumni associations, and led product and design at AfterCollege. Burned out after several early-stage startups, she turned to coaching product teams, then to teaching through the Product Talk Academy.

The book, published in 2021 by her own company, Product Talk, distills about eight years of that coaching into what she calls a product trio's guide to a structured and sustainable approach to discovery. It is meant to help teams bring customer input into their daily product decisions. Part one defines continuous discovery and sets out a common framework. Part two works through the habits: outcomes, interviewing, mapping opportunities, generating solutions, testing assumptions and measuring impact. Part three is about starting small. Chapters come with exercises and anti-patterns, and footnotes point to the research behind them.

## Key concepts

### 1. Weekly touchpoints, by the trio · Transcends

The book defines continuous discovery as "weekly touch points with customers by the team building the product," running small research activities toward a desired outcome. Two parts of that matter. First, the cadence: weekly, not a big study before each project. Second, who does it: the product trio, typically a product manager, a designer and a software engineer, who together decide what the wider team builds. The trio interviews customers together rather than relying on someone else's research summary. The goal is that the people making product decisions have recent customer input whenever a decision comes up.

*Why it transcends:* AI can summarize what customers said, but a trio that hears customers every week builds judgment no summary hands over.

### 2. Outcomes over outputs · Transcends

The third chapter starts discovery with an outcome rather than a feature list. Torres separates business outcomes, company results such as revenue or retention, from product outcomes, customer behaviors a team can directly influence that drive business results. Traction metrics, usage of one feature, are a narrower third kind. Outputs, the features and projects on a roadmap, are not outcomes. The outcome should come from a two-way negotiation between leaders and the trio, not simply handed down, and the chapter lists anti-patterns such as pursuing too many outcomes at once or treating outputs as outcomes.

*Why it transcends:* When AI makes shipping features nearly free, counting features delivered says even less about whether anything improved.

### 3. The opportunity solution tree · Evolves

The book's central tool is a visual map Torres first described on her blog in 2016. The desired outcome sits at the root. Below it are opportunities: customer needs, pain points and desires, framed from the customer's side and found through research. Solutions hang off the opportunities they address, and assumption tests hang off the solutions. The tree keeps the trio aligned, makes it obvious when a pet idea connects to no real customer need, and gives stakeholders a way to see the team's reasoning. The book adds rules for structuring it, such as framing each opportunity as a customer need, not a solution in disguise.

*Why it evolves:* AI can draft a tree from interview notes in seconds, which is the catch. The thinking happens in building the map, so a generated tree risks skipping it.

### 4. Story-based interviewing · Transcends

The fifth chapter covers continuous interviewing. People are poor at describing what they generally do or predicting what they will do, so the book has teams collect specific stories about past behavior: tell me about the last time you did this, what happened first, what happened next. The interviewer steers back to the specific instance whenever the participant drifts into generalities. After each interview the trio fills in a one-page interview snapshot capturing who it was, a memorable quote, the opportunities heard and other insights. To make the weekly cadence realistic, the book has teams automate recruiting so that interviewing is easier than not interviewing.

*Why it transcends:* Synthetic customers can answer questions, but they have no last time. A real person's specific story stays the raw material.

### 5. Compare and contrast, not whether or not · Transcends

A theme across the book is to stop asking whether one idea is good. Torres has argued since a 2017 Product Talk post that "good" is a relative trait, so asking whether one idea is good is the weaker question. Asking which of several options looks best is the stronger one. The book applies this at each level of the tree: compare sibling opportunities on factors like sizing, market, company and customer fit, then generate several solutions for the chosen opportunity and test them side by side, typically three at a time.

*Why it transcends:* AI makes generating options cheap, which removes the excuse for falling in love with the first one.

### 6. Test assumptions, not ideas · Evolves

Rather than build a whole solution to see if it works, the book has teams break each idea into the assumptions it rests on: desirability, viability, feasibility, usability and ethical assumptions. Story mapping and pre-mortems help surface them. The team then picks the riskiest, least-supported ones and designs small, fast tests for those, setting in advance what result would count as passing. Tests should simulate the real experience and observe what people do, not ask them what they would do. Later chapters turn to measuring impact once something ships and to showing stakeholders the team's work.

*Why it evolves:* When an AI tool can build a working version in an afternoon, the line between testing an assumption and shipping the idea blurs. Choosing which assumption to test first still decides what a team learns.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Continuous Discovery Habits* was written for teams where building was the slow, costly part, so the method protects engineering time by testing ideas first. AI shifts that balance. When a prototype takes an hour, ceremonies built around scarce build time compress, and the scarce input becomes recent, specific evidence from real customers. That is the book's core, which is why the panel ranked it above *Sprint*.

What holds: weekly contact, outcomes, story-based interviews and comparing options. What changes: assumption tests and real releases converge, and AI can draft trees, snapshots and syntheses, which helps only if the team does the thinking first.

Torres has written about this directly. In a June 2024 Product Talk article, she argued that generative AI cannot replace interviewing real humans as long as products are built for real humans, warned that letting AI write the first draft lets it dominate the conversation, and said she uses AI daily but wants teams to work out what they think before asking it. That is her later view, not the book's.

Verdict: 4 of 6 key concepts transcend and 2 evolve.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Turn a feature request into an outcome

Find the product outcome a requested feature is supposed to move.

```text
In "Continuous Discovery Habits," Teresa Torres separates business outcomes (company results like revenue or retention), product outcomes (customer behaviors a product team can directly influence that lead to business results) and outputs (features and projects). Teams should start from a product outcome, agreed in a two-way negotiation with leaders, not from a feature list.

Ask me four questions about the feature, who asked for it and what problem they think it solves. Then name the business outcome behind it, propose two or three product outcomes my team could move, say which one you would pick and why, and list the anti-patterns I should watch for, such as treating the output as the outcome.

[paste the feature request and any context here]
```

### 2. Plan a story-based interview

Write an interview guide that collects specific past stories, not opinions.

```text
"Continuous Discovery Habits" says people are poor at describing what they generally do or predicting what they will do, so interviews should collect specific stories about past behavior: "Tell me about the last time you...", "What happened first?", "What happened next?" The interviewer steers back to the specific instance when the participant generalizes. After each interview, the team fills in a one-page snapshot: who it was, a memorable quote, opportunities heard (needs, pain points, desires) and insights.

Ask me three questions about my product, my desired outcome and who I can reach. Then write the opening story prompt, five follow-up prompts, three ways to steer back from generalities, and a blank snapshot template I can fill in after the call.

[paste a description of your product and customers here]
```

### 3. Find the riskiest assumptions

Break solution ideas into assumptions and design small tests for the riskiest ones.

```text
Teresa Torres's "Continuous Discovery Habits" says to test assumptions, not whole ideas. Break each solution into its desirability, viability, feasibility, usability and ethical assumptions; pick the ones that matter most and have the least evidence; then design small tests that simulate the experience and watch what people do, with the passing result set in advance. Compare several solutions at once rather than asking whether one idea is good.

Ask me three questions about the opportunity these ideas address and what we already know. Then list the key assumptions for each idea by type, rank the three riskiest overall, and for each propose a test we could run this week, what we would measure and what result would count as passing.

[paste two or three solution ideas here]
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my discovery coach, grounded in Teresa Torres's "Continuous Discovery Habits." Core ideas: the product trio (product manager, designer, engineer) should have weekly touchpoints with customers and run small research activities toward a desired outcome; start from a product outcome, not outputs; map the work on an opportunity solution tree (outcome, then customer opportunities, then solutions, then assumption tests); interview for specific past stories and capture each in a snapshot; compare and contrast several options instead of asking whether one is good; test the riskiest assumptions, not whole ideas.

When I bring you a plan or decision: ask which outcome it serves, which opportunity it addresses, when we last heard from a customer about it, what alternatives we compared and which assumption is riskiest. Push back when I jump to a solution or let you do the first draft of my thinking. Keep a running note in this project of our outcome, the opportunity solution tree, interview snapshots, assumption tests and what we learned.
```

## Critiques and caveats

Readers on Goodreads mostly rate the book highly, but critical reviewers call it repetitive or dry, say it gives familiar product practices new names, and find the opportunity solution tree impractical. One argues it assumes plenty of customers a team can readily interview each week, and offers little help in coming up with new product ideas. Our own caveats: the evidence is Torres's coaching experience plus research footnotes, not studies of teams using the method, and it assumes leaders who will assign outcomes rather than features. Next to *Inspired*, it is narrower but more hands-on.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you are on a product team, especially a product manager, designer or engineer in a trio, and want a weekly routine with exercises you can use right away. **Skip it** if you want the broader case for empowered product teams: *Inspired* covers that, and the concepts above carry this book's method.
