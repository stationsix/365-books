## At a glance

**Good ideas rarely arrive in a flash: they grow slowly out of parts that already exist, and they grow fastest in crowded, open, connected environments where half-formed hunches can meet each other.**

Steven Johnson · 2010 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Steven Johnson, born in 1968, writes about science, technology and the history of ideas. He co-founded the early web magazine FEED, has been a contributing editor at *Wired*, and wrote *Emergence* and *The Ghost Map* before this book. Riverhead published it in October 2010, the year of his TED talk on its themes.

The book attacks the myth of the lone genius struck by a sudden "Eureka" and asks what environments produce good ideas. It opens with a 27-year-old Charles Darwin at the Keeling Islands in the Indian Ocean, puzzled that the islands held a thin array of plants and animals while the reef waters around them teemed with species. The reef becomes his model for a creative environment, followed by the city and the web.

Seven chapters each take one pattern: the adjacent possible, liquid networks, the slow hunch, serendipity, error, exaptation and platforms. A closing chapter sorts history's major innovations into four quadrants to test where ideas really come from.

## Key concepts

### 1. The adjacent possible · Evolves

Johnson borrows the term from the scientist Stuart Kauffman. At any moment, only certain new things are reachable: those that can be built by combining parts, tools and ideas that already exist. Each new idea opens doors onto further ones, the way stepping into a room in a house reveals new doors. This explains why several people often make the same discovery at once, and why ideas that arrive too early fail. On NPR's *Science Friday* in 2010, Johnson gave an example of each: oxygen, isolated by three people at about the same time, and Charles Babbage's analytical engine, whose concepts had to be rediscovered a century later.

*Why it evolves:* AI tools widen the set of parts a small team can reach, so the adjacent possible grows faster, but the discipline of asking "what is one door away from us?" stays the same.

### 2. Liquid networks · Transcends

Ideas are networks of other ideas, so they need an environment where many people and many thoughts can mix without dissolving into chaos. Johnson calls this a liquid network, and he finds it in dense cities, universities and the web. In his TED talk he describes the English coffeehouse: a population that had been effectively drunk all day switched to a stimulant, and met to trade ideas across fields. The book's broader claim, as *Kirkus* summarized it, is that open flows of information produce ideas faster than closed or hierarchical ones.

*Why it transcends:* Teams now meet in chat channels more than coffeehouses, but the rule holds: diverse people, frequent contact, and enough structure for ideas to stick.

### 3. The slow hunch · Evolves

Most important ideas begin as a hunch that matures over months or years. In his TED talk Johnson points to Darwin's notebooks, where Darwin wrote down every small idea and hunch, which show his theory forming gradually; in a 2010 NPR interview he contrasted them with Darwin's own later account of a sudden insight. The book's advice is to keep hunches alive: write everything down and keep a commonplace book, the scrapbook of quotations and notes kept by earlier readers. Hunches often become breakthroughs only when they meet another person's hunch, which is why the book revisits the FBI's failure to act on one field agent's hunch before 9/11.

*Why it evolves:* Digital notes and AI search make old hunches far easier to resurface and connect, though the slow part, living with a question long enough, cannot be outsourced.

### 4. Serendipity and error · Transcends

Two patterns share a lesson: ideas need some disorder. Serendipity means a lucky collision between an idea and the missing piece it needs; among the book's examples is Kekulé's insight into the structure of benzene, and Johnson suggests that the web, used well, can multiply such encounters. Error does similar work. In *Publishers Weekly*'s account, Johnson argues that being right lets you stay put, while being wrong pushes you to explore.

*Why it transcends:* Algorithmic feeds can narrow what people stumble on, which makes deliberately seeking odd inputs and tolerating useful mistakes more valuable, not less.

### 5. Exaptation · Transcends

Exaptation is a term from evolutionary biology for a trait built for one purpose that gets borrowed for another. Johnson applies it to ideas. A central example is Gutenberg, who adapted the wine press, a mature technology from another trade, to build the printing press.

*Why it transcends:* Repurposing is how most practical AI value is being found, as general tools are borrowed into specific jobs, which is exaptation in plain sight.

### 6. Platforms, and the 10/10 rule · Evolves

Some innovations become platforms that others build on, just as coral builds a reef that supports thousands of species. Darwin's theory became a platform for later fields such as population genetics. The web, built on the internet, became a stack on which a few people could build YouTube in months. In the twentieth century, Johnson notes, major communications platforms moved at a steady pace he calls the 10/10 rule: a decade to build the platform and another for it to find a mass audience. The web, he argues, broke that pace.

*Why it evolves:* AI models now act as platforms in their own right, which shrinks the build decade further, while the question of who controls the platform matters more than ever.

### 7. The fourth quadrant · Evolves

The closing chapter tests the patterns against history. Johnson compiled a list of a few hundred key innovations since about 1400 and sorted them on two axes: market or non-market, and individual or networked. The familiar story of a lone inventor working for profit, such as Willis Carrier and air conditioning, is, he argues, the exception. Non-market networks, he finds, produced more world-changing ideas than markets did, and the fourth quadrant, networked and non-market, as in universities and open, shared research, grew over time.

*Why it evolves:* Much frontier AI research sits inside a few companies, while open research and openly released models keep the fourth quadrant alive.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

This is a book about environments, and AI changes the environment. An AI assistant can act as an always-open liquid network, offering parts from fields a reader would never visit, and it can search years of notes for the hunch that fits today's problem. That widens the adjacent possible and makes the commonplace book far more useful.

What holds is the human side. Slow hunches still need someone to carry a question for years, serendipity still depends on what you expose yourself to, and error is still how people learn.

Johnson has written about AI himself. He has worked at Google since 2022, first as a part-time visiting scholar, on an AI research and note-taking tool, and in a June 2025 newsletter essay, "Machine Readable," he argued that such tools help most when the writer chooses the sources, and that they can surface surprising links between his own drafts and new material. He described the tool as reading with him rather than for him. Our own test, drawn from the book: does a tool widen your connections, or replace them?

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve.

### 1. Map the adjacent possible

Find which new products or features are one step away from what you already have.

```text

Each new idea opens doors to further ones, ideas that arrive too early fail, and useful parts are often borrowed from other fields (exaptation, as when Gutenberg adapted the wine press).

Ask me four questions about my product, my customers, and the skills, data and tools my team already has. Then list the parts I already own, propose five ideas that are one door away from them, flag any idea that looks too early because a needed part does not exist yet, and suggest one part worth borrowing from an unrelated industry.

[paste a short description of your product and team here]
```

### 2. Review your slow hunches

Go back through old notes to find hunches worth reviving or connecting.

```text
Steven Johnson's "Where Good Ideas Come From" argues that important ideas begin as slow hunches that mature over years, that Darwin kept notebooks recording every small hunch, and that hunches become breakthroughs when they collide with another hunch or new information. He recommends writing everything down and rereading it, in the spirit of a commonplace book.

Ask me three questions about what I am working on now and the problems I keep returning to. Then read my notes below and group recurring hunches into themes, point out two pairs of notes that could connect into a stronger idea, name one hunch that now looks ready to test, and suggest who else might hold the missing half of it.

[paste a batch of your notes, ideas or journal entries here]
```

### 3. Design a liquid network for your team

Plan how ideas will flow across people and functions without turning into noise.

```text
In "Where Good Ideas Come From," Steven Johnson argues that good ideas need liquid networks: environments dense and diverse enough for ideas to mix, like the English coffeehouse or a university, yet stable enough for them to persist. Open flows of information beat closed or hierarchical ones, serendipity and error help break routine, and many breakthroughs come from networked, shared work rather than lone experts.

Ask me four questions about my team's size, functions, meeting rhythms and where information gets stuck. Then propose three changes that increase useful contact across functions, one way to make half-formed ideas visible to others, and one safeguard so the network stays focused instead of chaotic. Say how I would know within a month whether each change is working.

[paste a short description of your team and how it communicates here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Cosma Shalizi, in his *Bactra Review* (2010), called Johnson one of the best popular science writers and credited him for separating market from non-market innovation, but said the book never explains what makes an idea good, since the same social processes can spread bad ideas. He also questioned how Johnson chose and counted the innovations on his list, and said he should have drawn on econometric research into whether patents and other intellectual property actually spur innovation. Bruce Ramsey, in *The Seattle Times*, thought Johnson gave markets too little credit for turning new ideas into useful tools. *Publishers Weekly* found the anecdotes at times almost disjointed, though it felt the digressions paid off. Our caveat: the patterns describe helpful conditions, not a method.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead or design creative work, enjoy history and science told through stories, or want the ideas behind brainstorming and open innovation in their original, better-argued form. **Skip it** if you want a step-by-step innovation process: the concepts above carry its patterns, and Scott Berkun noted in his review that it leans more toward science than business.
