## At a glance

**No one can predict the future, so instead of betting on one forecast, build a few sharply different stories of how the world could unfold, test your decisions against each, and watch for the signs of which one is arriving.**

Peter Schwartz · 1991 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Peter Schwartz trained as an aeronautical engineer, joined the Stanford Research Institute in 1972 and went on to direct its Strategic Environment Center. From 1982 to 1986 he headed scenario planning for Royal Dutch/Shell in London, where Pierre Wack had pioneered the method. He then co-founded Global Business Network, a scenario planning firm, with Stewart Brand, Jay Ogilvy, Napier Collyns and others.

The book sets out the method Shell made famous. Under Wack, Shell's planners had sketched futures with supply shortages and much higher oil prices, which alerted its top managers to the 1973 crisis before it came. Schwartz argues that what matters most is intangible, such as hopes, fears and beliefs, and that only stories capture it.

In a later paperback's table of contents, the chapters run from a worked case (the Smith & Hawken story) through uncovering the decision, information-hunting, building blocks, a new driving force he calls the global teenager, composing a plot, three scenarios for the world in 2005, and rehearsing the future. An appendix sets out the steps, usually summarized as eight. The 1996 paperback added a user's guide.

## Key concepts

### 1. Scenarios, not forecasts · Transcends

A scenario, for Schwartz, is a tool for ordering your perceptions about the alternative futures in which today's decisions might play out. A team typically builds two or three rather than one forecast. The point is not to predict but to perceive possible futures in the present. Stories do this better than numbers because they help people cope with complexity and get past denial: a good scenario, like good theater, asks its audience to suspend disbelief long enough to take seriously a future they would otherwise dismiss.

*Why it transcends:* Forecasts of AI's effects vary widely and keep changing. A method that does not depend on getting the forecast right fits that problem well.

### 2. Start with the decision · Transcends

Schwartz begins not with the world but with a specific decision or focal issue, then builds outward to the environment. He also starts with the mindset of the people deciding, since mindsets keep us from seeing the right questions to ask. The next step lists the key factors in the immediate environment that will decide success or failure, such as customers, suppliers and competitors, and asks what the decision makers will want to know when they choose.

*Why it transcends:* Scenarios with no decision attached turn into speculation. Anchoring the work to a real choice keeps it useful in any era.

### 3. Information-hunting at the fringes · Evolves

Schwartz treats gathering information as a craft. The researcher looks for facts and perceptions that challenge the team's assumptions, and watches the fringes, because new knowledge tends to develop at the edges of society before it reaches the center. He also urges seeking out unorthodox thinkers, reading outside your specialty and traveling to unfamiliar places.

*Why it evolves:* AI tools can scan and summarize far more than any researcher. But they tend to report the mainstream view, so finding the fringe, and the unorthodox people in it, takes deliberate effort.

### 4. Driving forces, predetermined elements and critical uncertainties · Evolves

The building blocks start with driving forces, which Schwartz checks in five categories: society, technology, economics, politics and environment. Some are predetermined elements: slow-moving trends such as demographics and infrastructure, resource constraints, and collisions that are bound to happen. These appear in every scenario. Critical uncertainties turn up when the team questions its assumptions about what is predetermined. The forces are then ranked by importance to the focal issue and by how uncertain they are, and the two or three that score highest on both become the axes that separate the scenarios.

*Why it evolves:* For many businesses AI now belongs on the list, often as a critical uncertainty. An assistant can draft a long list in seconds; ranking it still takes judgment about your business.

### 5. Composing a plot · Evolves

Scenarios need plots, Schwartz argues, and a handful recur. Winners and losers assumes a world of scarce resources where conflict is inevitable. Challenge and response, a term he takes from the historian Arnold Toynbee, follows a system that is brought to the brink but does not fail. Evolution traces slow change, and technology, he writes, is the most common evolutionary plot. He lists others too, such as revolution and cycles. The aim is a few scenarios that cover the range that matters to the decision, each with a name that condenses the story into a few words.

*Why it evolves:* Generating vivid narratives is now cheap. The skill shifts to choosing logics that truly diverge and resisting stories that are polished but implausible.

### 6. Rehearsing the future and watching for signposts · Transcends

Once the scenarios exist, the work returns to the decision. How does it look in each future, what vulnerabilities appear, and is it robust across all of them? A strategy that looks good in only one scenario is a big gamble. Using scenarios, for Schwartz, is rehearsing the future. The last step picks a few leading indicators that show early which scenario is unfolding. Reviewing the book, Terry O'Keefe singled out a case from Schwartz's Shell years, when vast Soviet gas reserves hung over the market: his team identified a few obscure politicians, among them Mikhail Gorbachev, whose rise would signal economic cooperation with the West. When Gorbachev rose, Shell read it as a sign of change.

*Why it transcends:* Rehearsing a decision against several futures, then watching named signals, is as useful for an AI investment as it was for an oil field.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*The Art of the Long View* was written for a world of oil shocks and the end of the Cold War, but its central problem is one AI makes sharper: decisions that must be made now, about a future no one can forecast with confidence.

Three concepts transcend. Scenarios instead of forecasts, starting from a real decision, and rehearsing strategies against several futures while watching for signposts all hold up. They suit questions such as how fast AI will change a market or a job.

Three evolve. AI makes research, lists of driving forces and scenario narratives cheap, which moves the hard work to what the tools do less well: finding unorthodox views, ranking forces by what matters to one business, and choosing plots that truly diverge.

The risk is the easy version: a set of fluent scenarios nobody tests a decision against. The AI reading here is ours, not the book's.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Find your critical uncertainties

Sort the forces around a decision into what is already set and what could go either way.

```text
In "The Art of the Long View," Peter Schwartz builds scenarios from driving forces in five categories: society, technology, economics, politics and environment. Some forces are predetermined elements (slow trends such as demographics, resource constraints, collisions bound to happen) that appear in every scenario. Others are critical uncertainties. Forces are ranked by importance to the decision and by uncertainty, and the two or three highest on both become the axes of the scenarios.

Ask me three questions about the decision I face, my time horizon and what success would look like. Then list the driving forces in each of the five categories, mark each as predetermined or uncertain, rank them by importance and uncertainty, and propose the two that should define my scenarios.

[paste a description of your decision here]
```

### 2. Build a small set of scenarios

Turn two critical uncertainties into a few named stories of the future.

```text
Schwartz ("The Art of the Long View") argues that a few scenarios whose differences matter to the decision beat a single forecast. Each needs a plot, such as winners and losers, challenge and response, evolution, revolution or cycles, and a short name that captures the story. Predetermined elements appear in every scenario.

Ask me two questions about my organization and the people who will use these scenarios. Then write three or four scenarios built on the two uncertainties below, each with a name, a plot type, a one-paragraph story and the predetermined elements it shares with the others.

[paste your focal decision and two critical uncertainties here]
```

### 3. Rehearse a decision and pick signposts

Test a plan against each scenario and decide what to watch.

```text
In "The Art of the Long View," the last steps return to the decision: how does it look in each scenario, what vulnerabilities appear, and is it robust across all of them? A strategy that works in only one scenario is a gamble. Leading indicators then show early which scenario is unfolding.

Ask me three questions about my plan and what I can still change. Then rate the plan in each scenario, name its biggest vulnerability in each, suggest how to adapt it so it holds up across more of them, and list two leading indicators per scenario that I could track.

[paste your plan and your scenarios here]
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my scenario planning partner, grounded in Peter Schwartz's "The Art of the Long View." Core ideas: scenarios are stories about alternative futures, used to make better decisions now, not to predict; the work starts from a specific decision and the mindset of the people making it; good information comes from the fringes and from people whose thinking is unorthodox; driving forces in society, technology, economics, politics and environment split into predetermined elements and critical uncertainties, ranked by importance and uncertainty; a few scenarios with clear plots and names, rehearsed against the decision and tracked with leading indicators.

When I bring you a plan or decision: ask what decision it serves, challenge my assumptions about which forces are certain, test the plan against each of my scenarios, and point out when it only works in one. Keep a running note in this project of my focal issues, my scenarios and their names, and the leading indicators I am watching.
```

## Critiques and caveats

For McKinsey, Charles Roxburgh warned that scenarios can induce complacency, a sense of having all your bets covered, and that they are harder to conceptualize and build than they look. Shardul Phadnis and colleagues note that the scenario researcher Paul Schoemaker called the evidence for the method anecdotal; in their own study, using several scenarios raised field experts' support for more flexible investments, though most still chose the least flexible option. Writing on Shell, Benjamin Cabanes, Orso Roger and Liliana Doganova note that top management found the early scenarios hard to accept and that success depends on building scenarios into an organization's processes and routines. Our caveat: the book's 2005 scenarios and many examples have dated.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you set strategy over a horizon of five years or more, or want the stories behind the Shell method along with the steps. **Skip it** if you need a quick process; the eight steps and the prompts above carry most of the method.
