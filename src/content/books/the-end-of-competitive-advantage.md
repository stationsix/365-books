## At a glance

**Lasting competitive advantage is now the exception, so companies should plan for a series of short-lived advantages, launching new ones and leaving old ones on purpose, instead of defending a single position.**

Rita Gunther McGrath · 2013 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Rita Gunther McGrath is a professor at Columbia Business School, where she has taught since finishing her PhD at Wharton in 1993. Before academia she was an IT director, worked in politics and founded two start-ups. She is best known for discovery-driven planning, a method for managing new ventures under uncertainty that she set out with Ian C. MacMillan in a 1995 Harvard Business Review article.

The book takes aim at the central idea of classic strategy: that a firm should find a sustainable competitive advantage and defend it. McGrath argues that in more and more markets advantages wear out quickly, copied by rivals or made irrelevant by new technology and new kinds of competitors. Strategy built for stability then becomes a trap.

Her alternative is the transient advantage: plan for a portfolio of advantages, each with a limited life, and get good at moving from one to the next. The book is practical in tone, and each chapter closes with a "new strategy playbook" table of shifts from old practice to new. It moves from continuous reconfiguration to disengagement, resource allocation, innovation and leadership, and ends with what the same shift means for individual careers.

## Key concepts

### 1. Transient advantage · Transcends

McGrath's core claim is that sustainable advantage is no longer the normal state of affairs. Serious competition often comes from outside a firm's own industry, so a strong position may not last long. A firm has to keep several advantages going at once, some new, some at their peak and some fading, and treat strategy as a continuous process rather than a periodic plan. The task becomes capturing opportunities quickly, exploiting them decisively and moving on before they are exhausted, favoring flexibility over optimization.

*Why it transcends:* Software, data and now AI tools make many features and services easy to copy within months. Assuming an edge will fade is a safer default than assuming it will last.

### 2. Arenas, not industries · Evolves

Classic analysis starts with the industry. McGrath argues that industry lines are a poor guide when threats come from firms that look nothing like you, and proposes thinking in arenas instead, defined by the link between a customer segment, an offer and the location where it is delivered. Customers are grouped by the jobs they want done, and the question becomes which alternatives could do the same job, whatever industry they come from. The book likens the industry view to chess and the arena view to Go.

*Why it evolves:* The arena view fits a world where a general-purpose AI tool can compete with travel agents, tutors and search engines at once. But mapping arenas now means tracking capabilities that shift every few months, not just new entrants.

### 3. The wave of advantage · Transcends

Each transient advantage, in McGrath's model, follows a wave with recognizable stages. It is launched, ramped up, exploited for profit, reconfigured as it erodes, and finally disengaged from. Each stage calls for different handling, and talent should move fluidly between them rather than sit in fixed roles. Trouble starts when a company puts most of its weight on the exploitation stage, judging new ideas by the standards of mature businesses and staying too long with fading ones. McGrath asks for equal attention across the whole cycle.

*Why it transcends:* The stages give managers a shared language for asking where each product sits and whether it is being run the right way for that stage. That question applies to any portfolio, at any speed.

### 4. Healthy disengagement · Transcends

Most companies defend an advantage to the end, then exit in a dramatic downsizing or restructuring. McGrath argues for disengagement as a routine skill: end advantages on a regular basis, act on subjective early warnings rather than waiting for hard facts, exit steadily and learn from each exit instead of treating it as a failure. She sorts exits by how core the capability is and how much time pressure there is, with options from an orderly migration to a new offer to a run-off that keeps serving customers while investment shrinks. Freeing resources from old advantages is what funds the new ones.

*Why it transcends:* Every organization carries products and processes that outlived their value. AI speeds up how fast work becomes obsolete, which makes a calm, planned way to stop doing things more useful, not less.

### 5. Stability that enables agility · Evolves

To see what works, McGrath screened 4,793 public companies worth more than $1 billion for those that grew net income by at least 5 percent every year from 2000 to 2009. Only ten qualified, among them Infosys, HDFC Bank, Cognizant, Yahoo! Japan and Atmos Energy. These growth outliers had no dramatic downsizings or restructurings. They combined internal stability, in leadership ambition, strategic themes, values and customer relationships, with constant change in what they actually did. Infosys, for example, reorganizes every two or three years. In her account, stability and agility work together rather than against each other.

*Why it evolves:* The pairing of a stable core with fluid work suits teams adopting AI, where roles and tools shift fast. Which parts should stay fixed is a harder question when the tools reshape the work so quickly.

### 6. Resources and innovation as routine · Evolves

Two systems turn the idea into practice. First, resource allocation: when business units hold budgets and talent, new ventures starve. McGrath argues that key resources should be governed centrally and moved deliberately toward new opportunities, and that firms should shift from owning assets to having access to them, since an asset's competitive life is often shorter than its accounting life. Second, innovation should be a steady, managed proficiency with its own budget, governance and dedicated people, not an occasional burst. Here she draws on discovery-driven planning: test assumptions cheaply, learn quickly and invest more only as uncertainty falls.

*Why it evolves:* AI cuts the cost of early experiments, so more tests can run on less money. The harder part, moving budgets and people away from businesses that still make money, remains a leadership problem.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book's central warning, that edges fade faster than planning cycles assume, has only become more pressing as software and AI make products easier to copy and markets easier to enter.

Three concepts transcend. The transient-advantage mindset, the wave of stages and healthy disengagement are habits of mind that do not depend on any one technology.

Three evolve. Arenas, the stable core and central resource allocation all still hold, but AI changes the details. Competitors can now emerge from general-purpose tools rather than from adjacent industries. Experiments get cheaper while decisions about which business to retire stay just as hard.

The book is less helpful on where durable advantages still exist. Network effects, scale and proprietary data can still protect a position for years, which is why it pairs well with a book like *7 Powers*.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

George S. Day, reviewing the book in *Strategy & Leadership*, said questions about its research model abound: he asked whether the outliers' growth was organic or bought through acquisitions, and why one slower year should matter more than a ten-year growth rate. Reviewers at Mind Tools found the argument too black and white, said it ignores the downsides of working in such a transient way, and argued that maintaining an existing advantage matters just as much as seizing new ones.

Our own caveats: the screen picks companies by their results and then studies what they share, the method *The Halo Effect* warns about, and ten firms is a small sample. The book says little about how to tell a lasting advantage, such as network effects or scale, from a passing one. And moving resources centrally is easier to state than to carry out against the managers who run the profitable core.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you run a portfolio of products or business units and suspect your planning assumes more stability than your market offers. **Skip it** if you want a theory of durable advantage; *7 Powers* covers that, and McGrath's later *Seeing Around Corners* goes deeper on spotting change early.
