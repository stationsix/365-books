## At a glance

**The internet did not repeal economics: information goods cost a lot to make and little to copy, and the old laws of pricing, switching costs and network effects decide who wins.**

Carl Shapiro and Hal R. Varian · 1998 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Shapiro and Varian are economists at the University of California, Berkeley. Shapiro had served in Washington from 1995 to 1996 as the Justice Department's deputy assistant attorney general for economics in the antitrust division, and Varian was dean of Berkeley's School of Information Management and Systems and the author of a leading microeconomics textbook. Varian later became Google's chief economist.

They wrote the book during the dot-com boom, when many voices claimed the internet needed a new economics. The authors disagree. Their mantra is "Technology changes. Economic laws do not." Pricing, product differentiation, switching costs and network effects were all studied long before the web, and managers who learn them will make better decisions than managers who chase hype.

The book runs through ten chapters in a clear order: the information economy, pricing information, versioning information, rights management, recognizing lock-in, managing lock-in, networks and positive feedback, cooperation and compatibility, waging a standards war, and information policy. Many cases predate the web, from telephones to railroads.

## Key concepts

### 1. Costly to produce, cheap to reproduce · Evolves

The book defines information broadly as anything that can be digitized: software, music, books, databases, web pages. Such goods have a peculiar cost structure. The first copy is expensive, and every later copy costs almost nothing. That rules out cost-plus pricing, because the cost of one more copy says nothing useful about what to charge. Sellers must price by what the product is worth to each customer. Information is also an experience good, since buyers must try it before they know its value, and in a world full of it, customer attention becomes the scarce resource.

*Why it evolves:* The pattern still fits most software and content, but AI bends both ends. Generating content gets cheaper, and running an AI service costs real compute on every request, so the marginal cost of some digital products is no longer close to zero.

### 2. Versioning and Goldilocks pricing · Transcends

Because customers value the same information very differently, the authors recommend selling it in versions. Versions can differ by delay, user interface, convenience, image resolution, format, capability, features, comprehensiveness, annoyance or support. Think hardback before paperback, or real-time stock quotes priced above delayed ones. The point is to let customers sort themselves by willingness to pay. If a market does not segment naturally, the book's rule of thumb is to choose three versions, just like Goldilocks. For the reasoning it cites research by Itamar Simonson and Amos Tversky on how buyers avoid extremes, which draws them to the middle option.

*Why it transcends:* Free, standard and premium tiers are now the default way to sell software and AI tools, often with usage limits or speed as the dimension that separates versions.

### 3. Rights management: maximize value, not protection · Evolves

Digital technology makes copying cheap and changes how intellectual property must be managed, and the authors argue that protection for its own sake is the wrong aim. The right question is which terms of use make the property most valuable. Cheap sampling can help sales, and looser terms, such as letting people share or resell a product, make it worth more to each buyer but can reduce the number of copies sold. The best license balances the two. The goal is to choose terms that maximize the value of intellectual property, not the ones that lock it down hardest.

*Why it evolves:* The principle holds, but the fights have moved. Whether content can be used to train AI models, and on what terms, is a question the book could not have anticipated, and it is still being settled in courts and contracts.

### 4. The lock-in cycle · Transcends

Lock-in happens when the cost of switching from one system to another becomes high. The book traces a cycle with four stages. A customer picks a brand, samples it, becomes entrenched as data, training and habits pile up, and finally reaches lock-in, where switching costs are high; the next choice of brand starts the cycle again. The book lists seven types of lock-in: contractual commitments, durable purchases, brand-specific training, information and databases, specialized suppliers, search costs and loyalty programs. Loyalty programs create switching costs on purpose: a customer who leaves an airline loses the miles, or the new airline must match them. Sellers should anticipate the whole cycle from the start; buyers should count switching costs before committing.

*Why it transcends:* Every cloud platform, data format and AI tool that learns your workflows creates switching costs. The book's advice to price the whole cycle, and to negotiate before entrenchment, applies directly.

### 5. Positive feedback and network effects · Evolves

When a product is worth more to each user as more people use it, economists call that a network externality. Such markets have positive feedback: the strong get stronger, the weak get weaker, and a market can tip to a single winner. Reaching critical mass is everything, which is why Netscape gave its browser away. Expectations matter as much as current sales, because customers want to back the product they believe will win. The authors sort network strategies by two choices: whether to stay compatible with what customers already have or make a clean break for better performance, and whether to keep control or open the technology to allies. The four combinations are controlled migration, open migration, performance play and discontinuity.

*Why it evolves:* Network effects still drive marketplaces and messaging. Whether AI products enjoy them is less clear: a better model can draw users away fast, so advantage depends more on distribution, data and switching costs than on user count alone.

### 6. Waging a standards war · Transcends

When two incompatible technologies struggle to become the standard, the book calls it a standards war. Its history runs from railroad gauges and Edison against Westinghouse in electricity to color TV, Sony against Matsushita in VCRs, and modems. Such wars can end in a truce, as in modems, a duopoly, or a market that tips to one winner. The book names seven assets that give a firm strength in such a war, and tactics including preemption (building an early lead), penetration pricing and managing customer expectations. The chapter before it covers finding allies, since joining a common standard can grow the whole market enough to offset a smaller share.

*Why it transcends:* Formats, protocols and interfaces for connecting AI tools are being contested now, and the same moves of alliances, an early lead and managing expectations are visible in them.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Information Rules* has aged better than most business books written during the dot-com boom, largely because it refused to treat the boom as new. Its core claim, that old economic laws govern new technology, is a useful check on claims that AI changes everything.

Three concepts transcend. Versioning describes how AI tools are sold today, in tiers with limits. Lock-in is sharper than ever as tools absorb a team's data and habits. Standards wars over AI interfaces follow the book's playbook.

Three evolve. The near-zero marginal cost of information assumed in 1998 does not hold for AI services that pay for compute on every request, which changes pricing and makes heavy users expensive. Rights management now includes the unsettled question of training data. Network effects matter less for AI products, where a better model can win users quickly, than they did for operating systems and browsers.

The examples are dated; the reasoning is not.

Verdict: 3 of 6 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

Decide whether to go it alone or join others when a market might tip.

```text

Firms choose whether to stay compatible with what customers already have or break cleanly, and whether to keep control or open the technology to allies. ## Critiques and caveats

Some readers found the book one-sided. The reviewer Roger Clarke called it "a remarkably amoral book," written wholly from the seller's side: customers are acquired and locked in, and consumer rights and public policy barely appear. He still called it a must for the bookshelf, as the most useful analysis of its kind. Its examples, from modems to VCRs, date it, and its cases center on firms fighting over standards. The book also predates search advertising, smartphones and app stores, so readers must make the leap to current markets themselves.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you price, sell or buy software, data or digital services, or work in any market where compatibility and switching costs shape the outcome. **Skip it** if you want a modern playbook for platforms; *Platform Revolution* covers that ground with current cases.
