## At a glance

**AI changes the firm itself, not just its products: a company run on data, algorithms and networks escapes the old limits on scale, scope and learning, and traditional firms must rebuild to compete with it.**

Marco Iansiti and Karim R. Lakhani · 2020 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Iansiti and Lakhani are professors at Harvard Business School. Iansiti is the David Sarnoff Professor of Business Administration, co-heads the school's Technology and Operations Management unit and cofounded the consultancy Keystone Strategy. Lakhani founded and co-directs the Laboratory for Innovation Science at Harvard and sits on the board of the Mozilla Corporation.

Their argument is that AI is a change in how firms operate, not a gadget bolted onto them. For more than a century, companies have been organized around human labor and management, and that design caps how far they can grow. A firm whose core processes run on software and data removes the human bottleneck. It does not take human-like intelligence. In the first chapter they write: "Imperfect, weak AI is already enough to transform the nature of firms and how they operate."

## Key concepts

### 1. Scale, scope and learning without the old limits · Transcends

The authors separate a firm's business model, how it promises to create and capture value, from its operating model, how it actually delivers that value. An operating model has three jobs: reach scale, achieve enough scope, and learn. Traditional operating models hit diminishing returns as they grow; digital ones do not, since software copies at almost no cost. Ant Financial is the book's lead case: with fewer than ten thousand employees it served more than 700 million customers, while Bank of America employed 209,000 people to serve 67 million. Its loans follow a "3-1-0" process: three minutes to apply, one second to approve, no human involved.

*Why it transcends:* Generative AI pushes software into drafting, coding and customer service, so the gap between digital and manual operating models has widened.

### 2. The AI factory · Evolves

At the center of the new firm sits what the authors call the AI factory, a scalable decision engine. It has four parts. The data pipeline gathers, cleans, normalizes and integrates data. Algorithm development turns that data into predictions about the business. The experimentation platform tests new prediction and decision algorithms before they go live. Software infrastructure ties it all together. Netflix is the chapter's main example: it uses viewing data to personalize recommendations, artwork and streaming quality for each member, runs constant A/B tests, and leaned on data in its bet on *House of Cards*.

*Why it evolves:* The four parts still hold, but many firms now rent the algorithm from a model provider instead of developing it. Advantage shifts toward the data pipeline, the experiments and how well the model is wired into the work.

### 3. From silos to an integrated data platform · Transcends

Traditional firms are built from specialized, semi-independent units, and their IT systems copy that structure, so data gets trapped in pockets. The remedy is an integrated architecture built on a shared data platform, with components connected through clean interfaces. Amazon is the key example, starting with Jeff Bezos's 2002 memo requiring teams to expose their data and functionality through internal interfaces, the step that led to Amazon's software platform and later AWS.

*Why it transcends:* A model can only work with the data it can reach, so trapped data still limits what AI can do inside a company.

### 4. Becoming an AI company · Transcends

The central case is Microsoft under Satya Nadella, rebuilt as a cloud and AI company around Azure, alongside research on some 350 enterprises and a closing look at Fidelity Investments. The authors set out five principles for a traditional firm's transformation: one clear strategy, a clear architecture, agile product-focused teams, capabilities built inside and through partners, and governance that spans disciplines. They also lay out four stages, from siloed data to pilots, then a shared data hub, and finally a full AI factory. The point is that pilots alone do not transform a firm; the whole operating model has to change.

*Why it transcends:* Many companies are now stuck at the pilot stage with generative AI, the early stage the book warns firms not to mistake for transformation.

### 5. Network and learning effects · Evolves

The strategy chapter pairs network effects, where a service becomes more valuable as more people use it, with learning effects, the value added as more data flows through the same network. Together they can produce increasing returns. Several forces weaken a network's grip: clustering into local networks, multihoming when users can join rival platforms at once, and disintermediation, when users bypass the hub after the first match. Network bridging, linking previously separate networks, can strengthen a weak position.

*Why it evolves:* Network effects still drive marketplaces, but learning effects look less secure. Large models trained on public data give rivals strong capability without the incumbent's user data, so a data lead is harder to defend than the book implies.

### 6. Strategic collisions · Transcends

A collision happens when a digital operating model targets a need a traditional firm serves. The entrant often delivers less value at first, but once it passes critical mass, network and learning effects drive it far beyond the incumbent. The book's cases run from smartphones, where the iPhone and Android overtook Nokia, to current battlegrounds such as home sharing and automotive, with Amazon against Barnes & Noble among earlier examples. Incumbents need not die. They can build a digital operating model of their own or make their products the best complement to the new platforms. Nokia, in the authors' telling, did neither.

*Why it transcends:* AI-native startups are now colliding with law firms, agencies and software vendors in the same pattern: weak at first, then improving fast.

### 7. The new meta and the ethics of digital scale · Evolves

Borrowing a term from games, the authors describe a "new meta," a change in the rules of the game itself. They give five rules: change is systemic and hits all industries at once; capabilities become horizontal and universal; industries recombine; operations move from constrained to frictionless; and value concentrates in hub firms, raising inequality. The ethics chapter covers digital amplification, algorithmic bias, data security and privacy (the Equifax breach), and platform control and equity (Facebook and Cambridge Analytica). Hub firms, the authors argue, must take responsibility for the networks they control.

*Why it evolves:* These risks are more pressing now, and synthetic media, model errors and training-data disputes join them, so the chapter reads as a starting list.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Competing in the Age of AI* predates the public arrival of large language models, but its point was that narrow, imperfect machine learning was already enough to reshape firms. Generative AI strengthens that argument by taking software into writing, analysis and conversation, the knowledge work that kept even digital firms dependent on people.

What holds is the organizational advice. Freeing siloed data, running one strategy, experimenting constantly and moving past pilots remain the hard parts of adopting AI.

What changes is the economics of the algorithm. The book pictures each firm building its own AI factory. Today the most capable models are rented, which lowers the barrier for traditional firms and weakens the learning effects the book counts on. The concentration it warned about has moved up a layer, to the few companies that train the largest models.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

The book leans on cases that moved fast after it came out. Ant Group's planned stock listing was suspended by Chinese regulators in November 2020, a reminder that the hub power the book describes invites political pushback. Peloton, one of the book's three digital unicorns, paused production and cut thousands of jobs in early 2022 as pandemic demand faded. Many cases come from firms the authors know well (the book carries a disclosures section), so they illustrate the argument more than test it. Srivatsa Krishna's Business Standard review notes it says little about AI in government and public services. In our reading, the prescriptions also assume a firm builds its own models, which fewer need to do now.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you lead a traditional company trying to become data-driven, or you want a clear framework for why digital firms grow the way they do. **Skip it** if you want practical guidance on using today's generative AI tools; *Power and Prediction* is more current on how AI reshapes decisions and systems.
