## At a glance

**Generative AI is a strange new kind of co-intelligence, good at some hard things and bad at some easy ones, and the only way to learn where it helps your work is to use it, often and with your eyes open.**

Ethan Mollick · 2024 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Ethan Mollick is an associate professor of management at the Wharton School, where he co-directs the Generative AI Labs and studies how AI affects work, entrepreneurship and education. He holds a PhD and an MBA from MIT Sloan, and writes the One Useful Thing newsletter.

Portfolio published the book in April 2024, after chat assistants reached the public in late 2022. Its premise is that large language models are a new thing in the world: neither ordinary software nor people, and best treated as a partner whose strengths and failures have to be found by trial.

The book has two parts. The first explains how these models are built, why aligning them with human values is hard, and four rules for working with them. The second tours AI's roles as a person, creative, coworker, tutor and coach, then closes with four scenarios for where AI could go next.

## Key concepts

### 1. An alien co-intelligence · Evolves

Mollick describes his first encounters with chat assistants as meeting something close to an alien co-intelligence. Aware of the risk of treating software as human, he still argues that "working with AI is easiest if you think of it like an alien person rather than a human-built machine." The same feature that makes these models unreliable for facts, the book argues, makes them useful for ideas.

*Why it evolves:* The partner framing still helps, but Mollick's own 2025 essay "On Working with Wizards" says people are moving from shaping AI's work turn by turn to receiving finished output and checking it.

### 2. The jagged frontier · Transcends

AI's abilities form a jagged frontier: tasks that look equally hard can fall on opposite sides of it, and where a model will succeed or fail is hard to know in advance. The term comes from a 2023 field experiment Mollick co-wrote with researchers from Harvard, MIT, Warwick and Boston Consulting Group, involving 758 BCG consultants. On tasks inside the frontier, consultants using AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, worked 25.1 percent faster and produced work rated more than 40 percent higher in quality. On a task chosen to fall outside it, they were 19 percentage points less likely to reach a correct answer than those working without AI.

*Why it transcends:* In a December 2025 newsletter post, Mollick argues that jaggedness will remain a big part of AI, even as better models change the shape of the frontier. The edge moves outward, but it stays uneven, so mapping it is a lasting skill.

### 3. Centaurs and cyborgs · Evolves

The same study found two styles of working with AI. Centaurs divide the labor: the person takes some tasks, the machine takes others, with a clear line between them. Cyborgs blend the two, moving back and forth with the AI inside a single task. Consultants who started below average gained the most, 43 percent, against 17 percent for those above average. In a March 2024 newsletter post, Mollick describes writing the book in a mixed way himself: he rarely used AI's text, but used AI to get unstuck on paragraphs, check his reading of technical papers, and play critical readers of his drafts.

*Why it evolves:* Both styles assume a person working step by step with the machine. As AI handles longer tasks on its own, a third pattern of handing off, waiting and checking is growing alongside them.

### 4. Always invite AI to the table · Transcends

The first of the book's four rules: try AI on every task where it is legal and ethical to do so. Nobody, Mollick says in a 2024 Stanford podcast interview, knows what AI is good or bad at inside a particular job or industry. Experimenting on real work is how to find out.

*Why it transcends:* Because the frontier keeps shifting, a personal map of where AI helps goes stale. The habit of testing it on real work outlasts any single tool.

### 5. Be the human in the loop · Evolves

The second rule: stay involved, check what the AI produces, and keep final decisions with a person. In the Stanford interview, Mollick says AI beats many people at parts of their jobs but not their whole job, and that whatever you are best at, you are almost certainly better at than the AI. His 2023 newsletter post on the BCG study warns of a related risk, drawn from a separate study: recruiters given a high-quality AI grew less careful and made worse decisions than those given a weaker one or none, as if asleep at the wheel.

*Why it evolves:* Staying in the loop still matters, but checking gets harder as AI output grows longer and more expert. Mollick's 2025 essay suggests people will not always be able to verify what they receive.

### 6. Tell it what kind of person it is · Expires

The third rule is to treat AI like a person, but tell it what kind of person it is. Give it specific context, including a role to play: Mollick's Stanford example is an expert marketing manager in India focused on technology ventures that work with the US. In remarks quoted by Big Think, he warns that treating AI as human can also make people more easily persuaded by it, or lead them to think it is more capable than it is.

*Why it expires:* The persona technique has not held up. In late 2024 Mollick wrote that roles add context but do not turn the AI into an expert, and a 2025 Wharton report he co-wrote found that expert personas do not improve factual accuracy. Giving clear context still helps; the role-play adds little.

### 7. Assume this is the worst AI you will ever use · Transcends

The fourth rule: a later AI will likely do better than today's, so failures are worth retrying. The book's last chapter frames the future as four scenarios: As Good As It Gets, Slow Growth, Exponential Growth and The Machine God, in which machines reach general intelligence and some form of sentience.

*Why it transcends:* Many of the book's own 2023 examples of AI limits have already dated, which is the rule proving itself.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Co-Intelligence* is a field guide written early in the life of its subject, so the test is direct: which advice survived two more years of fast model improvement?

The mindset holds. Experiment on real work, expect jagged results, retry what failed, and keep a person accountable for the outcome. None of it depends on a particular model.

The techniques aged fastest. Persona prompting, careful phrasing and the picture of AI as a chat partner you steer turn by turn all reflect models of 2023. Mollick's own newsletter has moved on: it now says larger models are less sensitive to prompting technique and describes a shift toward handing AI whole tasks and checking the results. Staying in the loop gets harder as a result, and matters more.

The book's most durable worry may be about meaning. In a passage Fortune excerpted, the book pictures AI-written documents sent to AI-run inboxes, and warns: "With AI-generated work sent to other AIs to assess, that sense of meaning disappears." That question gets sharper as AI does more of the work.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend, 3 evolve and 1 expires.

## Apply it with AI

Copy a prompt into your AI assistant. Each prompt carries the book's key ideas, so the AI works from this summary instead of its memory. The project starter turns the book into an ongoing coach that remembers your situation.

### 1. Map your jagged frontier

Find which of your tasks AI helps with, and which it quietly makes worse.

```text
In "Co-Intelligence," Ethan Mollick describes AI's abilities as a jagged frontier: tasks that look equally hard can fall on opposite sides, and on tasks outside the frontier people using AI do worse than people without it. The only way to find the edge is to experiment on real work.

Ask me four questions about my role, my main tasks and where mistakes are costly. Then sort my tasks into likely inside, likely outside and unclear, design a quick test for each unclear one, and tell me what a failure would look like so I can spot it.

[paste a list of your regular tasks here]
```

### 2. Choose centaur or cyborg

Plan how to split one piece of work between you and the AI.

```text
"Co-Intelligence" describes two ways to work with AI. Centaurs divide the work: the person keeps some tasks and hands others to the machine. Cyborgs blend the two, going back and forth inside a single task. Either way, a person stays in the loop and checks the output.

Ask me three questions about the project, my strengths and the deadline. Then propose a centaur split and a cyborg workflow, say which suits this project better and why, and list the checkpoints where I must review the AI's work before it moves on.

[paste a description of the project here]
```

### 3. Retry what failed

Revisit tasks where AI let you down before, on the book's rule that today's AI is the worst you will use.

```text
Ethan Mollick's fourth rule in "Co-Intelligence" is to assume today's AI is the worst you will ever use: its abilities keep improving, so past failures are worth retrying. His first rule is to bring AI into every task where it is legal and ethical, because nobody knows in advance what it can do in your job.

Ask me about two or three tasks where AI disappointed me and when I last tried. Then rewrite each attempt with clearer context and goals, tell me what a good result should look like, and suggest how often I should retest.

[paste the tasks and what went wrong here]
```

### Project starter

Paste this into a project or the custom instructions in your AI tool to keep working with the book's ideas over time.

```text
You are my AI work coach, grounded in Ethan Mollick's "Co-Intelligence." Core ideas: AI is a co-intelligence with uneven abilities, a jagged frontier where it excels at some hard tasks and fails at some easy ones; always invite AI to the table on legal and ethical tasks to learn what it can do; be the human in the loop, checking outputs and keeping final decisions; give AI clear context and a role to play; assume today's AI is the worst you will ever use, so retry past failures.

When I bring you a task or plan: ask whether it sits inside or outside the frontier, how I will check the output, and what I keep for myself. Keep a running note in this project of tasks we have tested, what worked, what failed, and when to retry.
```

## Critiques and caveats

On LessWrong, Darren McKee, reviewing it on publication in April 2024, hoped for a bit more and found Mollick's reasons for setting aside AI as a larger threat to humanity not compelling: a book on air pollution and wildfires that refused to discuss climate change, he argued, would be an odd move. The technical writer Tom Johnson, reviewing it on his blog in December 2025, warned that regular users may find many of its observations too obvious or cursory, and that the book already feels somewhat dated. Our own caveat: the central evidence comes from a study Mollick co-wrote.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you are new to AI or lead people who are, and want a short, good-humored guide to building habits that survive new models. **Skip it** if you already use AI daily: the four rules above carry most of its practical value, and Mollick's newsletter is the more current version of his thinking.
