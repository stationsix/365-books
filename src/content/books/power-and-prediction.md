## At a glance

**AI is a prediction technology, and its big payoff comes not from dropping better predictions into old processes but from redesigning whole systems, which shifts who makes decisions and so who holds power.**

Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb · 2022 · Strategy · 7-minute read

## The big idea

The three authors are economists at the University of Toronto's Rotman School of Management. Agrawal founded the Creative Destruction Lab, a program for science-based startups; Gans is its chief economist and Goldfarb its chief data scientist.

In the preface they admit that their 2018 book, *Prediction Machines*, told only the point-solutions part of the AI story. This one tells the systems part. Their footing is the same: "Technologies change, but economics doesn't." AI's power was already clear, yet it had transformed few industries. Their explanation is that decisions are tied together in systems, and the big gains wait for those systems to be rebuilt.

Its six parts cover the Between Times, Rules, Systems, Power, How AI Disrupts and Envisaging New Systems, with an epilogue on AI bias.

## Key concepts

### 1. The Between Times · Evolves

The book opens with a parable of three entrepreneurs in the shift from steam to electricity. The first wave were point solutions, swapping steam engines for electric motors to save on power. The bigger gain came when electricity decoupled machines from a central power source, so factories could be redesigned around the workflow. In the book's telling, Henry Ford could not have built the Model T production line with steam. That redesign took decades to show up in productivity. The authors place AI in a similar stretch, the Between Times: after the power of the technology is clear and before its widespread adoption.

*Why it evolves:* In our reading the lag is still visible: adding an AI tool to a job is far easier than rebuilding the work around it. But today's tools reach individual workers directly, which the factory story did not anticipate.

### 2. Point, application and system solutions · Transcends

The authors sort AI uses into three kinds. A point solution improves an existing procedure and can be adopted on its own. An application solution makes a new procedure possible, again without changing the rest of the system. A system solution changes the procedures that depend on the decision, which makes it harder and riskier to build but, the authors argue, likely to bring the greatest return. What separates the three is independence: if a better decision creates value without other decisions changing, a point solution will do. Restaurants show the knock-on effect: those that forecast demand with AI waste less, but their suppliers then face less predictable orders.

*Why it transcends:* Asking whether a decision's value depends on other decisions changing is a useful check on any AI project, whatever the model behind it.

### 3. AI is prediction, and decisions also need judgment · Evolves

The authors present AI as a prediction technology that bears directly on decision-making. A decision combines prediction, how likely each outcome is, with judgment, how much each outcome matters. Before AI, the two were bundled together, usually inside one person's decision. AI shifts the prediction to a machine, so its value shows up in the decisions it improves.

*Why it evolves:* Language models still work by prediction, which fits the frame. But AI now drafts, codes and carries out tasks, so treating it only as an input to someone else's decision undersells what it does.

### 4. Rules hide uncertainty, and rules are glue · Transcends

Rules are decisions made in advance: cheaper in thought and more reliable. Deciding case by case costs more and pays when the stakes are high. AI lowers the cost of information, which tips the balance toward decisions. The catch is that organizations build scaffolding around rules that hides the uncertainty behind them. Because rules are reliable, many can stick together, which is why "the age-based curriculum rule is the glue that holds together much of the modern education system." An AI that personalizes learning, added to that system as a point solution, has limited impact, because when one rule moves the others have to move with it.

*Why it transcends:* The best AI opportunities still sit in rules nobody questions, and finding them is still detective work.

### 5. Decoupling prediction from judgment · Transcends

Once a machine supplies the prediction, it can be separated from the judgment. That frees an organization to predict more often, to write judgment down so decisions can scale, or to hand judgment to different people. Radiology is their running case: machines may take over much of the prediction, but the authors push back on the idea that AI simply replaces radiologists, who do many tasks besides reading images. The engineers who build the machines, they argue, need to understand the judgment they embed in them.

*Why it transcends:* Instructions and policies for AI agents are judgment written down in advance, so deciding who writes them is this concept in current form.

### 6. Power moves with judgment · Evolves

Decision-making confers power, which brings profits in industry and control in society. Do machines have power? The authors say no, though machines can create power through scale. System change moves decisions, and those who lose decision rights may resist. Blockbuster, they note, could see where rentals were heading as clearly as Netflix could, but its franchisees stood to lose from the change and used their power to defend the old model.

*Why it evolves:* The logic holds, and a new holder of power has grown since: the few companies whose models carry judgment into millions of decisions they never see.

### 7. Designing from a blank slate · Transcends

The AI systems discovery canvas has three steps: state the mission, reduce the business to the fewest decisions needed to achieve it, and specify the prediction and judgment each key decision needs. Applied to home insurance, it reduces the business to marketing, underwriting and claims, and points to a bigger shift: an insurer that not only transfers risk but helps customers reduce it. In a healthcare thought experiment, a heart attack prediction used for hospital triage is a point solution; fed by smartwatch data, it could move care into patients' homes.

*Why it transcends:* Starting from the mission and the few decisions that matter, not from today's tasks, is a sound way to plan any AI redesign.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Power and Prediction* came out on November 15, 2022, about two weeks before the first widely used AI chatbot was released. It holds up four years on because its argument was about how organizations absorb a cheaper input, not about any particular model.

What holds is the system view. Much generative AI use looks like the book's point solutions: an assistant added to a job, with the job and the rules around it left alone. The three kinds of solution, the hunt for rules that hide uncertainty and the blank-slate canvas serve a team planning AI agents as well as they served a factory owner leaving steam behind.

What changes is the scope of the machine. The book treats AI as a supplier of predictions to human decision-makers. Current systems also write, act and follow instructions, so a growing share of judgment is written into prompts and policies ahead of time. That makes the book's power question sharper: whoever writes those instructions, inside the firm or at the model provider, holds the decision.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve. None expire.

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing *Prediction Machines* in the *Journal of Economic Literature*, the economist Laurence Kotlikoff doubted that today's AI is all about prediction, pointing to self-checkout and phone payments in China; this book keeps that premise. Frank Dillon in *The Irish Times* praised its healthy scepticism. The actuary Jim Lynch, in *Contingencies*, was cooler: he found the power struggle over judgment oversimplified, said insurers face a financial rather than organizational obstacle, and likened the blank-slate process to a standard blue-sky session. Our own caveats: the book predates the chat assistants that brought AI into daily office work, and several system cases, such as heart attack care at home, are thought experiments, not evidence that redesign pays.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you are deciding where AI fits in your organization and want a way to tell small fixes from real redesign, or if you care how AI will shift decision rights and power. **Skip it** if you want a hands-on guide to using today's AI tools; the three kinds of solution and the canvas above carry most of the practical method.
