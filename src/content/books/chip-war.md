## At a glance

**The modern economy and modern military power both run on computer chips, and the most advanced ones now depend on a handful of companies and places, which makes the chip supply chain a contest between nations.**

Chris Miller · 2022 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Chris Miller is an economic historian at Tufts University's Fletcher School whose three earlier books were on Russia. His interest in chips grew from a question in Soviet history: why a state that built atomic weapons and reached space first never mastered computing.

*Chip War* argues that semiconductors shape the world economy, military power and the balance between nations, yet most people never think about where they come from. Its answer is a story of a few inventors, a lot of government money, and supply chains that spread across the Pacific until the most advanced chips could be made in only a few factories, most of them in Taiwan.

The book runs in 54 short chapters across eight parts, from the transistor at Bell Labs to the US and China facing off over Taiwan. One reviewer compared its pace to a thriller. It won the 2022 *Financial Times* Business Book of the Year award.

## Key concepts

### 1. Computing power is military power · Transcends

The chip industry began as a defense industry. In the early 1960s, Fairchild and Texas Instruments, home to the two inventors of the integrated circuit, Robert Noyce and Jack Kilby, found their first big customers in NASA and the US military. The US could not match Soviet numbers in troops and tanks, so it set out to offset them with precision weapons guided by electronics. Miller argues that this edge, shown in the 1991 Gulf War, helped decide the Cold War. As he puts it, "the Cold War was over; Silicon Valley had won." He suggests computing power may decide the US-China rivalry too.

*Why it transcends:* AI makes the link tighter, since the most capable systems are limited by how much computing power their builders can get.

### 2. Copying cannot keep up · Evolves

The Soviet Union tried to catch up by copying. A KGB unit, Directorate T, stole American chips, and the state built Zelenograd, a city meant to replicate Silicon Valley. Spies could get the chips but not the manufacturing know-how, and by the time a copy worked, the American original had moved on. The book uses this to show why chipmaking resists theft: the hard part is the manufacturing process, not the chip itself.

*Why it evolves:* The lesson still fits chip factories, but software, AI models included, can often be imitated far faster than a manufacturing process, so copying is a weaker strategy in hardware than in code.

### 3. Governments build chip industries · Evolves

Markets alone did not create the industry. After the Pentagon came the industrial policy of Asia. Japan licensed American technology and, with government support and cheap capital, overtook US memory chip makers in the 1980s. Then South Korea's Samsung undercut Japanese producers with Silicon Valley's help. Taiwan's government courted American chipmakers from the late 1960s and supplied half of TSMC's starting capital. China has since spent billions on subsidies.

*Why it evolves:* State support matters more now, as governments compete to subsidize factories, but how well subsidies work is still an open question.

### 4. The foundry split · Transcends

Morris Chang rose to senior posts at Texas Instruments, was passed over for the top job, and was brought to Taiwan by the economy minister K. T. Li to build its chip industry. In 1987 he founded Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) with a simple plan: design nothing and manufacture for everyone. That let "fabless" firms such as Nvidia and Apple design chips without owning a factory. After the 2008 financial crisis, Chang, still chairman, removed his cost-cutting successor, rehired laid-off workers and spent heavily on research to win the iPhone's chips, a contract Apple later moved from Samsung to TSMC.

*Why it transcends:* The split between design and manufacturing is how most AI chips are made today, with fabless designers relying on a foundry.

### 5. Choke points · Transcends

Specialization made the industry efficient and fragile. Miller maps the places where one firm or country holds a capability no one else has. TSMC builds almost all of the most advanced processor chips. Only the Dutch firm ASML has mastered extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, whose machines fire lasers at droplets of tin 50,000 times a second and cost about $100 million each, which the book calls "the most expensive mass-produced machine tool in history." Three firms dominate the software used to design chips. In a 2023 interview, Miller pointed to the 2020 to 2021 shortage, when carmakers had nearly finished cars waiting for one missing chip.

*Why it transcends:* AI has added new pressure points, but the method of finding where one supplier controls everything applies to any supply chain.

### 6. Missing the next shift · Transcends

Intel shows that leaders lose by misjudging change, not only by being outrun. It once made a brave turn, leaving memory chips to Japan and betting on PC microprocessors. Later it passed on making the iPhone's chips, judging the price too low, and never found a foothold in mobile. Nvidia, meanwhile, introduced its CUDA software in 2006 so developers could use its graphics chips for general computing, which later suited AI. By the late 2010s, Intel had fallen behind TSMC in manufacturing.

*Why it transcends:* AI is producing the same kind of shift, and the Intel story is a clear warning against judging a new market by the margins of the old one.

### 7. China's long catch-up · Evolves

China spends more each year importing chips than importing oil. Under Xi Jinping it has poured tens of billions into subsidies, drawn Western firms into licensing deals and joint ventures and sought stakes in US chip companies, yet it remains behind at the cutting edge. Miller argues China cannot build a supply chain free of US technology, so American restrictions hold real leverage. The book also notes the risk the analyst Dan Wang raised: restrictions can spur China's drive for self-sufficiency.

*Why it evolves:* The question has moved from whether China can catch up in phones to whether it can get the chips for advanced AI, which raises the stakes on both sides.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*Chip War* went to press just before the US imposed sweeping limits on China's access to advanced chips and chipmaking tools in October 2022, so its last chapters read as a prelude. The economist Barry Eichengreen noted it does not assess whether such controls will work.

What holds is the structure. AI did not change the map Miller drew; it raised the price of every point on it. In a January 2023 interview, he noted that almost all the graphics chips that run AI applications are made in Taiwan. The foundry split, the EUV monopoly and the Taiwan risk sit under every large AI system.

What changes is the center of demand and the policy fight. The book tells the story through PCs and phones, and Nvidia appears mainly as a graphics chip designer whose software opened new uses. In a 2026 lecture, Miller said the largest US tech companies are struggling to get enough computing power and that export controls have changed the competition with China. Those are his later views, not the book's.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve.

### 1. Map your choke points

Find the places in your supply chain where one supplier, one site or one skill holds everything up.

```text
In "Chip War," Chris Miller shows that specialization made the chip industry efficient and fragile: one firm (ASML) makes the most advanced lithography machines, one foundry (TSMC) makes most leading-edge chips, and a carmaker can be stopped by one missing part. A choke point is any input with a single source and no quick substitute.

Ask me four questions about what my business makes or sells, my key suppliers and where they operate. Then list my likely choke points, rate each by how hard it would be to replace and how long that would take, and suggest one practical step to reduce the worst three.

[paste a list of your main suppliers, tools, platforms and their locations here]
```

### 2. Stress-test a single-source dependency

Play out what happens if your most critical supplier goes dark.

```text
Chris Miller, author of "Chip War," warns that losing a concentrated supplier is not like losing one vendor among many: rebuilding advanced chip capacity outside Taiwan would take years, and the parts that look small (one chip in a car) can stop everything. Concentration is often invisible until it fails.

Ask me three questions about the supplier, what we buy from them and our contracts. Then describe a realistic disruption scenario, trace what stops first and what stops later, estimate how long recovery would take, and list the warning signs and backup options we should set up now.

[paste a description of the supplier and what depends on it here]
```

### 3. Check whether you are Intel

Test whether you are judging a new market by the economics of your old one.

```text
In "Chip War," Intel passed on making the iPhone's chips because the price looked too low by PC standards, and never found a foothold in mobile. Nvidia, by contrast, opened its graphics chips to general computing with free software years before AI made that pay off. Leaders often lose by misreading a shift rather than by being outrun.

Ask me four questions about my business, its main product and the new opportunities we have turned down. Then identify any decision where we may be applying old margins to a new market, describe what the "Nvidia move" might look like for us, and suggest a small, cheap bet to keep the option open.

[paste a short description of your business and recent decisions here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

In the *Journal of Economic History*, the economist Kenneth Flamm praised the political story but judged it weaker as economic history, citing omissions such as Taiwan's failed 1990s memory chip push and little on how US trade pressure on Japan worked. He also doubted the book's account of how far Soviet microelectronics lagged. In the *Los Angeles Review of Books*, the historian W. Patrick McCray found little continuity from one short chapter to the next and judged Miller harder on Obama's China policy than on the later hawks. The newsletter *The Chip Letter* found it light on technology, software and commercial motives. Our caveat: the book views the industry from Washington.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want to understand the hardware every AI system depends on or work in supply chains, policy or technology strategy. **Skip it** if you want a technical guide to how chips work or a playbook for your own business: the choke point method above covers what most readers will apply.
