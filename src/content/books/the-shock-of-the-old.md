## At a glance

**Edgerton argues that the history of technology changes once you ask what people actually used rather than what was newly invented: old things turn out to last, spread and matter far longer than the stories of innovation admit.**

David Edgerton · 2006 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

David Edgerton, a British historian born in Montevideo in 1959, was the Hans Rausing Professor at Imperial College London and founding director of its Centre for the History of Science, Technology and Medicine; in 2013 he led the centre's move to King's College London. Travels in the mid-1990s to India, Malaysia, Argentina and Uruguay, full of long-lived machines, convinced him a global history of technology in use was needed.

The title plays on *The Shock of the New*, the 1980 television series. Edgerton's target is the innovation-centered history that dates each technology by its invention and first use, then sorts the century into ages such as electrification and computerisation. He asks instead what most people were really using, maintaining and fighting with. Profile Books published it in 2006 and Oxford University Press in the US in 2007. Its eight chapters are Significance, Time, Production, Maintenance, Nations, War, Killing and Invention.

## Key concepts

### 1. Technology in use, not innovation · Transcends

The book separates invention, the first idea, and innovation, its first use, from use, which can run for a century. Histories built on the first two follow technologies only while they are new and famous. A history of technology in use follows them through their lives. Edgerton has said the point is not to replace innovation-centered history but to stop confusing the two. Seen through use, most inventions fail, most patents lapse unused, and users, far from the laboratories, do much of the inventing as they adapt machines to their needs.

*Why it transcends:* Asking how many people use a technology, and for what, is still the quickest corrective to any launch story, an AI launch included.

### 2. Old and new side by side · Transcends

The book shows old and new technologies existing together. The B-52 bomber, introduced in the 1950s, dropped about a third of US bombs in the 1991 Gulf War. In 2004 it carried NASA's experimental X-43A aloft. American agricultural horsepower peaked around 1915. He also attacks what he terms passé futurism, the twentieth century's technological common sense.

*Why it transcends:* Most firms will run AI alongside spreadsheets, email and paper for years, and the useful question is how they combine rather than which one wins.

### 3. Significance needs alternatives · Transcends

The book argues that innovation-centered histories assume significance rather than ask about it. Alternatives exist for nearly all technologies, so importance should be measured against what people would otherwise have done. Airlines in the 1970s did not have to adopt supersonic flight, despite the billions spent developing it. Edgerton calls Germany's investment in the V-2 rocket economically and militarily irrational: each one cost about two lives to make and killed on average one civilian. Small things can matter more than big ones, and the book counts the condom and the sewing machine among the technologies that standard histories overlook.

*Why it transcends:* Before calling any tool transformative, ask what people would have done without it and at what cost; the question applies to AI as much as to rockets.

### 4. Production, maintenance and creole technologies · Transcends

Mass production, the book argues, is so common it is almost invisible. Maintenance is more neglected still, though it shapes which technologies people choose. In Ghana's vast workshop districts, mechanics have for decades adapted and refitted vehicles from the industrialised world, without spare parts or manuals. Edgerton calls this adaptation of rich-world goods in poor places "creole" technology. "At dusk," he writes, "bright intermittent light from welding illuminates streets all over the world." Rickshaws carry people; corrugated iron, oil drums and asbestos cement build the shanty towns of cities such as Lagos and Jakarta. He cites an estimate that a personal computer's purchase price is about a tenth of its lifetime cost, and admits the history of maintenance is too thin for an overview.

*Why it transcends:* Every AI system needs monitoring, retraining and repair, and the people who do that work are as overlooked as the Ghanaian mechanic.

### 5. Nations and techno-nationalism · Evolves

The chapter on nations attacks techno-nationalism, the habit of treating technology as a national possession and a race that a country wins by inventing. Most technology, Edgerton argues, crosses borders, so the nation is a poor unit for understanding it; even the Soviet MiG-15 flew on British Rolls-Royce engines. Countries that invent more do not necessarily grow faster.

*Why it evolves:* AI has revived the race-between-nations story, and his argument now has to be tested on chips and models that a few countries control tightly.

### 6. War and killing with cheap things · Evolves

Two chapters on war and killing follow the money and the dead rather than the headlines. Armies in the Second World War still relied on horses; the German army had about 1.2 million in 1945. In the First World War, artillery mattered more than gas, aircraft or tanks. Later the cheap Kalashnikov rifle and its descendants, more than the aeroplane, civilianised war. The killing chapter also takes in insecticides, slaughterhouses and chemical warfare.

*Why it evolves:* Cheap, mass-made drones on today's battlefields fit his point that cheap things do most of the damage, though they are new technology used his way.

### 7. Doubting the information age · Expires

The book questions the idea that the world entered an "information age." Beyond that doubt, it says little about new information and communication technologies, a gap the information scientist T. D. Wilson noted in his review. In 2007 Edward Tenner, in the *London Review of Books*, already pointed to cheap village cell phones in India and Africa as a limit of the argument.

*Why it expires:* Mobile phones have since spread deep into poorer countries, so the doubt fits the rhetoric of the information age better than its record.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book predates today's AI, but Edgerton has since spoken about it; his view is credited here and the rest is our reading. Giving evidence to the House of Lords Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence on December 19, 2017, he called talk of a fourth industrial revolution reheated rhetoric and, as Computer Weekly reported, called hyping AI "ahistorical, crude nonsense." He also warned against nationalistic approaches to AI.

Our reading: the book was picked as the strongest outside test of this site's working hypothesis that more ideas transcend than expire, so its argument should be turned on the site. Edgerton's evidence of persistence seems to support the hypothesis: old things last. But his method undercuts the way we tag. Each verdict here is made at the moment a new technology is announced, which is the innovation-centered timing he distrusts. Expires calls are the riskiest, since history is full of horses declared obsolete while still pulling carts. Transcends calls are claims of significance and need his alternatives test. This summary lands on the site's usual shape, which is a reason for readers to treat the tally as a judgment, not a measurement.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend, 2 evolve and 1 expires.

```text

Ask me four questions about how my team or business gets its work done day to day. ### 2. Test a claim of significance
```

Check whether a "transformative" technology beats its alternatives.

```text
David Edgerton argues that claims a technology is significant usually assume no alternative existed. Supersonic flight and the V-2 rocket looked transformative but cost far more than they delivered. Significance should be judged against what people would otherwise have done.

Ask me three questions about the technology I'm considering and the problem it is meant to solve. Then list the realistic alternatives, including doing nothing new, compare their costs and results, and say how much of the claimed benefit survives the comparison.

[paste the claim, pitch or business case here]
```

### 3. Price the maintenance

Count what a new tool will cost to keep running, not just to buy.

```text
"The Shock of the Old" argues that maintenance is the hidden side of technology: users adapt and repair what they have, and the purchase price can be a small share of lifetime cost. Edgerton cites an estimate that a personal computer's purchase price is about a tenth of what it costs over its life.

Ask me four questions about the tool I plan to adopt and who will look after it. Then list the ongoing work it will need (monitoring, updates, repair, training, adaptation), estimate who does it and what it costs over three years, and say what happens if nobody does it.

[paste a description of the tool and your plans here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Reviewing for *Times Higher Education* in 2007, W. Bernard Carlson said the book says little about why the innovation-centered view was so persuasive, a gap he tied to power: the state, big business and the professions gained from it. Edward Tenner, in the *London Review of Books*, found the persistence of old technology only partly explained and argued hype can build morale and investment against discouraging odds. *American Scientist* judged the examples chosen for vividness more than for the central themes, with nothing on patents or incentives. Bruce Sterling noted that Edgerton uses the rhetorical devices of the futurist pundits he attacks, H. G. Wells above all. Thomas Söderqvist observed that social historians of technology had said much the same before, and Chris Berg found it engaging but not innovative.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you plan, buy or write about technology and want a sharp, readable antidote to launch-day thinking, full of cases you will repeat. **Skip it** if you want a framework or a playbook: it is an argument made through examples, and the publisher's interview with Edgerton and the reviews above give its core claims.
