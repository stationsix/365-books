## At a glance

**How well people notice, manage and read emotions, their own and other people's, shapes how far their intellect takes them, and unlike IQ these skills can be learned.**

Daniel Goleman · 1995 · Mindset for Builders · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Goleman, a psychologist who wrote about psychology and brain science for *The New York Times*, set out to explain why people with high IQs often flounder while people of modest IQ do surprisingly well. His answer is a different way of being smart, built from self-awareness, self-discipline and empathy.

He borrowed the term **emotional intelligence** from the psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer, whose 1990 paper described skills for appraising, expressing and regulating emotion in oneself and others. Goleman widened it for a general reader and argued that these skills can be taught.

The book moves in five parts: the emotional brain, the nature of emotional intelligence, applications in marriage, work and health, childhood and temperament, and emotional literacy in schools. In October 1995 *Time* ran a long story on the idea, "The EQ Factor." His workplace books came later: *Working with Emotional Intelligence* (1998), the 1998 HBR article "What Makes a Leader?" and *Primal Leadership* (2002, with Richard Boyatzis and Annie McKee).

## Key concepts

### 1. A second kind of smart · Evolves

Goleman builds on Howard Gardner's case for multiple intelligences and on Salovey and Mayer's definition, which he lays out in five domains: knowing one's emotions, managing emotions, motivating oneself, recognizing emotions in others, and handling relationships. He calls the whole a meta-ability that decides how well people use their other gifts, including raw intellect. In a 1996 interview with *Educational Leadership*, he summed up the research he drew on: "IQ contributes, at best, about 20 percent to the factors that determine life success." In the same interview he said both kinds of intelligence matter, in different ways.

*Why it evolves:* The case for skills beyond raw intellect gets stronger as software handles more analysis, but the precise claims about how much each kind of smart counts have not held up well.

### 2. Anatomy of an emotional hijacking · Evolves

The book's best-known image comes from neuroscience. Drawing on the work of Joseph LeDoux at New York University, Goleman describes the amygdala as an emotional sentinel. Sensory signals can reach it before the thinking neocortex has finished analyzing what is happening, so it can trigger a response first. When that happens, the emotional brain takes over: people "lose it" and react out of all proportion. The prefrontal cortex can then step in to calm the reaction and correct a false alarm.

*Why it evolves:* The practical point, that a strong surge can outrun judgment, still holds. The brain science has moved on, and LeDoux himself has since warned against calling the amygdala a fear center.

### 3. Knowing one's emotions · Transcends

Self-awareness is the cornerstone of the whole framework: the capacity to recognize a feeling as it happens. Feelings, he argues, guide choices that logic alone cannot settle, such as whom to trust or what job to take. Goleman shows the cost of its absence through alexithymia, an inability to recognize and put words to feelings. Empathy, in his account, builds on self-awareness.

*Why it transcends:* No tool can tell someone what they are feeling in a tense meeting. That remains a human skill, and it sets the quality of every decision made in that state.

### 4. The master aptitude · Evolves

Goleman argues that emotions can help or wreck thinking. Strong upsets swamp working memory, while a measure of worry can focus the mind on a problem. His central evidence is Walter Mischel's marshmallow study from the 1960s: children offered a second marshmallow if they waited were followed into high school. Those who grabbed the first one at age four were more likely to be lonely, easily frustrated and stubborn as teenagers, and in his telling the ones who waited scored an average of 210 points higher on the SAT. He adds Martin Seligman's research on optimism among insurance salespeople and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's flow, the full absorption that comes when challenge matches skill.

*Why it evolves:* Self-control and the ability to stay in flow still matter, but the marshmallow evidence has shrunk under later study (see the critiques).

### 5. Empathy and the social arts · Transcends

Empathy starts in infancy, as babies mimic and later separate their own distress from others'. Goleman stresses that emotions travel mostly through tone of voice, gesture and expression, citing Robert Rosenthal's test of nonverbal sensitivity, on which high scorers tended to be more sociable and popular. The social arts build on that: handling relationships starts with managing one's own feelings well enough to manage other people's. Emotions are contagious, passing from the more forceful person to others. One story shows a conflict defused rather than won: on a Tokyo train, an aikido student, Terry Dobson, was ready to fight a drunk, angry laborer, but an elderly man calmed him by greeting him warmly and drawing him into conversation.

*Why it transcends:* AI can draft a message in a warm tone, but reading the room and handling a hard conversation still happen between people.

### 6. Managing with heart · Transcends

The workplace chapter argues that the bullying boss is giving way to managers who build consensus, partly because stress makes people worse at learning, attention and decisions. His model for feedback draws on the consultant Harry Levinson: be specific, offer a solution, deliver it face to face and in private, and be sensitive to how it lands. Personal attacks, sarcasm and contempt provoke defensiveness and leave the real problem unsolved.

*Why it transcends:* Performance reviews written with an assistant's help still land on a person. Specific, private, solution-focused feedback works whoever drafts the words.

### 7. Schooling the emotions · Evolves

The last part argues that rising depression, violence and other troubles among young people reflect a cost of emotional illiteracy, and that schools can teach these skills to every child, not only those in trouble. Goleman profiles programs such as Self Science at the Nueva Learning Center, where feelings, one's own and those in relationships, are the subject, and an inner-city program teaching children to name emotions and choose better responses than lashing out.

*Why it evolves:* Social and emotional learning has spread widely since 1995. The open question now is how these skills are taught to people who spend much of their day talking to machines.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

The book's core bet, that skills of self-awareness, self-control and empathy decide how far raw intellect goes, gets more interesting when software supplies more of the intellect. If analysis becomes cheap, the scarce skills are the ones the book names: staying calm under pressure, reading people and handling conflict.

Goleman has since written about this himself. In a July 2025 Korn Ferry article he called AI's lack of emotions its biggest limitation and named leadership as the most obvious role it cannot replace. That is his later view, not the book's, which was written long before today's AI tools.

What changes is the evidence and the terrain. The neuroscience and the marshmallow results have both been revised, so the book's case is better treated as a useful frame than as settled science. And the social arts now include working alongside tools that imitate warmth without feeling it.

Verdict: 3 of 7 key concepts transcend and 4 evolve.

```

Personal attacks, sarcasm and contempt make people defensive and leave the problem unsolved.

```

### 3. Read the room before a hard meeting

Prepare for a tense conversation by mapping the emotions in play.

```text
Goleman argues that empathy, reading others' feelings mostly from tone, gesture and expression, and the social arts of handling relationships decide how well people work together. Emotions are contagious, passing from the most forceful person in a room to everyone else.

Ask me four questions about the meeting, who will be there and what each person wants or fears. Then describe the likely emotional state of each person, where conflict could flare, what I should listen for, and two ways I could lower the temperature if it rises.

[paste notes on the meeting here]
```

## Critiques and caveats

Salovey and Mayer felt Goleman stretched their idea. "The claims made for emotional intelligence were unrelated to anything we have ever claimed," Mayer told *Salon* in 1999, and he said the claim that it predicts success better than IQ appears nowhere in their work. Reviewers at the time found the statistics glib and doubted the skills form one aptitude. Later research hurt key evidence: a 2018 study by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan, with more than 900 children, found the marshmallow link to later achievement small, and it disappeared once family background was taken into account. LeDoux has since argued that amygdala activity does not mean fear is felt.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want the original case for emotional skills, with the brain science, the classroom programs and the stories that made the idea famous. **Skip it** if you want the workplace version; *Working with Emotional Intelligence* or the HBR article "What Makes a Leader?" cover it more directly, and *Emotional Agility* by Susan David offers a newer take.
