## At a glance

**New ideas spread less on their merits than through people: how others perceive an innovation, who tells whom, and how a community's norms shape the decision decide how fast it travels.**

Everett M. Rogers · 1962 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Everett Rogers was born in 1931 on a farm near Carroll, Iowa. His father resisted hybrid seed corn for eight years, even though it yielded about a quarter more, until the 1936 drought left the neighbors' hybrid corn standing while the Rogers crop wilted. At Iowa State, where Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross's 1943 study of hybrid corn in two Iowa communities had helped found the field, Rogers took a PhD in rural sociology in 1957. The first edition grew out of his dissertation's literature review and appeared in 1962, while he was teaching at Ohio State. He later taught at Michigan State, Stanford, USC and the University of New Mexico, and died in 2004.

This summary follows the fifth and last edition (Free Press, 2003). The 1962 book drew on 405 publications; later versions appeared in 1971 (as *Communication of Innovations*, with F. Floyd Shoemaker), 1983 and 1995, and by 2003 Rogers put the count at more than 5,200. The fifth edition runs to more than 550 pages and adds the Internet. Its eleven chapters run from the elements of diffusion to consequences.

## Key concepts

### 1. Four elements and the S-curve · Transcends

Rogers defines diffusion as the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system. That sentence holds the four elements: innovation, communication channels, time and social system. An innovation only has to be perceived as new by the people deciding. Plot adopters over time and the cumulative count traces an S: slow at first, then a steep climb as adopters persuade their peers, then a flattening as few holdouts remain. In Ryan and Gross's study, hybrid corn took more than a decade to spread.

*Why it transcends:* Every new tool, AI assistants included, still has to travel through people, channels and communities over time, and the S still describes the shape.

### 2. Five perceived attributes · Transcends

Rogers argues that five qualities, as people perceive them, explain much of how fast an innovation spreads. Relative advantage is how much better it seems than what it replaces. Compatibility is its fit with existing values, past experience and needs. Complexity is how hard it is to understand and use. Trialability is whether it can be tried on a small scale first. Observability is whether others can see the results. Preventive innovations, whose payoff is a bad thing that does not happen later, have low perceived advantage and spread slowly.

*Why it transcends:* The checklist works on any launch, and it explains why a tool that is easy to try but hard to judge spreads in odd ways.

### 3. The innovation-decision process · Evolves

Iowa State's farm studies of the 1950s described adoption in five stages: awareness, interest, evaluation, trial and adoption. The fifth edition's innovation-decision process names five: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation and confirmation. Implementation is where re-invention happens, as users change an innovation to suit themselves, and Rogers insists re-invention is not necessarily bad. Confirmation allows for discontinuance, when people drop an innovation they had adopted. A new section covers how the Internet is changing the process.

*Why it evolves:* An AI assistant can now answer the knowledge questions on demand and help with re-invention, which shortens the early stages but leaves the decision and the doubts afterward with people.

### 4. Adopter categories · Evolves

Rogers sorts adopters by innovativeness, how early they adopt relative to others, into five ideal types: venturesome innovators (2.5 percent), respected early adopters (13.5), deliberate early majority (34), skeptical late majority (34) and traditional laggards (16). Rogers ties the split to the normal, bell-shaped distribution of adopters behind the S-curve. Peers look to early adopters. Later writers built on this map: Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991) placed a gap between early adopters and the early majority.

*Why it evolves:* The categories still help with targeting, but the same person can be an innovator with one AI use and a laggard with another, so the label fits a use better than a person.

### 5. Opinion leaders, homophily and change agents · Evolves

Ideas move mostly through networks. Opinion leaders are the people others turn to. The book profiles them by their outside contacts, accessibility, status and innovativeness, and shows that how innovative they are depends on their system's norms. Homophily, the degree to which people who interact are similar, makes talk easy but can trap ideas inside a group, and the strength-of-weak-ties theory shows how new information crosses between groups. Change agents, such as extension workers, succeed through client orientation, empathy and credibility, often by working through opinion leaders and aides closer to their clients' status.

*Why it evolves:* People now ask an AI assistant as well as a colleague, which adds a new kind of adviser but does not replace the trusted peer whose example carries persuasion.

### 6. Critical mass · Transcends

For interactive innovations such as the fax and the Internet, each adopter's benefit depends on how many others have adopted. Once enough people have adopted, at the critical mass, further adoption becomes self-sustaining. The book covers individual thresholds and strategies for reaching that point.

*Why it transcends:* Shared conventions inside a team, such as a common way of working with an AI assistant, still need enough people on board before they pay off.

### 7. Consequences and the pro-innovation bias · Transcends

The book opens with a failure. In the Peruvian village of Los Molinas, a health worker named Nelida spent two years urging its 200 families to boil drinking water. Local hot-and-cold beliefs reserved boiled water for the sick. "Nelida talked to villagers about germ theory, which they could not (and did not need to) understand." Of the 21 families she worked with most intensively, 11 adopted. Rogers names the field's own bias: assuming an innovation should be adopted by all. He sorts consequences as desirable or undesirable, direct or indirect, anticipated or unanticipated. In the steel axes case, women and young men could get steel axes at missions instead of relying on older men for stone axes, which unsettled the elders' authority.

*Why it transcends:* Any AI rollout can produce gains for some and losses for others, and asking about unanticipated, indirect consequences is still the discipline most launches skip.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

Rogers died before today's AI assistants, so what follows is our reading, applying his framework, not his view. His model fits them unusually well. Relative advantage is easy to show in a demo, and trialability is high: many people can try an assistant in minutes. Observability is the weak spot. Much use happens privately and answer quality is hard to judge from outside, so peers see less than they would of a neighbor's corn.

Re-invention is central: users shape these tools through prompts and workflows. The innovation-decision process speeds up at the knowledge stage, while confirmation and discontinuance matter more, since people try a tool, hit a bad answer and drop it.

The consequences chapter may be the most important for AI. Its warnings about pro-innovation bias and about gains flowing to those already ahead apply to any rollout that measures adoption rather than results.

Verdict: 4 of 7 key concepts transcend and 3 evolve.

## Critiques and caveats

Rogers raised the main criticisms himself: pro-innovation bias, an individual-blame bias that faults adopters rather than systems, reliance on people's recall of when they adopted, and neglect of equality. In a 1996 talk published by Arvind Singhal and Sweety Law, he admitted he had seldom looked at resistance to innovations. Critics such as Silvio Waisbord have faulted diffusion work for treating communication as top-down transmission of expert knowledge, and the management scholar Eric Abrahamson (1991) asked why inefficient innovations spread while efficient ones are rejected. The adopter percentages are notional, as Les Robinson's widely used summary of the theory notes. Some cases have been revisited: the anthropologist John Taylor's follow-up, "Goods and Gods," found totemic beliefs still alive at Edward River decades later, contrary to Lauriston Sharp's prediction that the steel axe would bring them down.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you design programs, launches or campaigns and want the research base behind adoption curves, opinion leaders and critical mass. **Skip it** if you want a quick, practical guide: it is a dense textbook, and *Crossing the Chasm* applies the adopter curve to selling technology in far fewer pages.
