## At a glance

**AI has moved from invention to application, where data, speed and hard-driving entrepreneurs count more than elite research, so China will rival the United States, and both must prepare for the jobs AI will take.**

Kai-Fu Lee · 2018 · Innovation and Technology · 7-minute read

## The big idea

Kai-Fu Lee built a speech recognition system for his Carnegie Mellon PhD, then led research groups at Apple, worked at Silicon Graphics, and helped set up Microsoft's research lab in Beijing, later Microsoft Research Asia. He ran Google China from 2005 to 2009, then started the fund now called Sinovation Ventures.

An early chapter describes what Lee calls China's Sputnik moment: AlphaGo's 2016 win over Lee Sedol, watched by more than 280 million people in China, after which a government plan set the goal of making China the world's center of AI innovation by 2030.

Reviewers describe a book in two halves. The first explains why China can match or overtake Silicon Valley. The second turns to what AI will do to work and, drawing on Lee's diagnosis of stage IV lymphoma in 2013, ends with a blueprint for how people and AI can coexist.

## Key concepts

### 1. From discovery to implementation · Evolves

Lee argues that the hard scientific work of deep learning is done. In a Fortune essay adapted from the book, he writes: "A.I. has moved from the era of discovery to the era of implementation." What wins now is applying known tools to real problems, which rewards execution, speed and data. That shift, he argues, favors China, with its huge market, its data and its tenacious entrepreneurs.

*Why it evolves:* Large language models grew out of fresh research, much of it from American labs, so discovery had not ended. Yet implementation still decides who profits, as Lee's own later moves show.

### 2. Data as the new oil · Expires

Lee calls China the new Saudi Arabia of data. Its huge, mobile-first population leaves rich records of daily life through apps and mobile payments, and looser privacy rules let firms collect more. Data is one of four building blocks the book gives for an AI superpower, as Liang Yin's review lists them: tenacious entrepreneurs, abundant data, well-trained AI scientists and a supportive policy environment.

*Why it expires:* Today's leading models learn from public text and huge amounts of computing power, so the bottleneck became chips more than consumer data. None of the four building blocks is about chips.

### 3. Copycats to gladiators · Transcends

James McGregor's review puts the book's argument this way: Chinese companies went from "copycats" to "gladiators" by surviving the most cutthroat competitive environment on the planet. In a 2018 Bloomberg interview, Lee's example was Wang Xing, who copied Facebook, Twitter and Groupon before building Meituan, which took on the hard work of hiring tens of thousands of delivery people. Tom Tunguz's notes recount how Meituan came out of a fight among hundreds of Groupon clones. Lee contrasts these hands-on founders with an American way that stays light on logistics.

*Why it transcends:* Lee still tells it this way. In a November 2026 Fortune interview, he called Chinese companies naturally thrifty and built to make money, crediting that more than US chip limits for their frugal spending.

### 4. The four waves of AI · Evolves

Lee sorts AI into four waves. Internet AI learns from what users click to tailor content. Business AI mines a company's own records, such as loans and repayments. Perception AI gives machines eyes and ears, from speech assistants to face recognition. Autonomous AI, the hardest, lets machines move and act, from self-driving cars to farm drones. Peter Diamandis's account of the book gives Lee's scorecard: even in internet AI with China set to edge ahead, China ahead in perception AI, and the United States far ahead in business and autonomous AI.

*Why it evolves:* All four waves arrived, but the largest one since, general-purpose models that write, code and reason, fits none of them neatly.

### 5. Two superpowers, everyone else behind · Transcends

The book's geopolitical claim is that AI will concentrate wealth in two countries and a few companies within them. Fortune's Clay Chandler summarizes the forecast: China would overtake the United States within a decade, and the two would become rival superpowers that leave other nations behind.

*Why it transcends:* The leading AI labs are still mostly American and Chinese. Whether China overtakes is still open: in March 2025 Reuters reported Lee saying China was about three months behind in some core technologies and ahead in some areas, and in November 2026 Fortune reported his estimate that Chinese open-weight models trail top US labs by six to 12 months.

### 6. The risk-of-replacement chart · Evolves

Lee maps jobs on two axes. For cognitive work, one runs from optimization-based to creative or strategic, the other from asocial to social; physical work swaps creativity for dexterity in unstructured settings. Dishwashers and entry-level translators fall in what the book calls the Danger Zone; psychiatrists and home-care nurses sit in the Safe Zone. Within 15 years, he predicts, AI will technically be able to automate about 40 to 50 percent of US jobs, a capability rather than a forecast of actual losses.

*Why it evolves:* The social axis holds up well. The creativity axis has weakened, since language models now draft, design and brainstorm, work the chart treated as safer.

### 7. Love and the social investment stipend · Evolves

Lee's cancer diagnosis, as the book tells it, showed him he had run his life like an optimization algorithm, and turned him toward sharing love with others. His TED talk in April 2018 made the same point: "humans are uniquely able to give and receive love, and that's what differentiates us from AI." He judges retraining, shorter hours and redistribution useful but not enough, and calls universal basic income a painkiller. He proposes a social investment stipend instead, a decent government salary for care work, community service and education.

*Why it evolves:* The question of paying people for care is sharper than ever. The claim that warmth is what machines lack is harder to hold now that AI chat can sound caring, even if it does not care.

## The AI-era lens

*AI analysis*

*AI Superpowers* is a forecast, and eight years on most of it can be graded.

The industry claims did better than the technology claims. The book bet on implementation, consumer data and gladiator founders. Then came a discovery it did not foresee: large language models, which reached the public with ChatGPT in November 2022. The new race turned on research and computing power, and on October 7, 2022 the US Commerce Department restricted China's access to advanced chips. Chinese firms went on to release cheaper open-weight models. Lee founded 01.AI in 2023 to build large language models; in March 2025 Reuters reported it shifting to tools that help clients put models to work, rather than competing on its own. Implementation, it turns out, still matters; it just came after a new discovery.

The jobs forecast is not due yet. Lee's window runs to about 2033. In October 2025, researchers at Brookings and the Yale Budget Lab found the US job mix broadly stable 33 months after ChatGPT's launch, while noting emerging evidence that AI may be adding to unemployment among early-career workers. In a 2022 IEEE Spectrum interview about his later book *AI 2041*, Lee still put routine jobs at 40 to 50 percent of the total.

Verdict: 2 of 7 key concepts transcend, 4 evolve and 1 expires.

## Critiques and caveats

In *Foreign Affairs* in November 2018, Remco Zwetsloot, Helen Toner and Jeffrey Ding faulted the book for zero-sum arms-race thinking, for hyping Chinese state plans that often under-deliver, for resting AI's progress on a single breakthrough, and for overgeneralizing how useful Chinese data is. Robert Atkinson of ITIF liked the first half but judged the economics weak, arguing that computer scientists should leave economics to economists. Liang Yin, reviewing it for the Thinking Ahead Institute, found it pro-China and limited on the downsides of a profit-above-all mentality. Our own caveat: its scorecards predate generative AI.

## Read it or skip it

**Read the full book** if you want an insider's account of how China's tech industry learned to compete, or a 2018 forecast to test your own predictions against. **Skip it** if you want a current picture of AI: the US-China story has moved on, and *Chip War* explains why chips came to matter more than data.
